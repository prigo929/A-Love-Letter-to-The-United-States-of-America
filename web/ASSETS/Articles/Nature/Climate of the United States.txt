# Climate of the United States

## Introduction

The climate of the United States spans a broad spectrum of types, from tropical in Hawaii, Puerto Rico, and southern Florida to arid in the Southwest deserts, temperate oceanic along the Pacific Northwest coast, humid continental across much of the interior, and polar in Alaska, as delineated by the Köppen-Geiger classification system which categorizes climates based on temperature and precipitation regimes. This diversity arises primarily from the country's vast latitudinal range, extensive mountain chains like the Rockies that create rain shadows, and moderating influences from the Atlantic, Pacific, and Gulf of Mexico. Annual average temperatures in the contiguous United States range from about 50°F (10°C) in the northern Rockies to over 70°F (21°C) in southern Florida and Arizona, while precipitation varies from under 10 inches (25 cm) in Nevada's basins to more than 150 inches (380 cm) in parts of Washington and Hawaii. Defining characteristics include pronounced seasonal contrasts in the Midwest and Northeast, with cold winters and hot summers, and vulnerability to extreme events such as Atlantic hurricanes, Great Plains tornadoes, western droughts, and northeastern blizzards, which underscore the interplay of synoptic-scale atmospheric dynamics and local geography.

## Physical Influences on Climate

### Topography and Elevation Effects

The United States' diverse topography, including major mountain ranges such as the Rockies, Sierra Nevada, and Cascades, profoundly influences local and regional climates through variations in elevation and landform barriers. Higher elevations generally correlate with lower temperatures due to the adiabatic lapse rate, where air cools as it rises, typically resulting in cooler conditions aloft compared to surrounding lowlands. For example, in western North Carolina, Asheville's elevated position in a broad valley yields consistently cooler annual temperatures than nearby lower-elevation sites, illustrating how altitude moderates thermal extremes across the country.

Mountainous topography drives orographic precipitation by forcing prevailing winds to ascend slopes, causing moist air to cool, condense, and release water as rain or snow on windward sides. The Cascade Mountains in the Pacific Northwest exemplify this effect, generating heavy precipitation—often exceeding 100 inches annually on western slopes—due to Pacific moisture-laden air rising over the range, fostering dense temperate rainforests while enhancing winter snowfall totals critical for water supply. Similarly, the Sierra Nevada orographically lifts air from the west, concentrating precipitation on windward faces and contributing to California's variable water resources.

Leeward sides of these ranges experience rain shadows, where descending air warms and inhibits further condensation, leading to pronounced aridity. In the western United States, the Cascades and Sierra Nevada create strong rain shadows eastward, resulting in semiarid to desert conditions in the Great Basin and Intermountain West, with annual precipitation often below 10 inches in shadowed valleys compared to over 50 inches on windward slopes. The Rocky Mountains similarly block moisture from the Gulf of Mexico and Pacific, exacerbating dryness in the Great Plains' leeward zones and influencing continental-scale aridity patterns. The Appalachian Mountains, though lower and more eroded, produce milder rain shadow effects, allowing greater moisture penetration to the interior compared to the higher western cordilleras.

### Oceanic Currents and Proximity

The proximity of the United States to the Atlantic Ocean, Pacific Ocean, Arctic Ocean, and Gulf of Mexico exerts a moderating influence on coastal climates, resulting in milder temperature extremes compared to the continental interior. Ocean waters have a higher heat capacity than land, absorbing and releasing heat more slowly, which dampens seasonal variations along coastlines: summers are cooler due to upwelling of deeper cold water and sea breezes, while winters are warmer from retained oceanic heat. This effect diminishes inland, typically within 50-100 miles of the shore, where continental climates exhibit greater diurnal and annual temperature swings, as seen in comparisons between coastal cities like San Francisco (average July high 67°F) and inland Sacramento (97°F).

On the Pacific coast, the cold California Current, a branch of the North Pacific Gyre flowing southward from Alaska, maintains sea surface temperatures 5-10°C cooler than subtropical latitudes would suggest, fostering fog, marine layers, and subdued summer highs in states like California and Oregon. This upwelling-driven cooling suppresses convective activity, contributing to the region's Mediterranean-like dryness in summer despite oceanic proximity, while enhancing winter precipitation through orographic lift of moist air masses. In contrast, the warmer Kuroshio Current extension influences Hawaii's tropical climate but has limited direct impact on the continental West Coast.

The Atlantic seaboard benefits from the northward-flowing Gulf Stream, which transports warm tropical waters from the Gulf of Mexico along the eastern U.S., elevating winter temperatures by up to 10-15°F in coastal areas from Florida to the Carolinas compared to similar latitudes inland. For instance, this current keeps Florida's east coast winters milder than the state's interior, with January averages around 60-70°F versus 50°F farther west, while also moderating summer heat through localized cooling effects. The associated North Atlantic Drift extends mild conditions northward to New England, though weakening trends observed since the 1990s—potentially linked to freshwater influx from Arctic melt—have raised questions about long-term stability without altering baseline warming influences.

### Atmospheric Circulation Patterns

The prevailing westerlies, dominant winds in the mid-latitudes of the Northern Hemisphere between approximately 30° and 60° N latitude, drive much of the weather patterns across the contiguous United States by transporting air masses and storm systems from west to east. These winds arise from the Ferrel cell of the three-cell global circulation model, where surface airflow converges toward subtropical highs and diverges poleward, resulting in a net westerly flow aloft and at the surface that facilitates the eastward progression of cyclones and fronts. In the United States, this circulation contributes to higher precipitation on the windward western slopes of mountain ranges like the Rockies, where orographic lift extracts moisture from Pacific air masses, while creating rain shadows in the interior Great Plains and Southwest.

The polar jet stream, a narrow band of strong westerly winds in the upper troposphere at around 200 hPa altitude and 5–9 miles above the surface, significantly modulates US temperature and precipitation by steering weather systems and influencing storm tracks. Typically positioned between 30° and 60° N, its position and strength vary seasonally—shifting northward in summer and southward in winter—and its meanders can lead to persistent weather anomalies, such as cold outbreaks in the eastern US when it dips equatorward or heat domes in the West when ridged. A weaker or more meridional (wavy) jet stream, often linked to reduced equator-pole temperature gradients, has been associated with increased blocking patterns that prolong droughts or floods, as observed in events like the 2011–2012 Texas drought.

Semi-permanent pressure systems further shape regional circulation: the Aleutian Low, a persistent subpolar low-pressure center over the North Pacific from fall to spring, intensifies storm tracks toward the US West Coast, delivering heavy precipitation to the Pacific Northwest through enhanced cyclogenesis and moisture transport. Conversely, subtropical highs—such as the North Pacific High (centered near Hawaii) in summer and the Bermuda-Azores High over the Atlantic—promote subsidence and clear skies, diverting storms northward in the West and fostering hurricane formation in the Gulf and Atlantic when the latter expands. These features interact with the jet stream; for instance, a deepened Aleutian Low can amplify southwesterly flow, bringing subtropical moisture ("Pineapple Express" atmospheric rivers) that cause extreme rainfall in California, with events exceeding 10 inches in 24 hours documented in NOAA records.

## Climate Classification and Zones

### Köppen-Geiger System Application

The Köppen-Geiger system applies empirical thresholds to monthly temperature and precipitation data to classify climates into five primary groups—A (tropical), B (arid), C (temperate), D (continental), and E (polar)—with subdivisions based on seasonal precipitation and temperature patterns. In the United States, this classification highlights extensive climatic variability driven by latitude, elevation, and proximity to oceans, encompassing nearly all major groups except certain highland (H) subtypes in the contiguous states. High-resolution maps at 1 km resolution, derived from gridded observational data such as WorldClim, delineate 22 distinct subtypes across the 50 states and territories for the period 1980–2016.

Tropical climates (A) occur sparingly, primarily as tropical monsoon (Am) and savanna (Aw) in Hawaii and southern Florida, where mean temperatures exceed 18 °C in all months and precipitation shows distinct wet and dry seasons. Arid and semi-arid climates (B), which receive less precipitation than potential evapotranspiration, dominate the Southwest, including hot desert (BWh) in low-elevation basins like the Sonoran Desert of Arizona and California, and cold semi-arid steppe (BSk) across the Great Plains and intermountain regions. These B climates cover substantial western and central areas, reflecting rain shadow effects from the Rockies and subtropical high pressure.

Temperate climates (C) with the coldest month between 0 °C and 18 °C characterize coastal and southeastern zones: humid subtropical (Cfa) spans from eastern Texas through the Gulf Coast to the mid-Atlantic states, featuring hot summers without a dry season; Mediterranean (Csa, Csb) aligns with California's coastal ranges, marked by dry summers; and oceanic (Cfb) prevails in the Pacific Northwest, with mild, wet winters and cool summers. Continental climates (D), defined by the coldest month below 0 °C, occupy the interior and northern contiguous states as well as much of Alaska: hot-summer humid continental (Dfa) in the Midwest Corn Belt, cooler variants (Dfb, Dwa) in the Northeast and Appalachians, and subarctic (Dfc, Dfd) in Alaska's interior and northern Rockies. Polar climates (E), including tundra (ET) and ice cap (EF), are confined to Alaska's arctic coast and high mountains, where even the warmest month averages below 10 °C.

### Distribution and Transitions

The distribution of Köppen-Geiger climate zones across the United States reveals a pronounced east-west gradient, with humid temperate and continental climates dominating the eastern two-thirds of the contiguous states and arid to semi-arid zones prevailing in the interior West. Humid subtropical (Cfa) climates characterize the Southeast, encompassing Florida, much of the Gulf Coast states, and extending northward to parts of Virginia and inland to eastern Texas, where average annual temperatures exceed 10°C in the coldest month and precipitation is ample year-round. Northward, these give way to humid continental (Dfa and Dfb) zones covering the Midwest, Great Lakes region, and Northeast, from Ohio to Maine, marked by coldest months below 0°C and significant seasonal temperature contrasts.

In the western United States, hot desert (BWh) and cold semi-arid (BSk) climates occupy the Southwest deserts and Great Basin, including Arizona, Nevada, and Utah, where precipitation falls below 250 mm annually in many areas due to subsidence from the subtropical high-pressure system. Mediterranean (Csa and Csb) climates line the California coast, with wet winters and dry summers, while oceanic west coast (Cfb) influences prevail in the Pacific Northwest, such as western Washington and Oregon, supporting mild, wet conditions. Alaska features extensive subarctic (Dfc), tundra (ET), and ice cap (EF) zones, particularly in the north and at high elevations.

Transitions between these zones are shaped by topographic barriers, latitude, and proximity to moisture sources. The most abrupt shift occurs along the Rocky Mountains, where the rain shadow effect blocks Pacific moisture, resulting in a rapid decline in precipitation from over 500 mm on eastern slopes to under 300 mm in intermountain valleys, demarcating humid continental or semi-arid plains (BSk) to the east from arid deserts (BWh) to the west. Latitudinal transitions are more gradual; for instance, the boundary between humid subtropical (Cfa) and continental (Dfa) climates aligns roughly with the 35°N parallel, transitioning across the Ohio River Valley and southern Midwest through changes in winter minimum temperatures. Elevational gradients further induce local transitions, such as from semi-arid lowlands to alpine tundra (ET) above 3,000 meters in the Sierra Nevada and Rockies. In the Great Plains, semi-arid (BSk) zones form a transitional belt between eastern humid areas and western deserts, influenced by variable Gulf moisture penetration.

## National-Scale Climatology

### Average Temperature Profiles

The contiguous United States has an annual average temperature of 53.3°F (11.8°C) based on the 1991–2020 NOAA climate normals, reflecting an area-weighted mean derived from thousands of weather stations. This value represents a slight increase from prior 30-year periods, with the 1981–2010 normal at approximately 52.8°F, consistent with observed long-term warming trends in station data. Including Alaska and Hawaii lowers the national average to around 50°F due to Alaska's colder continental and Arctic influences, though official normals emphasize the contiguous 48 states for baseline comparisons.

Seasonal temperature profiles exhibit a pronounced annual cycle, with January averages near 31°F and July near 74°F across the contiguous states, driven by solar insolation variations and hemispheric seasonality. Spring (March–May) transitions to about 52°F, summer (June–August) peaks at 72°F, and fall (September–November) cools to 56°F, showing minimal lag in continental interiors compared to moderated coastal areas. These profiles mask regional disparities: northern latitudes experience greater seasonal amplitude (up to 50°F range), while southern regions show subdued swings under subtropical influences.

Latitudinally, average annual temperatures decline northward at roughly 2–3°F per degree of latitude, from over 70°F in southern Florida to below 40°F in northern Maine, modulated by elevation and proximity to oceans that reduce extremes in the west and east coasts. Diurnal profiles feature larger daily ranges inland (20–40°F) versus coastal zones (10–20°F), with minimum temperatures more variable due to radiative cooling absent in urban heat islands. ### Precipitation and Seasonal Patterns

Precipitation across the contiguous United States averages approximately 30 inches annually based on the 1981-2010 U.S. Climate Normals, with totals varying widely from under 10 inches in arid southwestern deserts to over 100 inches in the wettest Pacific Northwest locales. This spatial heterogeneity arises from topographic barriers that enhance orographic lift on windward slopes while creating rain shadows in leeward areas, compounded by distance from moisture sources like the Pacific Ocean and Gulf of Mexico.

Seasonal precipitation patterns diverge regionally, reflecting influences from mid-latitude cyclones, convective activity, and monsoonal flows. Western coastal and mountainous areas receive the bulk of their rainfall in winter (October through March), fueled by Pacific storm tracks and atmospheric rivers that deliver intense, episodic events, while summers remain predominantly dry under the influence of high-pressure subsidence. In contrast, the Southwest exhibits a bimodal regime, with winter frontal precipitation supplemented by the North American Monsoon from July to September, which supplies more than 50% of annual totals in Arizona and New Mexico through thunderstorms originating from heated terrain and Gulf of California moisture.

Central and eastern regions feature a warm-season peak driven by convective thunderstorms, with spring maxima in the Great Plains from severe weather outbreaks and summer dominance in the Midwest and Southeast augmented by tropical systems. Northern states show relatively even distribution but with enhanced winter snowfall from lake-effect processes around the Great Lakes and nor'easters along the Atlantic seaboard. Alaska's precipitation concentrates in the southeastern panhandle, exceeding 150 inches yearly from persistent Pacific fronts, while Hawaii's windward slopes capture trade wind orographic rains year-round, moderated by seasonal shifts in the intertropical convergence zone.

### Long-Term Averages and Norms

The U.S. Climate Normals for the period 1991–2020, published by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration's National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI), constitute the current official long-term averages for key meteorological parameters, derived from observations at over 15,000 precipitation stations and more than 7,300 temperature stations nationwide. These 30-year baselines, updated decennially per World Meteorological Organization standards, enable standardized comparisons of recent conditions against historical norms and account for gradual shifts in the observational record.

For the contiguous United States, the annual mean temperature normal stands at 53.3°F, a 0.5°F increase from the 52.8°F average of the prior 1981–2010 normals, consistent with broader thermometer-based records showing multidecadal warming. Seasonal breakdowns yield winter (December–February) means of approximately 33°F, spring (March–May) at 52°F, summer (June–August) at 72°F, and fall (September–November) at 56°F, with greater variability in winter due to continental influences. Precipitation norms average around 30 inches annually across the lower 48 states, with increases noted in central and eastern regions relative to earlier periods, totaling about 1–2 inches more in many locales.

These norms exclude Alaska and Hawaii, where colder high-latitude and tropical influences respectively yield distinct profiles: Alaska's annual mean hovers near 28°F, while Hawaii's is about 73°F. Snowfall norms, relevant for northern and mountainous areas, average 20–30 inches in the Midwest and Northeast but exceed 100 inches in the Pacific Northwest and Great Lakes regions. The updated normals thus reflect empirical station data without adjustment for urban heat effects or sparse coverage in remote areas, providing a direct gauge of observed surface conditions over the reference interval.

## Regional Climates

### Southwest and Arid West

The Southwest and Arid West, encompassing Arizona, New Mexico, Nevada, Utah, and adjacent arid portions of California, Colorado, and Texas, exhibit predominantly arid and semi-arid climates driven by subsidence from the subtropical high-pressure ridge, orographic rain shadows from the Sierra Nevada and Rocky Mountains, and high evapotranspiration rates exceeding precipitation. These conditions result in Köppen classifications of hot desert (BWh) in low-elevation basins like the Sonoran and Mojave Deserts, cold desert (BWk) in intermountain valleys, and semi-arid steppe (BSh/BSk) on plateaus and transitional zones.

Annual precipitation averages 4 to 15 inches (10-38 cm), with Las Vegas, Nevada, recording about 4.2 inches and Albuquerque, New Mexico, around 9 inches based on 1991-2020 normals; much of this falls during the North American Monsoon (mid-June to September), which delivers convective thunderstorms and accounts for up to 50% of the yearly total in Arizona and New Mexico. Winter precipitation, when present, arrives via Pacific storms but diminishes eastward due to blocking topography. High interannual variability fosters recurrent droughts, as seen in the 2020-2021 event marked by record-low rainfall and elevated temperatures across the region.

Temperatures feature hot summers and mild winters in lowlands, with Phoenix, Arizona, averaging July highs of 106°F (41°C) and January lows of 43°F (6°C) per 1991-2020 data; Las Vegas mirrors this with July highs near 105°F (41°C). Elevations above 5,000 feet (1,500 m) shift to cooler regimes, with greater diurnal ranges and occasional freezing events. State records underscore extremes: Arizona's 128°F (53°C) at Lake Havasu City on June 29, 1994; Nevada's 125°F (52°C) at Laughlin on the same date; and New Mexico's 122°F (50°C) near Loving on June 27, 1994. These patterns reflect solar heating of sparse vegetation and soil, minimal cloud cover (over 300 sunny days annually in many areas), and limited moderating oceanic influences.

### Pacific Northwest and Coast

The Pacific Northwest coast, comprising western Washington and Oregon, exhibits a marine-influenced climate west of the Cascade Range, marked by mild temperatures and winter-dominant precipitation. Annual precipitation averages 70 to 100 inches on coastal plains, escalating to over 150 inches on windward mountain slopes due to orographic lift from moist Pacific air masses. This results in a pronounced rain shadow, with eastern slopes receiving far less moisture.

Temperatures remain moderate throughout the year, with January average maxima ranging from 38°F to 45°F and minima from 25°F to 32°F along the coast, while summer highs seldom exceed 80°F. High humidity and persistent cloud cover contribute to overcast conditions, particularly from autumn through spring, fostering temperate rainforest ecosystems in windward areas. Precipitation is concentrated in the cooler months, with dry summers reflecting the influence of subtropical high pressure over the Pacific.

Coastal cities like Seattle record about 38 inches of annual precipitation, while Portland averages approximately 43 inches, though both experience more rainy days than inches suggest due to frequent light drizzle. Extreme temperatures are rare; coastal lows infrequently drop below 20°F, and highs above 90°F occur sporadically, though events like the 2021 heat dome pushed records to over 110°F in parts of the interior valleys. The region's climate supports diverse agriculture, including berries and timber, but is vulnerable to variability from Pacific oscillations like El Niño, which can reduce winter rains.

### Great Plains and Central States

The Great Plains and Central States, encompassing regions from North Dakota to Texas and including states like Nebraska, Kansas, and Oklahoma, feature a continental climate dominated by large seasonal and diurnal temperature variations due to their interior location and distance from moderating oceanic influences. Annual average temperatures decrease northward, ranging from approximately 45°F (7°C) in the northern Plains to over 60°F (16°C) in the southern portions, with July mean daily ranges often exceeding 30°F (17°C) across the region. Winters bring frequent cold outbreaks from Canadian air masses, while summers are heated by subsiding air and intense solar radiation, occasionally pushing temperatures above 100°F (38°C).

Precipitation gradients westward, with annual totals averaging 20-30 inches (510-760 mm) in the central and western Plains, increasing to 30-40 inches (760-1020 mm) eastward due to greater influence from Gulf of Mexico moisture. Most rainfall occurs in convective thunderstorms during the warm season (April-September), contributing to high variability and flood risks, while winter precipitation often falls as snow in the north. The region is classified primarily under Köppen types Dfa (hot-summer humid continental) in the east and BSk (cold semi-arid steppe) in the west, reflecting the transition from humid to drier conditions.

Severe weather is a hallmark, driven by the collision of contrasting air masses: warm, moist Gulf air meets cool, dry air from the Rockies or Canada, fostering supercell thunderstorms and the world's highest tornado frequency in "Tornado Alley" spanning Texas, Oklahoma, Kansas, Nebraska, and adjacent areas. Over 500 tornadoes occur annually in this zone, peaking in spring (April-June), with historical data showing enhanced activity from flat terrain allowing unimpeded wind shear. Winter blizzards, such as the prolonged events of 1948-1949, deliver heavy snow and winds exceeding 50 mph (80 km/h), exacerbating isolation in rural areas.

### Midwest and Great Lakes

The Midwest and Great Lakes region predominantly exhibits a humid continental climate under the Köppen classification (Dfa in southern areas with hot summers and Dfb in northern areas with warm summers), marked by significant seasonal temperature contrasts, ample year-round precipitation, and variable weather driven by cyclonic storms. This climate type prevails across states including Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Michigan, Minnesota, Missouri, Ohio, and Wisconsin, with continental influences causing cold winters from polar air outbreaks and warm, humid summers from southerly flows.

Annual average temperatures vary latitudinally, ranging from below 40°F (4°C) in northern Minnesota to at least 56°F (13°C) in southern Missouri, reflecting the region's span from subarctic frontal boundaries to more temperate southern margins. Summer highs typically reach around 85°F (29°C) in July, with high relative humidity enhancing discomfort, while winter lows average near 15°F (-9°C) or colder in January, often accompanied by wind chills below 0°F (-18°C). For instance, Wisconsin's average winter (December-February) temperature is about 17°F with annual snowfall around 51 inches, much colder than southern states such as Alabama (~47°F/2 in), Arkansas (~42°F/5 in), Florida (~59°F/0 in), Louisiana (~51°F/0 in), Mississippi (~47°F/1 in), Oklahoma (~39°F/8 in), South Carolina (~46°F/1 in), Tennessee (~39°F/6 in), and Texas (~48°F/2 in), which have mild winters and little to no snow; compared to other northern states like Iowa (~22°F/35 in), Kansas (~32°F/15 in), Ohio (~30°F/28 in), Missouri (~32°F/17 in), and West Virginia (~33°F/62 in), Wisconsin is colder but has more snow than most except West Virginia. Precipitation averages 30 to 40 inches (760 to 1,020 mm) annually, distributed fairly evenly but peaking in spring and summer from convective thunderstorms; near the Great Lakes, totals exceed 40 inches (1,020 mm) due to orographic and lake-enhanced effects.

The Great Lakes exert a moderating influence on adjacent lands, tempering extremes—cooler summers and milder winters—while fostering lake-effect precipitation, particularly snowfall, when cold northwest winds traverse relatively warm, ice-free waters. This results in heavy snow bands downwind, with annual accumulations surpassing 100 inches (2,540 mm) in leeward areas of Michigan's Upper Peninsula and New York’s Tug Hill Plateau, far exceeding inland values. Transitional seasons feature dynamic weather: spring brings severe thunderstorms and peak tornado activity in the region's "Dixie Alley" extensions, while autumn sees clear skies interspersed with early frosts.

### Southeast and Gulf Coast

The Southeast and Gulf Coast regions exhibit a humid subtropical climate (Köppen Cfa), characterized by hot, humid summers and mild winters influenced by warm maritime air from the Gulf of Mexico and Atlantic Ocean. Average annual temperatures range from 60°F (16°C) in northern areas like Virginia to over 70°F (21°C) in southern Florida, with July mean temperatures typically 77–85°F (25–29°C) and January means 45–65°F (7–18°C). Relative humidity frequently surpasses 70% during summer, exacerbating heat stress through elevated heat indices often exceeding 100°F (38°C).

Precipitation averages 45–65 inches (1,140–1,650 mm) annually, with relatively even distribution but peaks in summer from intense convective thunderstorms and tropical cyclones. Winter precipitation arises mainly from frontal systems, while the Gulf's warmth sustains moisture influx year-round. The region's flat terrain and proximity to warm seas promote frequent severe weather, including thunderstorms producing hail and gusty winds. Droughts occur infrequently but can intensify during El Niño phases, reducing rainfall by 10–20%.

This area faces elevated risk from tropical cyclones, with the Gulf Coast recording an average of 2–3 hurricane or tropical storm landfalls per decade since 1851. NOAA records show no statistically significant increase in continental U.S. hurricane landfall frequency or intensity since 1900, despite variability such as the active 2017 season. Major hurricanes (Category 3+) make landfall roughly every 2–3 years in the broader Atlantic basin affecting the region.

### Northeast and Mid-Atlantic

The Northeast and Mid-Atlantic encompass states from Maine to Pennsylvania, characterized primarily by a humid continental climate under the Köppen classification (Dfa in warmer lowlands and Dfb in cooler, higher elevations), marked by large annual temperature swings exceeding 50°F (28°C) between winter lows and summer highs, and year-round precipitation without a pronounced dry season. This regime results from the region's position between the moderating influence of the Atlantic Ocean and continental air masses, with westerly winds and cyclonic storms driving variability; southern Mid-Atlantic areas occasionally transition toward humid subtropical (Cfa) traits near the Virginia border, featuring milder winters.

Average annual temperatures decrease northward and inland, ranging from 45–50°F (7–10°C) in northern New England to 52–55°F (11–13°C) in coastal Mid-Atlantic zones like New York City and Philadelphia, based on 1991–2020 normals. Winter (December–February) means hover around 25–35°F (-4 to 2°C), with frequent subfreezing spells and occasional Arctic outbreaks pushing lows below 0°F (-18°C); July averages reach 70–75°F (21–24°C), though heat indices can exceed 100°F (38°C) during humid spells from southerly flows. Spring and fall serve as transitional seasons with volatile weather, including late frosts into May and early ones by October in interior areas.

Precipitation totals average 40–45 inches (1,000–1,140 mm) annually across the lowlands, rising above 50 inches (1,270 mm) in the Appalachians due to orographic lift, distributed fairly evenly but peaking slightly in late summer and winter from tropical remnants and nor'easters—extratropical cyclones that deliver heavy snow and rain along the coast. Snowfall accumulates 30–50 inches (760–1,270 mm) in coastal New England and Mid-Atlantic cities, escalating to 60–100+ inches (1,520–2,540+ mm) in mountainous New England interiors like the Green and White Mountains, often in multi-day events exceeding 12–24 inches (300–610 mm).

### Alaska

Alaska encompasses a wide range of climates, from temperate oceanic in the southeast panhandle to continental subarctic in the interior and polar tundra in the north, influenced primarily by its latitudinal extent, mountainous terrain, and marine air flows from the Pacific and Arctic Oceans. The Köppen-Geiger classification identifies predominant types as Dfc (subarctic with cool summers) in much of the interior and southcentral regions, Cfc (cold oceanic) along the southern coast, and ET (tundra) in the Arctic north, with limited polar ice cap (EF) areas. These variations result from orographic effects, where coastal mountains intercept moist Pacific air, leading to heavy precipitation on windward slopes, while the interior's rain shadow produces drier conditions.

Mean annual temperatures decrease northward and inland, ranging from the low 40s°F (about 4–5°C) in the maritime southeast to around 10°F (-12°C) along the Arctic coast. In Anchorage, a representative southcentral location, the 1991–2020 normal annual average is 35.0°F, with January means at 15.3°F and July at 57.0°F. Fairbanks in the interior records a cooler 24.2°F annual average, featuring January lows averaging -19.2°F and marked diurnal ranges due to continentality. Northern stations like Utqiaġvik (Barrow) exhibit even colder norms at 11.5°F annually, with perpetual permafrost and minimal summer thaw. Seasonal patterns show short, cool summers across the state, with frost possible year-round in the north, while winters lengthen northward, often exceeding six months with subzero temperatures.

Precipitation is highly regional, with the southeast panhandle receiving over 100 inches (2,540 mm) annually, primarily as rain due to prevailing westerlies and orographic lift, fostering temperate rainforests. In contrast, the interior and north average under 15 inches (380 mm), mostly as snow, with dry summers prone to wildfires. Statewide, snowfall dominates winter precipitation, accumulating 50–200 inches (1,270–5,080 mm) in coastal areas but less in the dry interior; Bristol Bay and Aleutian regions see frequent fog and drizzle from marine influences. Long-term data indicate modest increases in annual precipitation since the mid-20th century, particularly in autumn and winter, though variability tied to Pacific sea surface temperatures drives multidecadal fluctuations.

### Hawaii and Pacific Territories

Hawaii's climate is predominantly tropical, characterized by mild temperatures year-round due to its mid-ocean location and prevailing northeast trade winds, with average highs ranging from 80°F to 85°F and lows from 65°F to 75°F at sea level across the main islands. August marks the warmest month with an average temperature of 79°F (1950–2020), while February is the coolest at 72°F, though significant variations occur due to elevation, with summits like Mauna Kea experiencing freezing temperatures and occasional snowfall above 11,000 feet. Precipitation is highly variable, influenced by orographic lift on windward slopes; annual totals exceed 150 inches in mountainous windward areas like Hilo, while leeward coasts such as Kailua-Kona receive under 20 inches, with most rainfall occurring from October to April during the wet season.

The islands generally avoid extreme heat waves or cold snaps at lower elevations, but volcanic activity and microclimates create localized diversity, including rain shadows and inversion layers that cap cloud formation. Tropical cyclones pose a threat, though direct hurricane impacts are infrequent—only three have made landfall since 1950—with El Niño phases increasing central Pacific storm activity by steering systems northward.

The U.S. Pacific Territories—American Samoa, Guam, and the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands (CNMI)—exhibit tropical marine climates with consistent warmth, high humidity, and seasonal rainfall patterns moderated by trade winds. In American Samoa, daytime highs average 84–88°F and nighttime lows 77–78°F year-round, with a wet season from January to March yielding peak precipitation and a drier period from June to August. Guam maintains similar uniformity, with temperatures ranging from the low 70s°F to mid-80s°F, annual rainfall averaging around 90 inches concentrated in July–August typhoon season, when easterly trades weaken. The CNMI experiences averages of 70–85°F, with 85 inches of yearly rain, abundant from July to October under variable winds, and moderated northeast trades from November to March.

### Caribbean Territories

The U.S. Caribbean territories, including Puerto Rico and the U.S. Virgin Islands, feature a tropical climate dominated by warm temperatures, high humidity, and easterly trade winds that moderate heat and distribute precipitation unevenly. Annual mean temperatures average 80–82°F (27–28°C) in coastal regions like San Juan, with minimal seasonal variation—monthly means ranging from 78°F in January to 82°F in July—while diurnal ranges typically span 10–15°F due to nocturnal cooling. Higher elevations, such as Puerto Rico's Cordillera Central, record cooler averages around 70°F (21°C), reflecting orographic influences on local thermodynamics.

Precipitation totals vary by topography and exposure to trade winds, averaging 45–60 inches (1,140–1,520 mm) annually on Puerto Rico's northern coasts and the U.S. Virgin Islands' northern islands like St. Thomas, with a wet season from May to November delivering 60–70% of the yearly total through convective showers and tropical waves. Southern and leeward areas, including St. Croix and Puerto Rico's south coast, receive 30–45 inches (760–1,140 mm), fostering drier conditions akin to tropical savanna. Mountainous interiors, such as El Yunque National Forest, exceed 200 inches (5,080 mm) due to uplift-induced rainfall, supporting rainforest ecosystems. Dry seasons from December to April see reduced convective activity, though occasional cold fronts can bring brief showers.

The region's position in the Atlantic hurricane belt exposes it to frequent tropical cyclones from June to November, with historical data recording multiple major landfalls; Puerto Rico has endured eight billion-dollar disasters from 1980 to 2024, all tropical cyclones, including Hurricane Maria's Category 4 strike on September 20, 2017, which generated sustained winds over 150 mph and triggered widespread flooding. Such events amplify seasonal precipitation extremes, with single-storm totals occasionally surpassing 20 inches, underscoring the causal role of warm sea surface temperatures in intensifying storm dynamics. Temperature records show a rise of nearly 2°F since 1950, linked to observed increases in heat index days exceeding 100°F.

## Temperature Extremes

### Record High Temperatures

The highest air temperature officially recorded in the contiguous United States is 134 °F (56.7 °C) at Greenland Ranch (present-day Furnace Creek) in Death Valley, California, on July 10, 1913. This measurement, taken with mercury thermometers in a Stevenson screen, has faced historical scrutiny for possible overexposure to solar radiation or calibration issues common in early 20th-century stations, yet it persists as the verified national record per the National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) after review by the National Climate Extremes Committee. Subsequent highs in Death Valley, such as 130 °F (54.4 °C) on August 16, 2020, have approached but not exceeded this benchmark, underscoring the rarity of absolute extremes amid regional heat events driven by subsidence, low humidity, and terrain trapping.

State-level records in the contiguous United States predominantly date to the 1930s Dust Bowl period, when persistent high-pressure systems, soil desiccation, and reduced cloud cover amplified surface heating through feedback mechanisms like decreased evapotranspiration. For instance, Arizona's 128 °F (53.3 °C) occurred at Lake Havasu City on June 29, 1994; Nevada's 125 °F (51.7 °C) at Laughlin on June 29, 1994; and New Mexico's 122 °F (50 °C) at Warm Springs on June 18, 1954, reflecting aridity's role in permitting unchecked radiative warming. Eastern states show lower maxima due to moisture-laden air masses and frontal boundaries that promote convection and cloud formation, limiting surface insolation; examples include Missouri's 118 °F (47.8 °C) at Warsaw and Union on July 14, 1954, and Iowa's 118 °F (47.8 °C) at Keokuk on July 20, 1934. These pre-1940 peaks contrast with post-1950 records, which are fewer and often tied to localized urban heat islands or measurement site changes, rather than widespread synoptic-scale anomalies.

In Alaska, the record high is 100 °F (37.8 °C) at Fort Yukon on June 27, 1915, facilitated by a rare influx of continental air masses overriding the typical maritime moderation. Hawaii's equivalent is also 100 °F (37.8 °C), recorded at Pahala on the Big Island on April 27, 1931, during a downslope wind event compressing and heating trade winds against volcanic topography. Among territories, Puerto Rico's provisional high is 104 °F (40 °C) at Maunabo on August 26, 1956, though under NWS review for validity, with a confirmed 100 °F (37.8 °C) at Ponce on August 21, 2003; American Samoa reached 99 °F (37.2 °C) at Pago Pago in 1994. These non-contiguous extremes highlight elevation, ocean proximity, and orographic effects as primary causal factors over long-term trends in absolute maxima.

### Record Low Temperatures

The lowest temperature ever officially recorded in the United States was -80°F (-62.2°C) at Prospect Creek Camp in Alaska on January 23, 1971, verified by the National Climatic Data Center (NCDC). This measurement occurred during a prolonged Arctic outbreak influenced by a deep upper-level trough, with clear skies and radiational cooling exacerbating the extreme cold at the remote site north of Fairbanks.

In the contiguous 48 states, the record low stands at -70°F (-56.7°C), measured at Rogers Pass in Montana on January 20, 1954, as confirmed by the National Weather Service (NWS) and State Climate Extremes Committee (SCEC). The reading was taken at a mining camp near the Continental Divide, where katabatic winds and inversion layers trapped cold air, with subsequent reviews upholding its validity despite debates over instrumentation at high-elevation sites. Other notable sub- -60°F readings in the lower 48 include -66°F (-54.4°C) at Old Faithful, Wyoming, on February 9, 1933, and -65°F (-53.9°C) near Peter Sinks, Utah, on February 1, 1985, both ratified by SCEC standards requiring well-sited stations.

State-level records, maintained by NOAA's SCEC, cluster in the northern and interior regions due to continental polar air masses and topographic trapping, with most set between 1899 and 1983. For instance, North Dakota's -60°F (-51.1°C) occurred at Parshall on February 15, 1936, while Minnesota reached -60°F (-51.1°C) at Tower on February 2, 1996, the most recent among the sub- -60°F state marks. Verification processes emphasize metadata on station exposure and sensor calibration, rejecting unrepresentative or unverified claims, such as disputed pre-1900 readings.

### Heat Waves and Cold Outbreaks

Heat waves in the United States are defined by the National Weather Service as periods of abnormally and uncomfortably hot and humid weather lasting at least two days, often with temperatures exceeding 90°F (32°C) and accompanied by high heat index values. The 1930s Dust Bowl decade produced the most intense and widespread heat waves in instrumental records, driven by persistent high-pressure systems over drought-stricken regions, with July 1936 standing out for its national scope. During that event, temperatures reached 121°F (49°C) in North Dakota, and over 5,000 heat-related deaths occurred, surpassing modern events in duration and areal coverage across the Plains, Midwest, and Northeast.

Longer-term data from weather stations show that heat wave frequency and intensity peaked in the 1930s, with many state all-time high temperature records set then, including in 23 states. For example, the frequency of days exceeding 95°F (35°C) nationwide was approximately 18% higher during 1901–1960 than in 1961–2020, challenging narratives of uninterrupted increases. Recent analyses confirm the 1930s as the benchmark for severity, attributing much of the era's extremes to natural variability amplified by land-use changes like overplowing, rather than solely greenhouse gas forcing. While regional studies report rising heat wave occurrences in the Southeast and Northwest since the 1960s—such as HI-defined events (consecutive days with heat index above the 95th percentile)—these remain below 1930s levels in metrics like population-weighted temperature anomalies.

Notable modern heat waves include the 1980 event, which caused over 1,700 deaths amid a humid Midwest dome, and the 2021 Pacific Northwest dome, where Lytton, British Columbia (near the US border), hit 121.9°F (49.9°C), breaking Canadian records but aligning with US border anomalies rather than widespread national extremes. Economic costs have escalated with population growth and urbanization, with the 2012 heat wave ranking among the costliest at $11 billion in damages, yet per capita impacts lag historical fatalities adjusted for era demographics.
