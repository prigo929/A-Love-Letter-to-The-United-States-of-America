# Constitutional Convention (1787)

## Introduction

The Constitutional Convention of 1787, convened in Philadelphia from May 25 to September 17, was a gathering of 55 delegates from twelve states tasked initially with revising the Articles of Confederation but which instead produced an entirely new Constitution establishing a federal republic with separated powers among legislative, executive, and judicial branches. Under the presidency of George Washington and with proceedings shrouded in secrecy to foster open debate, the delegates navigated deep divisions over representation, state sovereignty, and slavery through compromises that balanced large and small state interests while accommodating Southern economic reliance on enslaved labor.

The convention's most significant achievement was crafting a durable framework that addressed the Confederation's failures—such as inability to tax, regulate commerce, or maintain a standing army—by granting Congress enumerated powers, creating a national judiciary, and instituting an indirectly elected executive, though these innovations sparked immediate controversy for exceeding the assembly's amendatory mandate and centralizing authority in ways that alarmed advocates of state autonomy. Key resolutions included the Virginia Plan's proportional representation in a lower house, modified by the New Jersey Plan's equal state suffrage in an upper house via the Great Compromise, and the Three-Fifths Clause counting enslaved persons for apportionment but not taxation, reflecting pragmatic concessions rather than principled abolitionism. Only 39 delegates ultimately signed the draft on September 17, omitting notable absentees like Patrick Henry and delegates from Rhode Island, which boycotted the proceedings.

Ratification proved contentious, requiring nine states' approval without revisions, as Federalists like James Madison argued the document's checks and balances prevented tyranny, while Anti-Federalists decried the absence of a bill of rights and potential for consolidated power; eventual adoption by all states solidified the Constitution's role in enabling national unity and economic stability, though its compromises perpetuated slavery until the Civil War.

## Historical Prelude

### Weaknesses of the Articles of Confederation

The Articles of Confederation, effective from March 1, 1781, after ratification by all thirteen states, created a unicameral Congress with limited enumerated powers while reserving sovereignty to the states. This framework, intended to prevent the overreach experienced under British rule, instead produced a national government too feeble to address collective challenges, as Congress derived authority solely from state delegations and lacked mechanisms for independent enforcement.

A core fiscal weakness lay in Congress's inability to impose taxes or duties directly on individuals or imports; Article VIII confined revenue to state requisitions proportional to land value, which states often ignored or underpaid, crippling debt repayment from the Revolutionary War and routine operations. For instance, requisitions issued in the early 1780s for war debts totaling millions in continental dollars yielded minimal compliance, forcing Congress to print depreciated currency and borrow from foreign lenders without repayment capacity, which eroded creditor confidence and national credit by mid-decade.

Congress also held no authority to regulate interstate commerce or impose uniform trade policies, allowing states to erect tariffs and barriers against each other—such as New York's duties on New Jersey goods in 1785—fostering economic fragmentation and retaliatory measures that hindered national cohesion. In foreign affairs, this impotence extended to treaty enforcement; while Congress could negotiate accords like the 1783 Treaty of Paris ending the war, it could not compel states to honor terms, such as repaying pre-war British debts, leading European powers like Britain to retain frontier forts in violation of agreements.

### Shays' Rebellion and Calls for Stronger Union

Shays' Rebellion erupted in western Massachusetts amid a severe postwar economic crisis characterized by deflation, high state taxes imposed to service Revolutionary War debts, and aggressive debt collection practices that led to foreclosures and imprisonment of farmers unable to pay mortgages or taxes. Many debtors, including Revolutionary War veterans, faced property seizures without sufficient circulating currency or federal relief, exacerbating grievances against state policies under Governor James Bowdoin that prioritized creditor interests and rejected paper money issuance.

The uprising began in August 1786 when over 500 armed farmers, led by Captain Daniel Shays—a Continental Army veteran—prevented the Northampton County Court from convening on August 29 to halt foreclosure proceedings. By September, similar court closings occurred in Worcester, Springfield, and Concord, disrupting judicial processes across the region and drawing up to 4,000 participants who sought debt relief, tax moratoriums, and relocation of the state legislature westward. The rebels organized under loose military discipline, marching on armories to secure arms and powder, culminating in a failed assault on the federal Springfield Armory on January 25, 1787, where approximately 1,200 insurgents clashed with a militia force of about 1,000 under General William Shepard, resulting in four rebel deaths and the dispersal of Shays' forces.

The state government, lacking immediate federal support under the Articles of Confederation, suppressed the rebellion through a privately financed army of 4,400 men raised by Bowdoin and commanded by General Benjamin Lincoln, funded by wealthy merchants who contributed over $100,000 in loans since Congress could not requisition funds or troops swiftly from states. Lincoln's forces decisively defeated remaining rebels at Petersham on February 4, 1787, leading to the capture of Shays and hundreds of followers; while over 100 were initially sentenced to death for treason, most received pardons or light penalties by 1788, with Shays fleeing to Vermont. This reliance on ad hoc private funding underscored the Confederation's impotence in quelling domestic insurrections, as Article VI prohibited federal interference in state matters without consent, and Congress's inability to enforce requisitions left Massachusetts to act alone.

### Annapolis Convention and Authorization for Philadelphia

The Annapolis Convention convened from September 11 to September 14, 1786, in Annapolis, Maryland, following an invitation issued by the Virginia General Assembly in late 1785 to address interstate commercial regulations under the Articles of Confederation. The meeting aimed to discuss uniform trade policies amid growing economic disarray, including barriers imposed by individual states that hindered national commerce. Only five states—New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware, and Virginia—sent delegates, totaling twelve commissioners, due to insufficient notice and competing priorities among state legislatures. Prominent attendees included Alexander Hamilton of New York, who played a leading role in drafting the proceedings.

Faced with incomplete representation, the delegates refrained from substantive deliberations on commerce and instead produced a report on September 14, 1786, acknowledging the "defects" in the federal system that impeded effective governance. The report, primarily authored by Hamilton, urged Congress to summon a broader convention of all thirteen states to propose revisions to the Articles of Confederation, specifying a meeting in Philadelphia on the second Monday of May 1787. It emphasized that such amendments required "essential" changes beyond mere commercial adjustments, reflecting delegates' recognition of systemic weaknesses exposed by events like state trade disputes and fiscal instability. The document was transmitted to Congress and the state executives, bypassing direct legislative endorsement to expedite action.

Congress received the Annapolis report by September 20, 1786, and after debate, adopted a resolution on February 21, 1787, authorizing the proposed convention in Philadelphia for the "sole and express purpose of revising the Articles of Confederation." This endorsement legitimized the gathering, inviting states to appoint delegates while stipulating that any alterations must be ratified by state legislatures or conventions to maintain the amendment process under Article XIII of the Articles. The resolution passed amid concerns over congressional quorum issues and external pressures, such as Shays' Rebellion, which underscored the urgency for federal reform without altering the convention's limited mandate. Twelve states ultimately participated, with Rhode Island absent due to opposition to centralized power.

## Convening and Organization

### Delegate Selection and Attendance

The Confederation Congress, acting on recommendations from the Annapolis Convention of 1786, passed a resolution on February 21, 1787, authorizing each state legislature to appoint commissioners—delegates—to assemble in Philadelphia on May 14, 1787, for the limited purpose of revising the Articles of Confederation to render the federal constitution "adequate to the exigencies of the Union." State legislatures selected delegates through legislative votes or gubernatorial appointments with legislative approval, typically favoring prominent political leaders, lawyers, merchants, and planters experienced in governance under the Articles or state constitutions; no direct popular election occurred, preserving the confederal structure's emphasis on state sovereignty. Instructions to delegates varied by state but generally confined their role to amendments rather than wholesale replacement, though some states like Virginia granted broader latitude.

All thirteen original states except Rhode Island appointed delegates, with Rhode Island's legislature declining participation due to opposition from agrarian interests fearing enhanced federal taxing powers that could undermine paper money policies and debt relief favored by debtors. In total, the twelve participating states—Connecticut, Delaware, Georgia, Maryland, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New York, North Carolina, Pennsylvania, South Carolina, and Virginia—appointed approximately 70 individuals, though 18 declined service, including notable Anti-Federalists such as Patrick Henry and Richard Henry Lee of Virginia, who suspected the convention masked ambitions for a consolidated national government. Of those appointed, 55 delegates ultimately attended sessions at the Pennsylvania State House (Independence Hall), representing a cross-section of regional interests: Southern planters, Northern merchants, and middling-state moderates, with an average age of 42 and professions dominated by law (about two-thirds).

Attendance fluctuated significantly over the convention's duration from May 25 (when a quorum of seven states was first achieved) to September 17, 1787, with never more than 46 delegates present simultaneously and an average daily attendance closer to 30 due to illnesses, farm duties, and political withdrawals. New Hampshire's delegation arrived late in July, temporarily dropping quorum risks, while delegates like Alexander Hamilton of New York attended sporadically (only a few days total) amid state intrigues, and George Mason of Virginia left without signing in protest over the lack of a bill of rights. Pennsylvania hosted the largest contingent (8 delegates, all in regular attendance), while smaller states like Delaware (5) and Georgia (4) sent fewer but ensured consistent presence to safeguard equal state suffrage.

### Leadership, Rules, and Secrecy Measures

George Washington was unanimously elected president of the Constitutional Convention on May 25, 1787, upon achieving a quorum of delegates from seven states. In this role, Washington presided over sessions, maintained order, and lent prestige to the proceedings, though he spoke rarely and focused on facilitating consensus rather than dominating debates. William Jackson of South Carolina was appointed secretary, responsible for recording official minutes and handling correspondence, with assistance from delegates like James Madison who maintained private notes.

A committee led by George Wythe of Virginia proposed procedural rules on May 28, 1787, which the convention adopted with minor amendments the following day. These rules, drawing from congressional practices under the Articles of Confederation, established a quorum of two delegates from seven states, initial voting by state delegations (one vote per state), and later shifts to per capita voting for certain resolutions to reflect individual merits over strict state equality. Provisions for decorum allowed any member or the president to call another to order for indecorous conduct, permitting explanation but requiring submission to majority will; committees of the whole were used for flexible debate, and a nine-state majority was needed for major decisions after initial organization.

Secrecy formed a cornerstone rule, adopted explicitly on May 29, 1787, stipulating that "nothing spoken in the House be printed, or otherwise published or communicated without leave." To enforce this, delegates shuttered windows, posted sentries at doors, and pledged not to divulge proceedings publicly—many honoring the vow for decades, with Madison's detailed notes released only posthumously in 1840. This measure, justified by delegates like Washington to shield deliberations from external influence and foster candid compromise amid regional tensions, prevented premature partisan attacks but drew later criticism for opacity in a republican process.

## Core Proposals and Initial Debates

### Virginia Plan: Blueprint for National Supremacy

The Virginia Plan, drafted primarily by James Madison in preparation for the Constitutional Convention, was presented by Virginia Governor Edmund Randolph on May 29, 1787, as a framework for revising the Articles of Confederation into a stronger national government. This proposal outlined 15 resolutions that fundamentally shifted authority from state sovereignty toward national supremacy, empowering a central legislature to address deficiencies in the confederation system where individual states proved inadequate or disruptive to union harmony.

Central to the plan's design for national dominance was a bicameral national legislature: the first branch elected directly by the people in proportion to population quotas, and the second branch chosen by the first from candidates nominated by state legislatures, also apportioned by population or contribution quotas. The legislature would hold broad legislative powers, including those previously vested in Congress under the Articles, plus authority over all cases where states lacked competence or where state actions threatened national unity, such as trade regulations or interstate disputes. To enforce supremacy, the plan granted the national legislature a veto over state laws conflicting with national policy, a mechanism intended to prevent state encroachments and ensure uniform application of federal authority.

The executive and judiciary branches further entrenched national control, with an executive selected by the legislature to implement laws faithfully and a supreme national tribunal appointed similarly to adjudicate cases under national authority, including appeals from state courts. This structure subordinated states by expanding national jurisdiction over citizenship, naturalization, and even state boundaries with legislative approval, while prohibiting states from interfering in foreign affairs or emitting paper money without consent. Madison's preparatory notes reveal his intent to create a consolidated system where national institutions could coerce compliance from states, addressing the Articles' failures in taxation, commerce, and military enforcement through direct national power rather than reliance on state voluntarism.

### New Jersey Plan: Preservation of State Equality

William Paterson of New Jersey introduced the New Jersey Plan on June 15, 1787, as a counterproposal to the Virginia Plan during the Constitutional Convention. This plan aimed to revise the Articles of Confederation to grant the federal government greater authority while preserving equal representation for all states in a unicameral legislature, where each state cast one vote irrespective of population or wealth. The approach reflected the priorities of smaller states, which sought to prevent domination by larger ones through proportional apportionment.

The first resolution explicitly called for correcting and enlarging the Articles to meet the union's exigencies without supplanting the confederation's core structure of state equality. Subsequent resolutions empowered Congress to collect taxes, imposts, and duties directly from individuals, bypassing unreliable state requisitions that had plagued the confederation since 1781. Congress would also gain authority to regulate foreign and interstate commerce, appoint a plural executive for enforcement, and establish a supreme judiciary to adjudicate federal matters, all while maintaining the single-vote principle to safeguard state parity.

This preservation of equal suffrage addressed causal weaknesses in the confederation, such as interstate disputes and economic disarray, by bolstering federal powers without eroding state sovereignty or allowing demographic imbalances to skew decision-making. Paterson argued that the Virginia Plan's proportional model would render small states "ciphers in the Nation," emphasizing that equal votes had sustained the union during the Revolutionary War and should continue to ensure balanced governance. The plan's rejection of bicameralism and population-based seats underscored a commitment to confederative realism, where state equality functioned as a bulwark against centralized overreach by populous entities.

### Pinckney and Paterson Plans: Alternative Frameworks

Charles Cotesworth Pinckney submitted a detailed draft plan for a federal government on May 29, 1787, the same day as the Virginia Plan's presentation. The original document is lost, with scholars relying on a synopsis in James Wilson's handwriting and later reconstructions for its contents. Key provisions included a bicameral legislature: a House of Delegates apportioned by population (one representative per 1,000 inhabitants, elected directly by the people) and a Senate selected by the House, divided into four rotating classes serving four-year terms. The plan vested broad legislative powers in Congress, such as taxation, commerce regulation, coining money, and declaring war (requiring two-thirds approval for some acts), alongside exclusive federal authority over treaties and naturalization.

The executive under the Pinckney Plan featured a single president, elected annually or for seven years by a joint legislative ballot, serving as commander-in-chief with pardon powers (except in impeachment cases) and the ability to convene or prorogue the legislature. A national judiciary, including a Supreme Court with jurisdiction over cases involving federal law, foreign affairs, and interstate disputes, was to be established by the legislature, with judges holding office during good behavior and fixed salaries. Unlike the Virginia Plan's emphasis on state legislatures electing the upper house and broader national veto powers, Pinckney's framework proposed House selection of senators and included provisions for state compliance with federal acts enforced by national authority, positioning it as a parallel nationalist blueprint that influenced later committee work but received limited direct debate.

William Paterson of New Jersey introduced an alternative proposal on June 15, 1787, aimed at revising the Articles of Confederation rather than supplanting them with a consolidated national government. This Paterson Plan, often termed the New Jersey Plan, retained a unicameral Congress with equal suffrage for each state, countering the proportional representation favored by larger states in the Virginia and Pinckney frameworks. It enhanced congressional powers to include direct taxation, commerce regulation, and army maintenance, while proposing a plural executive (a council of states) and a federal supreme tribunal to adjudicate interstate disputes.

## Legislative Branch Resolutions

### Apportionment: Proportional versus Equal Suffrage

The debate over legislative apportionment at the Constitutional Convention arose from conflicting visions of federal structure, pitting advocates of representation proportional to population against proponents of equal state suffrage akin to the Articles of Confederation. On May 29, 1787, Edmund Randolph introduced the Virginia Plan, which in its fourth resolution stipulated that "the rights of suffrage in the National Legislature ought to be proportioned to the Quotas of contribution, or to the number of free inhabitants, as the one or the other rule may seem best in different cases," applying this principle to both branches of a proposed bicameral congress. This approach, favored by larger states like Virginia, Pennsylvania, and Massachusetts, aimed to reflect demographic realities and ensure that legislative power aligned with the economic and human resources of each state, thereby promoting a robust national government capable of addressing collective action problems under the weak confederation.

Smaller states, including New Jersey, Delaware, and Maryland, vehemently opposed proportional representation, viewing it as a threat to their sovereignty and influence. They argued that equal suffrage preserved the confederated equality of states as distinct political entities, preventing domination by populous states that could marginalize smaller ones in decisions on taxation, commerce, and war. On June 11, 1787, in the Committee of the Whole, delegates voted 9–2 to affirm proportional suffrage in the first branch based on free inhabitants (with Delaware and New Jersey dissenting), but rejected extending proportionality to the second branch by a narrow 5–6 margin, signaling deepening divisions. James Madison defended proportionality as essential for republican justice, contending that unequal state sizes demanded weighted representation to avoid the tyranny of small states over large ones, much as majority rule operated within states despite varying county populations; he warned that equal suffrage would incentivize small states to fragment the union or ally against larger ones.

In contrast, delegates like William Paterson of New Jersey emphasized the confederation's foundational principle of state equality, asserting on June 15, 1787, in presenting the New Jersey Plan that each state deserved one vote in a unicameral legislature to safeguard against absorption into a consolidated national system favoring population centers. John Dickinson of Delaware echoed this, arguing that proportional schemes ignored states' role as compacting entities and could lead to instability, as smaller states might secede rather than submit to majority rule by sheer numbers. The impasse intensified through June, with small-state delegates threatening to abandon the convention and revert to the Articles, while large-state proponents like Gouverneur Morris highlighted empirical disparities—Virginia's population exceeding that of several small states combined—as evidence that equal votes would distort incentives for national cohesion and fiscal responsibility. These exchanges underscored a core tension: proportionality promised efficiency and fairness to inhabitants but risked alienating state-level interests, whereas equality upheld federal balance at the cost of representational equity.

### Bicameralism and the Great Compromise

The Virginia Plan, presented by Edmund Randolph on May 29, 1787, outlined a bicameral legislature for the national government, with representation in both houses determined proportionally by state population or contributions to the federal treasury, aiming to reflect the democratic principle of popular sovereignty while enhancing national authority. Larger states such as Virginia and Pennsylvania supported this structure, viewing it as essential for a robust federal system where legislative power scaled with demographic and economic weight.

Smaller states, including New Jersey and Delaware, vehemently opposed proportional representation in both chambers, arguing it would diminish their influence and erode the equal sovereignty states enjoyed under the Articles of Confederation, potentially leading to domination by populous states. On June 15, 1787, William Paterson introduced the New Jersey Plan, which retained a unicameral Congress with one vote per state to preserve confederative equality, highlighting the tension between national and federal models of union. Initial votes on June 11 had affirmed proportional representation for a first branch but deadlocked on the second, prompting extended debates and near-impasse by early July.

The breakthrough came with the Connecticut Compromise, also known as the Great Compromise, proposed by Connecticut delegates Roger Sherman and Oliver Ellsworth around July 14, 1787, which reconciled the divide by establishing a bicameral Congress: the lower house (later the House of Representatives) apportioned by population, granting larger states greater influence, and the upper house (the Senate) providing equal representation with two senators per state to safeguard smaller states' interests. This hybrid structure affirmed bicameralism—drawing from colonial precedents like the British Parliament and state constitutions—while balancing popular and state-based representation, as James Madison noted it served to refine legislation through successive filters.

### Three-Fifths Clause and Population Counting

The debates over apportioning representation in the House of Representatives hinged on how to count population, particularly enslaved persons, who comprised a significant portion of the population in Southern states but lacked political rights. Delegates from Southern states, including South Carolina's Charles Pinckney and Pierce Butler, advocated counting enslaved individuals equivalently to free persons for representation purposes, arguing that slaves contributed to state wealth and military strength despite their involuntary status. Northern delegates, such as Pennsylvania's Gouverneur Morris and Massachusetts's Rufus King, countered that counting non-voting, non-taxpaying enslaved persons would inflate Southern influence in Congress without corresponding obligations, equating it to granting political power to property rather than people.

The three-fifths ratio originated in a 1783 Continental Congress resolution, which proposed apportioning direct taxes among states by counting the "whole number of white and other free citizens and inhabitants" plus three-fifths of "all other persons" (enslaved individuals) to equitably distribute fiscal burdens based on productive capacity, as slaves generated economic output but were not free laborers. This formula, initially for taxation under the Articles of Confederation, was revived at the 1787 Convention during apportionment discussions following the Great Compromise on legislative structure. On July 11, 1787, South Carolina's John Rutledge moved to adopt the three-fifths rule for representation, with minimal opposition, as it balanced Southern demands against Northern resistance to full inclusion; the motion passed without division the next day, extending the ratio to both House seats and direct taxes.

The clause, enshrined in Article I, Section 2, Clause 3 of the Constitution, stated: "Representatives and direct Taxes shall be apportioned among the several States which may be included within this Union, according to their respective Numbers, which shall be determined by adding to the whole Number of free Persons, including those bound to Service for a Term of Years, and excluding Indians not taxed, three fifths of all other Persons." This applied the ratio to enslaved Africans but excluded Native Americans not taxed and indentured servants (counted fully as they had eventual freedom). The provision incentivized Southern states by enhancing their congressional delegation—adding roughly 20-30 extra House seats initially—while tying taxation to the same metric, though direct taxes were rarely levied post-ratification.

## Executive Branch Formation

### Single Executive versus Committee Structure

The debate over the structure of the national executive at the Constitutional Convention of 1787 centered on whether to establish a single individual or a plural committee to exercise executive power, reflecting concerns about efficiency, accountability, and the risk of monarchical abuse. On June 1, delegates initially considered the Virginia Plan's provision for a single executive but postponed a decision to first define the executive's powers, such as implementing laws and appointing officers. Elbridge Gerry of Massachusetts advocated for a council to advise the executive, arguing it would lend weight and public confidence, while Edmund Randolph of Virginia opposed a unitary executive, fearing it concentrated too much authority in one person. George Mason of Virginia proposed a three-member executive divided by geographic regions to ensure mutual checks and prevent corruption.

Proponents of a single executive emphasized its advantages in promoting responsibility, decisiveness, and vigor in administration. James Wilson of Pennsylvania argued that all thirteen state constitutions vested executive authority in a single governor without notable abuses, asserting that a plural executive would diffuse accountability and invite intrigue among members. He contended that unity would enable clear attribution of success or failure, contrasting it with a committee's potential for divided blame. Gouverneur Morris and others echoed this, warning that multiple executives could lead to paralysis, likening a three-headed executive to an impractical entity. Roger Sherman of Connecticut supported plurality to mitigate corruption risks but acknowledged the need for council advice even under a single head.

On June 4, 1787, the Convention voted 7 states to 3 in favor of a single executive, with New York, New Jersey, and Maryland dissenting, effectively rejecting a committee structure. This decision aligned with state-level precedents and addressed fears of executive weakness under the Articles of Confederation, where congressional committees had proven inefficient. Although some delegates like Gerry continued to favor an advisory council, the final Constitution vested executive power solely in one President without institutionalizing a plural body or mandatory council, relying instead on checks from Congress and the judiciary.

### Mode of Election and Term Length

The delegates initially favored election of the executive by the national legislature to promote independence from legislative influence while ensuring qualified selection, with a vote on June 1, 1787, approving this method by 7 to 3. James Wilson of Pennsylvania advocated direct popular election, arguing it would foster public accountability and prevent aristocratic control, but this faced opposition due to concerns over voter ignorance in a large republic and uneven state populations. Charles Cotesworth Pinckney and John Rutledge of South Carolina warned that popular election could lead to factionalism and regional divisions, preferring legislative selection to maintain balance.

By early September, after multiple reconsiderations, the Committee of Eleven proposed the Electoral College system on September 4, 1787, wherein electors—equal to a state's congressional representation—would be appointed by state legislatures and vote for president, with the House selecting from top candidates if no majority emerged. This compromise addressed fears of congressional corruption in direct appointment, as Alexander Hamilton noted the risk of executive dependence on legislators, while accommodating small states' insistence on influence beyond population proportion. The convention approved the electoral method on September 6 after over 60 votes on variations, rejecting pure popular vote (opposed by delegates like Roger Sherman for logistical impracticality) and reverting to legislative election.

Regarding term length, the convention on June 4, 1787, adopted a seven-year single term with ineligibility for reelection, aiming to balance experience with prevention of monarchical entrenchment, as proposed by Madison and supported by eight states to two. Elbridge Gerry and others argued a single term would encourage short-term popularity-seeking over long-term governance, while Gouverneur Morris later contended reelection eligibility would incentivize fidelity to public good. On August 24, the delegates shifted to a four-year term with reelection permitted, reflecting a compromise to allow tested leaders continuity without fixed lifetime tenure, as Hamilton had suggested good-behavior terms akin to judges but deemed impractical. This four-year duration was finalized on September 17, 1787, providing flexibility for removal via impeachment while enabling periodic accountability through electors.

### Powers, Veto, and Removal Processes

The delegates to the Constitutional Convention of 1787, building on the Virginia Plan's seventh resolution, vested the national executive with the power to carry federal laws into effect and to exercise a general executive authority independent of the legislature. On June 1, 1787, the Convention, sitting as a Committee of the Whole, unanimously resolved that the executive should possess the authority to implement national laws, reflecting concerns over the Confederation's weak enforcement mechanisms. Additional powers outlined included serving as commander in chief of the United States' military forces, granting reprieves and pardons (except in cases of impeachment), and, with Senate advice and consent, making treaties and appointing key officers such as ambassadors, judges, and cabinet members. These allocations aimed to ensure energetic administration while separating executive functions from legislative control, as advocated by figures like James Wilson, who emphasized a unitary executive to prevent diffusion of responsibility.

Debates on the executive's veto power over legislation intensified on August 15, 1787, amid fears of legislative dominance and executive weakness. James Madison proposed granting both the executive and the Supreme Court a veto, overridable by a two-thirds vote of each congressional house, to protect against hasty or unjust laws; James Wilson seconded the motion, arguing for judicial involvement to safeguard constitutional limits. Opponents, including John Dickinson and Roger Sherman, contended that judicial veto would undermine separation of powers and invite bias, while Gouverneur Morris advocated an absolute executive veto to check legislative overreach. The Convention rejected the joint veto and absolute authority, instead adopting a qualified veto for the executive alone: the president could return bills with objections within ten days, subject to override by two-thirds majorities in both houses of Congress, a compromise Madison framed as balancing executive independence with legislative supremacy.

The process for removing the executive centered on impeachment to address misconduct without destabilizing the office's independence. On July 20, 1787, delegates like Edmund Randolph argued the executive should be impeachable for treachery, corruption of electors, or incapacity, rather than mere good behavior tenure, to maintain accountability. By September 8, 1787, George Mason successfully moved to expand grounds beyond treason and bribery to include "other high crimes and misdemeanors against the United States," warning that narrow definitions might enable constitutional subversion, such as obstructing amendments; Madison countered that vague terms like "maladministration" would foster legislative dependence, preferring Supreme Court trials over Senate jurisdiction. The Convention resolved impeachment by the House of Representatives, with trial by the Senate (requiring two-thirds conviction for removal), applying to the president, vice president, and civil officers, thereby institutionalizing a political check calibrated to serious abuses rather than policy disputes.
