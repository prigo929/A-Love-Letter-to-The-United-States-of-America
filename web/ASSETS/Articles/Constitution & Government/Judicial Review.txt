# Judicial Review

## Introduction

Judicial review in the United States is the doctrine empowering federal courts, led by the Supreme Court, to assess the constitutionality of statutes enacted by Congress, executive actions by the President, and occasionally state laws, with the authority to nullify those found violative of the U.S. Constitution. This mechanism, integral to the separation of powers and checks and balances, originated implicitly from constitutional structure rather than explicit textual grant, as the framers anticipated judicial invalidation of unconstitutional measures without codifying the process.

The foundational precedent emerged in Marbury v. Madison (1803), where Chief Justice John Marshall declared a provision of the Judiciary Act of 1789 unconstitutional, asserting that "it is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is" and establishing the Constitution's supremacy over conflicting legislation. This decision transformed the judiciary from a passive interpreter to an active guardian of constitutional boundaries, enabling the Court to void federal laws in pivotal cases spanning commerce regulation, civil liberties, and federalism disputes.

While judicial review has fortified individual rights against majority overreach and constrained governmental excess, it remains contentious for vesting policy-shaping authority in unelected judges, potentially supplanting legislative will and fueling cycles of perceived activism that alternate with partisan shifts in Court composition. Proponents emphasize its role in upholding original constitutional limits amid evolving political pressures, whereas detractors highlight risks of interpretive overreach absent direct electoral accountability, prompting ongoing scholarly and political scrutiny of its democratic compatibility.

## Historical Foundations

### Judicial Review Before the Constitution

In the colonial era, American judges occasionally invoked higher law principles to assess the validity of statutes, drawing from English common law precedents such as Dr. Bonham's Case (1610), where Chief Justice Edward Coke asserted that common law could override parliamentary acts repugnant to reason or fundamental rights. This doctrine, emphasizing that "an act of Parliament can be controlled by the common law," influenced colonial legal thought by suggesting courts could void legislation conflicting with natural rights or charters. However, practical exercises of such review were limited, as colonial courts primarily deferred to royal charters and Privy Council disallowances, which functioned more as executive oversight than judicial nullification.

A pivotal early argument for judicial oversight emerged in Paxton's Case (1761) in Massachusetts, where lawyer James Otis Jr. challenged general writs of assistance—open-ended search warrants issued by customs officials—as violations of natural rights and English law, contending that statutes enabling them were void if contrary to fundamental liberties. Otis's oration, later recounted by John Adams as a catalyst for independence, invoked a "law of nature" superior to positive law, laying groundwork for courts to judge legislative acts against constitutional limits, though the Superior Court ultimately upheld the writs on February 24, 1761. This case highlighted tensions between statutory authority and inherent rights, prefiguring post-independence judicial practices without establishing routine review.

Following the Declaration of Independence, several state courts between 1776 and 1787 exercised what resembled judicial review by invalidating laws under new state constitutions, reflecting framers' familiarity with limited legislative power. In Holmes v. Walton (1780) in New Jersey, the state supreme court voided a treason law provision allowing six-man juries, deeming it inconsistent with the 1776 constitution's guarantee of twelve-person petit juries for criminal trials. Similarly, in Bayard v. Singleton (1787) in North Carolina, judges declared a statute barring judicial inquiry into foreign jury attachments unconstitutional, asserting the court's duty to disregard laws violating the state bill of rights. Rhode Island's Superior Court of Judicature also struck down a 1786 paper money law as impairing contracts, though this provoked legislative backlash and judges' impeachment, underscoring uneven acceptance.

### Provisions of the Constitution

Article III of the United States Constitution establishes the federal judiciary and delineates its core powers, providing the structural basis for judicial review without explicitly naming it. Section 1 vests "the judicial Power of the United States" in "one supreme Court, and in such inferior Courts as the Congress may from time to time ordain and establish," with judges holding office during good Behaviour and receiving compensation that "shall neither be increased nor diminished during the Period for which they shall have been elected, or during which they shall have held their Offices." This tenure protection insulates federal judges from political pressures, enabling independent assessment of legislative and executive actions against constitutional limits.

Section 2, Clause 1 of Article III extends the judicial power to "all Cases, in Law and Equity, arising under this Constitution, the Laws of the United States, and Treaties made, or which shall be made, under their Authority," as well as to controversies involving ambassadors, public ministers, states, citizens of different states, and other specified parties. This jurisdictional grant empowers federal courts to adjudicate disputes where the validity of federal laws or actions is questioned in relation to the Constitution, implying a role in interpreting constitutional supremacy over ordinary statutes. Clause 3 further specifies that trials for certain federal crimes occur via jury in the state of the offense, reinforcing procedural safeguards but underscoring the judiciary's interpretive authority in enforcing constitutional rights.

Article VI, Clause 2—the Supremacy Clause—declares that "this Constitution, and the Laws of the United States which shall be made in Pursuance thereof; and all Treaties made, or which shall be made, under the Authority of the United States, shall be the supreme Law of the Land; and the Judges in every State shall be bound thereby, any Thing in the Constitution or Laws of any State to the Contrary notwithstanding." This clause prioritizes the Constitution over conflicting state laws and establishes that only federal laws enacted "in Pursuance" of constitutional authority qualify as supreme, thereby implying a mechanism to void non-conforming federal enactments as well. Clause 3 mandates that "all executive and judicial Officers, both of the United States and of the several States, shall be bound by Oath or Affirmation, to support this Constitution," obligating judges to uphold it as paramount, even against federal statutes lacking constitutional fidelity.

### Framers' Views on Judicial Review

The Framers of the U.S. Constitution did not explicitly debate or codify a power of judicial review over acts of Congress during the 1787 Constitutional Convention, with records showing limited discussion focused more on judicial structure, tenure, and review of state laws rather than federal statutes. Convention notes indicate concerns over judicial independence and the supremacy clause's implications, but delegates like James Madison emphasized the judiciary's role in interpreting the Constitution without granting it explicit veto authority over legislative acts. This omission reflected an assumption among proponents that such power was implied through Article III's vesting of "the judicial Power" and Article VI's supremacy clause, obligating officials to uphold the Constitution as supreme law.

Alexander Hamilton provided the most articulate endorsement in Federalist No. 78 (June 14, 1788), arguing that the judiciary, as the "least dangerous" branch lacking purse or sword, must possess the authority to declare legislative acts void if they contravene the Constitution, serving as a bulwark against legislative overreach. Hamilton contended this power was essential to prevent temporary majorities from undermining fundamental law, likening the Constitution to a "fundamental law" that courts enforce through interpretive supremacy, though he anticipated its exercise would be rare and confined to clear constitutional violations. In Federalist No. 81, he extended this to affirm courts' role in checking state encroachments but reiterated limits, noting the judiciary's dependence on other branches for enforcement. James Madison echoed support in Federalist No. 39 and convention notes, viewing judicial review as compatible with republican principles by ensuring laws conformed to constitutional bounds, though he prioritized departmental interpretation where each branch asserts its view of constitutionality.

Thomas Jefferson expressed reservations, warning in letters post-ratification that unchecked judicial review risked "despotism" by unelected judges, advocating instead for periodic constitutional revisions and coordinate branch interpretations to avoid judicial monopoly. Antifederalists, such as Brutus in essays opposing ratification, criticized the implied power as enabling judicial oligarchy, predicting it would expand federal authority beyond enumerated limits. Collectively, while Hamilton and Madison defended judicial review as a defensive check, the Framers envisioned it as subordinate to interbranch balance, not a supremacy eclipsing legislative will, with enforcement reliant on executive and congressional acquiescence.

## Establishment Through Key Cases

### Pre-Marbury Developments

Prior to the establishment of judicial review by the U.S. Supreme Court in Marbury v. Madison (1803), instances of courts invalidating legislative acts occurred in colonial America and proliferated in state courts following independence, laying groundwork for the federal practice. In the colonial period, judicial nullification of laws was rare but precedent-setting, often drawing on English common law principles like those articulated in Dr. Bonham's Case (1610), where Sir Edward Coke asserted that statutes repugnant to reason or common law were void. The earliest clear American example was Giddings v. Brown (1657) in a Massachusetts Bay Colony court, which invalidated a legislative resolution granting a monopoly on horse trading as contrary to common right and natural equity, without explicit reference to a written constitution.

After the Revolution, state courts frequently exercised judicial review against state legislatures, particularly to enforce new state constitutions' protections for jury trials, property rights, and judicial independence, with at least 19 documented cases between 1780 and 1803 resulting in 15 invalidations. In Holmes v. Walton (1780), the New Jersey Supreme Court struck down a state law limiting jury sizes in treason trials as violating the state constitution's jury trial guarantee. Trevett v. Weeden (1786) saw Rhode Island's Superior Court invalidate a statute denying jury trials for penalties on depreciated currency, citing natural law and historic rights, though the decision sparked legislative backlash without nullifying the court's authority. Similarly, in Bayard v. Singleton (1787), the North Carolina Superior Court nullified a law excluding Loyalist testimony in property confiscation cases, holding it unconstitutional for infringing the right to trial by jury under the state constitution and separation of powers, marking one of the most explicit assertions of review power. Other notable state cases included the Symsbury Case (1785, Connecticut), where the Superior Court rejected a legislative land grant as an improper judicial function, and Kamper v. Hawkins (1793, Virginia), invalidating a statute expanding district courts' equity jurisdiction without tenure protections, as violating constitutional judicial independence.

Under the federal Constitution, early judicial review emerged in circuit courts and the Supreme Court, often applying structural constitutional limits, treaties, or the Supremacy Clause, with at least seven federal invalidations by 1803. Hayburn's Case (1792) involved federal circuit courts refusing to adjudicate invalid pension claims under the 1792 Invalid Pensions Act, deeming it an unconstitutional assignment of non-judicial duties to the judiciary, as expressed in opinions by Justices Wilson, Blair, and Peters. In Vanhorne's Lessee v. Dorrance (1795), a Pennsylvania circuit court (with Justice Paterson) struck down a Pennsylvania quiet-title statute for retroactively divesting vested property rights without due process, invoking both state and federal constitutional protections. The Supreme Court in Ware v. Hylton (1796) invalidated a 1777 Virginia sequestration law confiscating British debts, prioritizing the Treaty of Paris and Supremacy Clause over conflicting state legislation. Cases like Hollingsworth v. Virginia (1798) further demonstrated review by denying retroactive application of the Judiciary Act's appellate provisions in light of the Eleventh Amendment, effectively limiting congressional jurisdiction. These federal instances reflected a structural approach, with stricter scrutiny for laws encroaching on judicial functions or federal supremacy, and growing acceptance without widespread reversal by other branches.

### Marbury v. Madison and Its Immediate Context

The contentious election of 1800 pitted incumbent Federalist President John Adams against Democratic-Republican Thomas Jefferson, resulting in Jefferson's victory after a prolonged deadlock in the House of Representatives that was resolved on February 17, 1801. In the lame-duck period, the outgoing Federalist-controlled Congress passed the Judiciary Act of 1801 on February 13, creating new circuit courts and sixteen new judgeships to bolster Federalist influence in the judiciary. President Adams promptly nominated and the Senate confirmed Federalist appointees to these positions, known as "midnight judges" due to the rushed commissions signed in his final days before Jefferson's inauguration on March 4, 1801.

Among these appointees was William Marbury, nominated on February 27, 1801, and confirmed by the Senate as a justice of the peace for the District of Columbia, a position with original jurisdiction over minor civil and criminal matters. Adams signed Marbury's commission, but as Secretary of State John Marshall—recently confirmed as Chief Justice on January 27, 1801, yet continuing in his prior role until March 4—he failed to deliver it before the administration change, leaving approximately seventeen such undelivered Federalist commissions, including Marbury's. Upon taking office, President Jefferson instructed his new Secretary of State, James Madison, to withhold these undelivered commissions, viewing the appointments as an illegitimate Federalist entrenchment effort, while the incoming Republican Congress repealed the Judiciary Act of 1801 on March 3, 1802, and briefly abolished lower federal courts before restoring them under the Judiciary Act of 1802.

On December 21, 1801, Marbury petitioned the Supreme Court directly for a writ of mandamus compelling Madison to deliver his commission, invoking Section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, which authorized the Court to issue such writs in cases warranting judicial control over executive officers acting in ministerial capacities. The case, argued before the Court in February 1803 amid heightened partisan tensions—including Republican impeachment efforts against Federalist judges—culminated in Chief Justice Marshall's opinion on February 24, 1803. Marshall affirmed Marbury's legal entitlement to the commission and the appropriateness of mandamus as a remedy but held that Section 13 unconstitutionally expanded the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction beyond Article III's limits, which confine such jurisdiction to cases involving ambassadors, public ministers, consuls, and states. By voiding this statutory provision as repugnant to the Constitution, the Court effectively denied Marbury relief while asserting its authority to declare acts of Congress unconstitutional, thereby establishing judicial review as a core mechanism for enforcing constitutional supremacy. This ruling navigated political risks by rebuking Congress without directly challenging the Jefferson administration, preserving the Court's institutional position in a era of executive dominance.

### Early Expansion in the 19th Century

Following Marbury v. Madison (1803), the Supreme Court under Chief Justice John Marshall expanded judicial review by invalidating state laws that conflicted with the U.S. Constitution, beginning with Fletcher v. Peck (1810), where the Court struck down a Georgia statute rescinding a land grant as violating the Contract Clause in Article I, Section 10, marking the first exercise of this power against a state legislature. In Martin v. Hunter's Lessee (1816), the Court upheld Section 25 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, affirming its authority to review state court decisions implicating federal law or treaties, as Justice Joseph Story argued that uniform interpretation required federal supremacy over state judiciaries to prevent inconsistent applications of national authority.

This authority was further solidified in McCulloch v. Maryland (1819), where the Court invalidated Maryland's tax on the Second Bank of the United States as unconstitutional under the Supremacy Clause, while upholding Congress's implied powers under the Necessary and Proper Clause to charter the bank, thereby extending judicial review to protect federal institutions from state interference. Similarly, in Cohens v. Virginia (1821), the Court confirmed its appellate jurisdiction over state criminal convictions raising federal questions, rejecting Virginia's sovereign immunity claims and reinforcing that states could not evade federal oversight even in non-capital cases. These rulings collectively entrenched the judiciary's role in enforcing federal supremacy, as Marshall's opinions emphasized the Constitution's binding nature over state actions.

The expansion continued with applications to interstate commerce and contracts, as in Gibbons v. Ogden (1824), which broadly interpreted the Commerce Clause to strike down a New York steamboat monopoly conflicting with federal licensing, though primarily affirming congressional power rather than directly invalidating state law. However, enforcement limits emerged in Worcester v. Georgia (1832), where the Court declared Georgia's extension laws over Cherokee lands unconstitutional under federal treaties and the Commerce Clause, asserting exclusive federal jurisdiction over Indian affairs, yet President Andrew Jackson declined to enforce the ruling, reportedly stating that Marshall's decision lacked practical effect without executive support.

## Evolution and Doctrinal Development

### 20th-Century Growth and Landmark Cases

In the early 20th century, the Supreme Court expanded judicial review through the doctrine of substantive due process under the Fourteenth Amendment, frequently invalidating state economic regulations deemed to infringe on freedom of contract and liberty interests. In Lochner v. New York (1905), the Court struck down a New York law limiting bakers' work to ten hours per day or sixty per week, holding in a 5-4 decision that it violated due process by interfering with employer-employee agreements absent a legitimate health justification. This case epitomized the "Lochner era" (roughly 1905–1937), during which the Court invalidated approximately 197 state and federal regulations on similar grounds, prioritizing individual economic liberty over legislative reforms addressing industrialization's excesses. Other notable decisions included Adkins v. Children's Hospital (1923), which struck down a District of Columbia minimum wage law for women as an arbitrary interference with contract freedom.

The Court's interventionist stance peaked during the New Deal, challenging President Franklin D. Roosevelt's expansive federal programs. In A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States (1935), the Court unanimously invalidated the National Industrial Recovery Act's delegation of rulemaking to private industry codes, ruling it exceeded Congress's commerce power and violated separation of powers. Similarly, United States v. Butler (1936) struck down the Agricultural Adjustment Act's processing taxes funding farmer subsidies, deeming them an unconstitutional invasion of state sovereignty under the Tenth Amendment. These rulings prompted Roosevelt's 1937 court-packing plan to add justices and dilute conservative influence, but following the plan's defeat and Justice Willis Van Devanter's retirement, the Court shifted in cases like West Coast Hotel Co. v. Parrish (1937), upholding a Washington minimum wage law and signaling deference to economic legislation. This "switch in time that saved nine" marked a retreat from economic substantive due process, with the Court sustaining New Deal measures such as the National Labor Relations Act in NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp. (1937).

Post-1937, judicial review contracted in economic spheres but surged in civil liberties and individual rights, particularly under Chief Justice Earl Warren (1953–1969), as the Court incorporated Bill of Rights protections against states via the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. Brown v. Board of Education (1954) unanimously declared state-mandated school segregation unconstitutional under the Equal Protection Clause, overruling Plessy v. Ferguson's "separate but equal" doctrine and catalyzing desegregation efforts despite southern resistance. The Warren era produced landmark expansions, including Gideon v. Wainwright (1963), extending the Sixth Amendment right to counsel to indigent felony defendants in state courts; Miranda v. Arizona (1966), requiring police to inform suspects of rights to silence and counsel to safeguard Fifth Amendment protections; and New York Times Co. v. Sullivan (1964), establishing an "actual malice" standard for defamation suits by public officials to bolster First Amendment freedoms. Griswold v. Connecticut (1965) recognized a right to marital privacy, invalidating a state ban on contraceptives and laying groundwork for later privacy-based rulings.

### Standards of Judicial Review

In the exercise of judicial review, United States federal courts apply tiered standards of scrutiny to assess the constitutionality of government actions, particularly under the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment and the Due Process Clauses of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments. These standards determine the degree of deference afforded to legislative or executive decisions, with higher scrutiny reserved for burdens on fundamental rights or discrimination against suspect classes, reflecting a judicial balancing of democratic processes against constitutional protections.

The lowest tier, rational basis review, applies to most social and economic regulations lacking a suspect classification or impact on fundamental rights. Under this standard, a law is upheld if it is rationally related to a legitimate government interest, with courts granting substantial deference to the legislature and rarely invalidating statutes—upholding them in approximately 99% of challenges. This test presumes validity and allows considerable flexibility in means-ends fit, as seen in applications to distinctions based on age, wealth, or economic activity.

Intermediate scrutiny, or heightened review, occupies the middle tier and requires the government to demonstrate that the challenged action is substantially related to an important governmental objective, employing a more probing examination of the fit between means and ends without demanding the least restrictive alternative. It typically applies to gender-based classifications or content-neutral restrictions on speech, where laws survive in about half of cases due to the balanced burden on the government.

### Administrative Review and Deference Doctrines

Judicial review of federal administrative agency actions occurs under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) of 1946, which empowers courts to set aside agency actions, findings, and conclusions determined to be arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law; in excess of statutory jurisdiction, authority, or limitations; short of statutory right; without observance of required procedure; unsupported by substantial evidence in formal proceedings; or unwarranted by the facts as found after hearing. This framework ensures agencies adhere to statutory mandates while allowing courts to assess legal interpretations without automatic deference. Deference doctrines have historically modulated the intensity of review applied to agency readings of statutes and regulations, balancing agency expertise against judicial independence.

The baseline for deference emerged in Skidmore v. Swift & Co. (1944), where the Supreme Court ruled that administrative interpretations merit respect based on their persuasiveness, evaluated by factors including the thoroughness of consideration, validity of reasoning, consistency with prior positions, and overall power to convince, rather than mandatory compliance. This non-binding approach acknowledges agency specialization without ceding interpretive authority to the executive branch. Skidmore deference applies broadly to informal agency guidance, such as opinion letters or bulletins, where Congress has not signaled intent for greater weight.

Chevron deference, established in Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc. (1984), imposed a stricter obligation on courts: if a statute is silent or ambiguous on a precise issue and the agency administers the statute, courts must accept the agency's reasonable interpretation under a two-step test—first ascertaining clear congressional intent, and second upholding permissible agency constructions. This doctrine expanded agency leeway, particularly in notice-and-comment rulemaking, but faced critique for undermining judicial statutory interpretation and enabling executive overreach beyond legislative text. In United States v. Mead Corp. (2001), the Court limited Chevron's reach, holding it applies only when procedures suggest Congress expected deference, such as formal rulemaking carrying lawmaking force; tariff rulings via letter, lacking such indicia, revert to Skidmore.

## Limitations, Reforms, and Recent Developments

### Statutory and Constitutional Limits

Article III of the United States Constitution confines federal judicial power to actual "cases" or "controversies," thereby prohibiting advisory opinions and requiring concrete disputes between adverse parties with sufficient stakes to ensure rigorous adjudication. This limitation, rooted in the framers' intent to prevent courts from issuing abstract rulings akin to legislative policy declarations, was affirmed early by Chief Justice John Jay in 1793 correspondence declining hypothetical questions from President Washington. Derived doctrines of justiciability further operationalize these bounds: standing demands that plaintiffs demonstrate personal injury in fact causally linked to the defendant's conduct and redressable by judicial relief, as articulated in Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife (504 U.S. 555, 1992), where the Supreme Court rejected environmental groups' challenge to overseas Endangered Species Act regulations for lacking concrete harm to U.S. citizens. Ripeness requires disputes to be sufficiently mature to avoid premature entanglement in speculative issues, while mootness dismisses cases where intervening events eliminate live controversies, such as a temporary injunction rendered obsolete post-expiration.

The political question doctrine constitutes another constitutional barrier, withholding review where issues are textually committed to coordinate branches, lack judicially manageable standards, or risk improper judicial intrusion into executive or legislative prerogatives, per the multifactor test in Baker v. Carr (369 U.S. 186, 1962). This doctrine has barred federal courts from adjudicating matters like the President's impeachment process (Nixon v. United States, 506 U.S. 224, 1993) or certain foreign relations decisions, preserving separation of powers by deferring to political accountability mechanisms. The Eleventh Amendment imposes sovereign immunity, prohibiting federal courts from entertaining suits against states by citizens of other states or foreign nations unless waived or abrogated under specific constitutional authority, as interpreted to embody broader state immunity principles in Hans v. Louisiana (134 U.S. 1, 1890). This limit curtails judicial review of state actions in federal forums, compelling alternative remedies like state courts or congressional overrides via Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment, though abrogation attempts have faced strict scrutiny for exceeding remedial scope (Seminole Tribe v. Florida, 517 U.S. 44, 1996).

Statutory limits, enacted pursuant to Congress's Article III authority to ordain inferior courts and regulate appellate jurisdiction via the Exceptions Clause, include jurisdiction-stripping provisions that channel or restrict review of specific subjects. For instance, the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996 (AEDPA) imposes one-year filing deadlines and deferential standards for habeas corpus review of state convictions, limiting federal relitigation of claims adjudicated in state courts (28 U.S.C. § 2254). Immigration statutes, such as 8 U.S.C. § 1252, strip jurisdiction over certain removal orders, confining review to the courts of appeals and precluding district court intervention. The Administrative Procedure Act (APA) permits agencies to structure actions to evade review through timing or form, though courts assess such maneuvers against due process norms, with a general six-year statute of limitations applying to many claims (28 U.S.C. § 2401(a)). Congress has occasionally withdrawn jurisdiction mid-litigation, as in the 2005 REAL ID Act shifting certain immigration habeas to appellate review, but such measures must preserve core access to federal courts for constitutional claims to avoid invalidation under the Suspension Clause or due process. These statutory mechanisms reflect congressional efforts to manage docket overload and policy outcomes, yet judicial precedents enforce a presumption of reviewability absent clear statement, ensuring limits do not wholly insulate unconstitutional acts.

### Post-Chevron Shifts and Agency Power

In Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo, decided on June 28, 2024, the Supreme Court overruled the Chevron doctrine in a 6-3 decision authored by Chief Justice John Roberts, holding that courts must exercise independent judgment in interpreting statutes under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), rather than deferring to agencies' reasonable interpretations of ambiguous provisions. The ruling emphasized that Chevron conflicted with the APA's directive in 5 U.S.C. § 706 for courts to "decide all relevant questions of law," interpreting such questions de novo without deference to agency views. This shift ended a 40-year practice that had allowed executive agencies substantial leeway to expand statutory authority through interpretive rulemaking, particularly in areas like environmental regulation and fisheries management as challenged in the case.

The decision curtailed agency power by requiring agencies to adhere more strictly to congressional text, reducing their ability to fill statutory gaps with policy preferences deemed reasonable by courts. While agencies' interpretations may still receive respect under the Skidmore doctrine—proportional to their persuasiveness, expertise, and consistency with law—courts no longer presume agency superiority in legal interpretation. Critics of Chevron, including originalists, argued it unconstitutionally transferred interpretive authority from Article III courts to Article II agencies, enabling executive overreach; the majority endorsed this view, citing separation-of-powers principles. Dissenters, led by Justice Elena Kagan, contended the ruling would politicize agency actions and burden courts lacking technical expertise.

Companion developments amplified the erosion of agency autonomy. In Corner Post, Inc. v. Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System (July 17, 2024), the Court held that APA challenges to regulations accrue under the six-year statute of limitations when a plaintiff suffers injury, not when the rule is finalized, enabling suits against longstanding rules like the Federal Reserve's Regulation II debit-card interchange fees. Similarly, SEC v. Jarkesy (June 27, 2024) ruled that the Seventh Amendment guarantees jury trials for SEC civil penalty actions in federal court, invalidating in-house adjudication by administrative law judges for such claims and limiting agencies' self-contained enforcement powers. These rulings collectively facilitate greater judicial scrutiny, with post-Loper litigation surging—federal courts vacated or remanded dozens of agency actions by mid-2025, including EPA emissions standards and FTC non-compete bans—though agencies retain rulemaking initiative subject to heightened review.

### Ongoing Debates on Judicial Restraint

Contemporary debates on judicial restraint in the United States center on its tension with the judiciary's role in checking executive and legislative overreach, particularly amid a conservative Supreme Court majority emphasizing originalism and textualism since 2022. Proponents argue that restraint—manifested through adherence to constitutional text and historical meaning—prevents unelected judges from imposing policy preferences, as seen in the 2022 Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization decision, where the Court overruled Roe v. Wade (1973) and Planned Parenthood v. Casey (1992) by declining to recognize an unenumerated right to abortion, thereby returning authority to state legislatures. Critics, including some Democratic senators, contend this constitutes selective activism, overriding settled precedent without textual basis, though the majority framed it as fidelity to judicial limits by avoiding substantive due process expansions beyond historical bounds.

A notable shift in rhetoric has occurred, with liberals—who historically favored activist interpretations for progressive rights—now invoking restraint to critique originalist rulings, while conservatives, previously tolerant of deference doctrines, champion it to curb perceived judicial excesses from prior eras. This flip reflects partisan incentives rather than consistent principle, as evidenced by post-Dobbs calls for court reforms from left-leaning groups decrying "power grabs," despite their earlier support for expansive readings in cases like Griswold v. Connecticut (1965).

In administrative law, the 2024 Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo ruling exemplifies restraint debates by overruling Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc. (1984), ending mandatory deference to agency interpretations of ambiguous statutes and restoring courts' primary interpretive role under the Administrative Procedure Act. Originalists view this as restraint against unaccountable agency policymaking, aligning with separation-of-powers principles, but detractors argue it invites judicial activism by empowering courts to second-guess expert regulators on complex issues like environmental or health policy.
