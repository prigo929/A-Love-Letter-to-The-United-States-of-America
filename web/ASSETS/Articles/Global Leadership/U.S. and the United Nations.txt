# U.S. and the United Nations

## Introduction

The relationship between the United States and the United Nations centers on the U.S. role as a founding member that led the organization's creation in 1945 through initiatives like the San Francisco Conference, establishing it as a forum for postwar international cooperation on peace, security, and collective problem-solving. As the UN's largest donor, providing 22 percent of the regular budget and approximately 27 percent of peacekeeping assessments, the U.S. sustains much of the organization's operations while exercising veto power as a permanent Security Council member to block resolutions conflicting with its national interests.

This partnership has yielded collaborative successes, such as U.S.-backed UN actions in maintaining ceasefires and delivering humanitarian aid, but it is characterized by persistent tensions arising from the UN's perceived inefficiencies, selective enforcement of resolutions, and institutional biases—particularly in disproportionate condemnations of Israel—and U.S. assertions of sovereignty that have led to unilateral military actions like the 2003 Iraq intervention without full Security Council endorsement. Repeated U.S. funding withholdings, conditioned on reforms to curb waste and politicization, alongside withdrawals from bodies such as UNESCO in 2025 and the Human Rights Council, underscore demands for accountability amid criticisms that the UN often amplifies anti-Western agendas over empirical effectiveness.

Key defining features include the U.S. veto's use to safeguard allies and principles, having been cast over 80 times historically, frequently on Israel-related matters, which has both preserved strategic equilibria and fueled accusations of obstructionism, while broader controversies involve arrears exceeding $1 billion tied to congressional caps and the organization's evolving mandate clashing with American exceptionalism in foreign policy.

## Historical Foundations

### US Leadership in Founding the UN

President Franklin D. Roosevelt first coined the term "United Nations" in the Declaration by United Nations, signed on January 1, 1942, by the United States and 25 other Allied nations, committing them to fight Axis powers and foreswearing separate peace treaties, which laid an early conceptual foundation for postwar international cooperation. Roosevelt, drawing from the Atlantic Charter jointly issued with British Prime Minister Winston Churchill on August 14, 1941, envisioned a permanent organization to maintain peace, emphasizing self-determination, disarmament, and collective security as alternatives to the failed League of Nations, which the U.S. Senate had rejected in 1919 due to isolationist sentiments. His administration actively promoted this vision domestically to overcome lingering isolationism, framing the UN as essential for preventing future wars through enforceable commitments rather than mere diplomacy.

Under Secretary of State Cordell Hull, whom Roosevelt tasked with preparatory work, the U.S. hosted and led the Dumbarton Oaks Conference from August 21 to October 7, 1944, at its Washington, D.C., estate, where American, British, Soviet, and Chinese delegates drafted the foundational proposals for the UN Charter, including the structure of the General Assembly, Security Council, and an international court. Hull's efforts in coordinating these talks, despite wartime tensions, earned him recognition as the "father of the United Nations," culminating in his posthumous Nobel Peace Prize in 1945 for pivotal contributions to its creation. The U.S. delegation, emphasizing great-power primacy through the proposed veto in the Security Council, ensured the draft balanced enforcement mechanisms with national sovereignty, addressing Soviet concerns over voting procedures clarified at the Yalta Conference in February 1945.

The U.S. further demonstrated leadership by hosting the United Nations Conference on International Organization in San Francisco from April 25 to June 26, 1945, where delegates from 50 nations revised and finalized the Dumbarton Oaks proposals into the UN Charter, signed on June 26 by all participants, including a prominent U.S. delegation headed by Secretary of State Edward Stettinius. The conference incorporated U.S.-backed provisions for regional arrangements and economic cooperation, while rejecting expansive amendments that could dilute Security Council authority, reflecting American priorities for effective collective security over universalist idealism. Following ratification by the U.S. Senate on July 28, 1945, by a vote of 89-2, the Charter entered force on October 24, 1945, with the U.S. as a founding member and permanent Security Council seat holder, marking its shift from interwar non-interventionism to proactive global institution-building.

### Ratification of the UN Charter and Early Commitments

The United Nations Charter emerged from the United Nations Conference on International Organization, held in San Francisco from April 25 to June 26, 1945, where delegates from 50 countries, including a prominent U.S. delegation headed by Secretary of State Edward Stettinius Jr., finalized the text emphasizing collective action against aggression and promotion of human rights. The Charter was signed on June 26, 1945, by representatives of those nations, with the United States committing to its core provisions on maintaining international peace, sovereign equality of states, and cooperation in economic and social matters. This signing reflected U.S. postwar strategy to institutionalize multilateralism, avoiding the isolationist pitfalls that doomed the League of Nations, though domestic debates centered on preserving American sovereignty and veto power in the Security Council.

Ratification required U.S. Senate approval under Article II of the Constitution, treating the Charter as a treaty needing a two-thirds majority. After hearings and advocacy from President Harry S. Truman, who addressed the Senate on July 25, 1945, urging swift action amid World War II's conclusion, the Senate voted 89-2 in favor on July 28, 1945. The dissenting votes came from isolationist Senators who argued the Charter unduly entangled U.S. forces in foreign conflicts without explicit congressional war declarations. Truman signed the ratification instrument on August 8, 1945, paving the way for the Charter's entry into force on October 24, 1945, once ratified by the five permanent Security Council members (China, France, the Soviet Union, the United Kingdom, and the United States) and a majority of other signatories. This bipartisan Senate support—spanning Democrats and Republicans—signaled a congressional pivot from prewar non-interventionism toward structured international engagement.

Post-ratification, the United States fulfilled early commitments by actively shaping UN operations and infrastructure. U.S. delegates participated in the preparatory and transitional committees in London, contributing to the election of the first Secretary-General, Trygve Lie of Norway, in February 1946. The U.S. hosted the initial General Assembly session in New York later that year after an interim London meeting in January 1946, and in 1946, Congress authorized the purchase of 17 acres in Manhattan for the permanent headquarters, with the U.S. government advancing $65 million in loans and grants for construction, repayable via assessed dues. Financially, the U.S. accepted its Charter-mandated obligation to fund a significant share of the UN budget, providing over 40% of initial operating costs through voluntary and assessed contributions, underscoring its role as the organization's primary backer from inception. These steps operationalized U.S. pledges under Articles 43 and 106 for potential military contributions to Security Council enforcement, though early focus remained on institutional buildup amid emerging Cold War frictions.

## US Contributions to the UN

### Financial Obligations and Disproportionate Burden

As the UN's largest donor, the United States is assessed 22% of the regular budget and 26.15% of peacekeeping operations (though capped at 25% by congressional mandate), funding core operations and missions worldwide.

For peacekeeping operations, the U.S. assessment stands at 26.15 percent for 2025, reduced from 27 percent following formula revisions that increased shares for emerging economies like China (to 23.78 percent). The 2024-2025 peacekeeping budget totals $5.6 billion, implying U.S. payments of around $1.46 billion before congressional caps limiting actual disbursements to 25 percent. Including voluntary contributions to agencies, the U.S. accounted for 28 percent of the $46.4 billion in total member state funding to the UN system in 2023, surpassing the combined contributions of 183 other members.

This funding structure imposes a disproportionate burden on the U.S., as it finances a majority of core operations while other major economies contribute proportionally less relative to benefits derived or economic capacity utilization. U.S. policymakers have repeatedly cited inefficiencies, procurement scandals, and perceived anti-U.S. biases in UN bodies—such as disproportionate focus on Israel in resolutions—as justifications for the disparity, arguing that freerider behavior by low-contributors undermines accountability. To address this, Congress enacted the Helms-Biden Act in 1998, conditioning full payments on UN reforms in management, budgeting, and oversight, leading to arrears peaking at $1.6 billion by the late 1990s and partial withholdings into the 2000s. Similar tactics persisted under subsequent administrations, with U.S. arrears reaching about $1.5 billion in recent years amid demands for cost-cutting and efficiency gains. These measures have prompted incremental UN adjustments, including reduced U.S. shares in 2025, but critics maintain the burden remains outsized given persistent operational waste and misalignment with U.S. security priorities.

### Security Council Influence via Permanent Seat and Veto

The United States holds one of five permanent seats on the United Nations Security Council (UNSC), alongside the United Kingdom, France, Russia, and China, a structure established in 1945 to reflect the major Allied powers' roles in defeating fascism during World War II. This status grants the U.S. veto power over substantive resolutions under Article 27(3) of the UN Charter, which requires the concurring votes of all permanent members (P5) for adoption, alongside nine affirmative votes from the Council's 15 total members; a single negative vote from any P5 member blocks the resolution. The veto applies to non-procedural matters, such as authorizing force, imposing sanctions, or admitting new members, enabling the U.S. to safeguard its core national interests and those of close allies against multilateral coercion.

Since the UNSC's inception in 1946, the U.S. has exercised its veto 83 times as of October 2024, fewer than Russia's 155 but concentrated in targeted areas rather than routine obstruction. Over 40 of these vetoes—approximately half—pertain to resolutions addressing the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, including condemnations of Israeli settlements, military actions in Gaza or the West Bank, or calls for Palestinian statehood without preconditions aligned with U.S. policy. For instance, on December 8, 2023, the U.S. vetoed a Brazil-drafted resolution demanding an immediate humanitarian ceasefire in Gaza, citing the need to avoid undermining ongoing hostage negotiations and Israel's right to self-defense following the October 7 Hamas attacks; the vote was 13-1, with the UK abstaining. Earlier examples include 26 vetoes between 1980 and 1990 alone on drafts criticizing Israel's policies in Lebanon and the occupied territories.

This veto authority has profoundly amplified U.S. influence, allowing it to prevent the UNSC from legitimizing actions that could constrain American strategic priorities, such as countering Soviet expansion during the Cold War or maintaining alliances in the Middle East. By blocking resolutions perceived as biased or premature—often those initiated by non-aligned or Arab states—the U.S. has ensured that UNSC outputs align more closely with empirical assessments of threats, like terrorism or aggression, rather than politicized narratives. Critics, including some UN member states, argue this skews the Council toward P5 dominance, paralyzing responses to conflicts involving veto-holders' interests, yet data shows U.S. vetoes have rarely impeded consensus on non-adversarial issues, such as authorizing interventions in Korea (1950) or the Gulf War (1991), where it supported or led resolutions. The mechanism's design, insisted upon by the U.S. during Charter negotiations, reflects a realist acknowledgment that great-power consensus is prerequisite for effective collective security, averting the League of Nations' fate of irrelevance amid superpower discord.

### Contributions to Peacekeeping and Specialized Agencies

The United States has been the primary financial backer of United Nations peacekeeping operations, covering approximately 26.95% of assessed contributions for the 2024-2025 period, making it the largest single donor ahead of China at 18.69%. This funding supports over a dozen active missions deploying around 70,000 personnel globally, with U.S. contributions exceeding $15 billion in assessed payments since 2016 alone, in addition to voluntary support for logistics, training, and equipment. However, U.S. troop contributions remain minimal, typically numbering in the dozens—far below those of major providers like Bangladesh, Nepal, or India—reflecting a policy preference for financial and indirect support over direct combat involvement to mitigate risks to American personnel. In fiscal year 2025, U.S. peacekeeping funding totaled about $1.2 billion before recent congressional cuts reduced commitments to $680 million for nine missions, amid broader debates over efficiency and mission mandates.

Historically, U.S. involvement in peacekeeping dates to the UN's early observer missions in 1948, evolving into substantial logistical aid during operations like those in the Balkans and Africa, where American airlift capabilities and intelligence have bolstered mission effectiveness without large-scale ground deployments. This approach aligns with congressional caps on U.S. assessments at 25% (despite the formulaic 26.94% rate), ensuring funding leverage for reforms while avoiding open-ended commitments. Recent U.S. policy under both administrations has emphasized performance-based funding, tying contributions to measurable outcomes in stabilizing conflict zones, though critics argue this has strained operations amid rising global instability.

In specialized agencies, the U.S. provides both assessed dues and voluntary funds across entities like the World Health Organization (WHO), Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), and others, comprising a significant portion of the UN system's $46.4 billion in member-state funding for 2023, with U.S. payments at 28% or $13 billion overall. For fiscal year 2024, Congress allocated $1.54 billion through the Contributions to International Organizations account for UN specialized agencies and related bodies, supporting technical assistance in agriculture, health, and aviation standards. The U.S. played a foundational role in establishing agencies like the FAO in 1945, contributing expertise and resources to initiatives such as sustainable fisheries management and food security programs that align with American agricultural interests.

## Areas of Cooperation and Achievements

### Postwar Stabilization and Cold War Containment

Following World War II, the United States played a pivotal role in leveraging the United Nations to foster global stabilization, emphasizing collective security mechanisms to avert future conflicts akin to the failures of the League of Nations. The UN Charter, ratified by the U.S. Senate on July 28, 1945, enshrined principles of sovereign equality and non-aggression, with the U.S. as one of five permanent Security Council members wielding veto power to safeguard its interests. American leadership facilitated early UN efforts in humanitarian relief and reconstruction, including through the United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Administration (UNRRA), which distributed over $2.7 billion in aid by 1947, much of it funded by U.S. contributions exceeding 70% of the total. This support aimed at economic recovery in war-torn Europe and Asia, aligning with broader U.S. initiatives like the Marshall Plan, though the UN's framework provided multilateral legitimacy for stabilizing nascent international order against revanchist threats.

As the Cold War intensified, U.S. containment strategy—articulated in George F. Kennan's 1947 "Long Telegram" and formalized in the Truman Doctrine—intersected with UN mechanisms to counter Soviet expansionism, though institutional vetoes frequently stymied unified action. The Security Council's paralysis, marked by over 100 Soviet vetoes between 1946 and 1960, limited its efficacy, prompting the U.S. to bypass it for alliances like NATO while selectively utilizing UN resolutions where Soviet absences created opportunities. A key instance occurred during the Korean War, when North Korean forces invaded South Korea on June 25, 1950; the Soviet boycott of the Council enabled swift U.S.-backed resolutions. Security Council Resolution 82 condemned the aggression that day, Resolution 83 on June 27 authorized member states to repel it, and Resolution 84 on July 7 established a unified command under U.S. General Douglas MacArthur, with American forces comprising 90% of the 21-nation coalition that restored the pre-invasion boundary by 1953.

This episode exemplified how the UN served U.S. containment objectives by providing international sanction for military intervention, mobilizing 16 nations' contributions and framing the conflict as a defense of collective security rather than unilateral aggression. However, subsequent Cold War flashpoints, such as the 1956 Suez Crisis where the U.S. opposed Anglo-French-Israeli actions via UN pressure, highlighted divergences; Eisenhower's administration withheld support from allies to prioritize de-escalation, underscoring the UN's role as a diplomatic tool for American realpolitik amid superpower rivalry. Overall, while the UN enabled targeted stabilizations aligned with U.S. interests—evident in Korea's repulsion of communist incursion—the organization's utility waned as East-West divisions entrenched, with U.S. policymakers increasingly viewing it as an arena for ideological contestation rather than reliable enforcement.

### Humanitarian and Development Initiatives

The United States has been the largest single contributor to United Nations humanitarian efforts, providing approximately $18.1 billion to the UN system in 2022, including substantial allocations to agencies such as the World Food Programme (WFP), UNICEF, and the UN High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR). In 2024, the U.S. government reported over $9.8 billion in funding tracked through the UN's Financial Tracking Service for global humanitarian appeals, enabling responses to crises in regions like Ukraine, Yemen, and the Horn of Africa. This support has facilitated the delivery of emergency food aid, medical supplies, and shelter, with U.S.-funded WFP operations alone averting famine for millions in acute hunger hotspots as of 2023.

In development initiatives, the U.S. has sustained core funding to the UN Development Programme (UNDP) at $80 million annually since 2012, supporting poverty alleviation, infrastructure projects, and progress toward the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) in over 170 countries. Collaborative efforts have included U.S.-backed UNDP programs for economic recovery post-disaster, such as socioeconomic impact assessments in more than 120 nations following COVID-19, which integrated UN-wide recovery plans with local governance reforms. UNICEF, bolstered by U.S. contributions exceeding 20% of its voluntary funding in recent years, has advanced child health and education initiatives, including vaccination campaigns that contributed to global reductions in measles mortality by over 80% since 2000 through joint UN efforts.

U.S.-UN cooperation has yielded measurable outcomes in averting deaths from preventable causes, with analyses attributing millions of lives saved annually to foreign aid channeled through UN mechanisms, including malaria prevention (around 293,000 deaths averted yearly) and broader humanitarian interventions. However, U.S. funding often includes conditions for transparency and neutrality, as seen in repeated pauses to the UN Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees (UNRWA)—totaling hundreds of millions prior to 2018 suspensions—due to documented staff involvement in militant activities undermining aid impartiality. These measures reflect U.S. insistence on accountability, ensuring resources prioritize civilian welfare over politicized distributions.

## Persistent Tensions and Disputes

### Membership and Representation Conflicts

The United States has historically contested United Nations decisions on state representation that it viewed as undermining democratic governance or strategic interests, particularly during the Cold War era when challenges to Soviet bloc credentials were frequent. In the late 1940s and 1950s, the US and its allies repeatedly questioned the legitimacy of seating delegates from Eastern European states under Soviet influence, arguing that regimes installed without free elections did not represent their populations authentically; for instance, following the 1956 Hungarian Revolution, the US sought to deny credentials to Hungarian representatives, but General Assembly votes upheld seating them by majorities reflecting bloc alignments. These disputes highlighted procedural tensions, as the UN Charter's emphasis on sovereign equality clashed with US insistence on representational authenticity tied to electoral consent, though such challenges rarely succeeded due to numerical disadvantages in the Assembly.

A pivotal conflict arose over China's representation, culminating in United Nations General Assembly Resolution 2758 on October 25, 1971, which expelled the Republic of China (ROC, seated on Taiwan since 1945) and transferred the seat to the People's Republic of China (PRC). The US opposed the resolution, favoring a "dual representation" formula that would have retained the ROC while admitting the PRC, but it passed 76-35-17 amid shifting diplomatic winds, including US rapprochement with Beijing; this decision effectively barred Taiwan from UN membership or observer status thereafter. The US has maintained that Resolution 2758 addresses only the PRC's seating and does not determine Taiwan's political status or preclude its participation in UN-affiliated bodies, a position reiterated in State Department statements condemning PRC efforts to invoke the resolution for broader exclusion. Congressional resolutions, such as S. Res. 86 in 2025, have echoed this, asserting the resolution's limited scope and calling for Taiwan's inclusion in UN processes without implying sovereignty judgments.

In recent decades, US-UN friction has centered on Palestinian representation, where the US has blocked full membership bids to prioritize negotiated settlements with Israel. Palestine, granted non-member observer state status by General Assembly Resolution 67/19 on November 29, 2012, sought full admission in 2024; on April 18, the Security Council voted 12-1-2 to recommend membership, but the US vetoed it, with Ambassador Linda Thomas-Greenfield stating that statehood must emerge from direct negotiations addressing Israel's security concerns, not unilateral UN action amid ongoing violence. The veto drew criticism from 143 General Assembly states in a May 10, 2024, resolution urging reconsideration, yet it underscored US prioritization of bilateral peace processes over Assembly majorities, which often reflect non-aligned bloc pressures rather than consensus on state viability. These cases illustrate recurring US resistance to representational changes perceived as rewarding aggression or bypassing verification of state attributes like effective control and democratic legitimacy, contrasting with the UN's procedural formalism.

### Middle East Resolutions and US Vetoes

The United States has invoked its United Nations Security Council veto power more than any other permanent member to block draft resolutions addressing Middle East conflicts that it deems biased against Israel or insufficiently responsive to threats from non-state actors such as Palestinian militant groups. The initial such veto took place on September 10, 1972, when the US opposed a resolution demanding that Israel cease fire amid the aftermath of the Munich Olympics massacre perpetrated by Black September militants. By November 2024, the US had cast 49 vetoes specifically on Israel-related drafts, comprising over half of its total 89 vetoes since 1945, with further vetoes in 2025 concerning the Israel-Hamas war. These actions stem from US assessments that many proposals, often advanced by Arab and non-aligned states, prioritize condemnation of Israeli defensive measures while omitting references to incitement, rocket attacks, or suicide bombings by groups like Hamas and Palestinian Islamic Jihad.

In explanations of its votes, US representatives consistently argue that unbalanced resolutions undermine bilateral negotiations, incentivize violence against Israel, and contravene the Council's responsibility to address all parties' actions impartially. For example, during the 1980s, the US vetoed at least 15 resolutions criticizing Israel's 1982 Lebanon operation, which aimed to dismantle PLO infrastructure following attacks that killed over 30 Israelis, contending that the drafts ignored PLO terrorism and sought to delegitimize Israel's right to self-defense under Article 51 of the UN Charter. Similarly, in the post-Oslo era, vetoes targeted drafts on West Bank settlements that failed to acknowledge Palestinian rejectionism or the failure to dismantle terror networks as stipulated in agreements like the 1993 Oslo Accords and 1998 Wye River Memorandum. The US has emphasized that effective resolutions must condition Palestinian concessions on verifiable steps toward peace, such as ending incitement in official media and halting arms smuggling, rather than imposing unilateral obligations on Israel.

Recent vetoes, particularly amid the October 7, 2023, Hamas attacks that killed 1,200 Israelis and took over 250 hostages, illustrate this pattern's persistence. The US vetoed four Gaza-related drafts in 2024 alone, including one on December 8, 2023, that called for an immediate humanitarian ceasefire without requiring Hamas to release hostages or cease hostilities, arguing it would embolden militants and prolong the conflict. In 2025, the US cast its sixth veto over the Gaza war on September 18, blocking a non-permanent members' draft demanding an unconditional ceasefire, as it omitted demands for Hamas disarmament or the return of remaining hostages, potentially rewarding the group's October 7 atrocities. These decisions reflect a US policy prioritizing Israel's security as a democratic ally facing existential threats, while critiquing the Security Council's frequent paralysis on other global issues like Syrian chemical weapons use or Iranian nuclear proliferation.

### Financial Arrears as Leverage for Accountability

The United States has employed the strategic withholding of assessed contributions to the United Nations as a mechanism to demand greater fiscal accountability, operational reforms, and reductions in inefficiency within the organization. As the largest donor, funding roughly 22 percent of the UN regular budget and 25 to 28 percent of peacekeeping operations, the US holds significant leverage, since UN liquidity depends heavily on timely American payments. This approach intensified in the 1990s, when US arrears exceeded $1 billion amid congressional concerns over unchecked budget growth, duplicative programs, and lack of oversight; lawmakers conditioned clearance of these debts on verifiable UN changes, such as zero nominal budget growth and enhanced procurement transparency.

A pivotal example occurred through the Helms-Biden legislation enacted in 1998, which tied the payment of $819 million in back dues—part of a $926 million package approved by the Senate in June 1999—to specific benchmarks, including caps on administrative costs, staff reductions, and improved management practices. This pressure yielded tangible outcomes: the UN implemented a zero-growth budget policy for several years, streamlined operations by eliminating redundant positions, and adopted stricter financial controls, demonstrating how arrears can compel accountability in an institution prone to expansion without corresponding performance gains. Proponents of this tactic, including members of Congress, argue it counters systemic waste, such as overstaffing and inflated salaries exceeding comparable US government scales by 10 to 20 percent. Critics within diplomatic circles contend that prolonged arrears risk eroding US influence, as seen in delayed peacekeeping missions, but historical evidence indicates withholding has repeatedly forced concessions absent other incentives.

More recently, during the Trump administration from 2017 to 2021, the US enforced a unilateral 25 percent cap on peacekeeping assessments—below the UN's formula of up to 28.5 percent—resulting in arrears accumulating to approximately $1.22 billion by September 2018 and further buildup thereafter. This stance pressured the UN to adhere to the cap in practice and highlighted inefficiencies, such as mission overruns, while signaling intolerance for disproportionate burdens on American taxpayers. By 2025, US arrears had escalated to over $3 billion, encompassing $826 million for the current regular budget cycle plus prior obligations for peacekeeping and other programs, amid a broader UN liquidity shortfall exceeding $2.4 billion in outstanding assessments. This ongoing financial pressure continues to serve as leverage for advocating reforms, including salary adjustments and bias mitigation in bodies like the Human Rights Council, where US policymakers have linked funding to addressing perceived anti-Western and anti-Israel tilts. Despite UN assertions of crisis—such as near-bankruptcy risks without payments—the US strategy underscores a causal link between funding dependency and the need for empirical accountability, prioritizing verifiable efficiencies over unconditional support.

## Key Case Studies in Divergence

### The Iraq War and UN Inspections Regime

Following the 1991 Gulf War, United Nations Security Council Resolution 687 established the United Nations Special Commission (UNSCOM) to oversee the destruction and monitoring of Iraq's weapons of mass destruction (WMD) programs, including chemical, biological, and ballistic missile capabilities, in coordination with the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) for nuclear aspects. Iraq agreed to unrestricted inspections as part of the ceasefire, but faced repeated obstructions, such as denial of access to sites, concealment of documents, and interference with monitoring equipment. By 1998, UNSCOM had overseen the destruction of over 480,000 liters of chemical weapons agents, 48 operational missiles, and significant production facilities, yet key discrepancies persisted, including unaccounted biological weapons and VX nerve agent precursors. On December 16, 1998, Iraq expelled UNSCOM inspectors, prompting their withdrawal and subsequent U.S.-led airstrikes under Operation Desert Fox to degrade remaining capabilities.

No inspections occurred from 1999 to 2002 after UNSCOM's replacement by the United Nations Monitoring, Verification and Inspection Commission (UNMOVIC), amid ongoing sanctions and debates over Iraq's compliance. On November 8, 2002, the Security Council unanimously adopted Resolution 1441, declaring Iraq in "material breach" of prior disarmament obligations and offering a "final opportunity" to comply through enhanced inspections, with warnings of "serious consequences" for non-compliance. Inspections resumed on November 27, 2002, with UNMOVIC conducting over 550 visits to approximately 350 sites by early 2003.

UNMOVIC Executive Chairman Hans Blix reported to the Security Council that while Iraq provided increased access and destroyed some proscribed items, such as Al-Samoud missiles on February 1, 2003, cooperation remained incomplete: unresolved questions lingered over anthrax and botulinum toxin stocks, undeclared missile engines, and cluster bomb remnants, with no evidence of ongoing WMD production found but also no full accounting for past programs. Blix noted in his January 27, 2003, briefing that Iraq's efforts accelerated post-resolution but urged more time for verification, contrasting U.S. intelligence assessments claiming active concealment and reconstitution of WMD capabilities.

### Challenges to UN Leadership Selection

The selection of the United Nations Secretary-General, recommended by the Security Council and approved by the General Assembly, has frequently encountered challenges from the United States due to its veto authority as a permanent member. This power enables the US to block candidates perceived as misaligned with its priorities, such as institutional reform or fiscal accountability. In 1996, the US exercised its veto against incumbent Boutros Boutros-Ghali's bid for a second term, marking the first such instance in UN history despite 14 Security Council members supporting him; the decision stemmed from US frustrations over Boutros-Ghali's handling of peacekeeping operations in Somalia and Bosnia, slow progress on administrative reforms, and disputes involving over $1 billion in US arrears to the UN. This action paved the way for Kofi Annan's appointment, highlighting how US opposition can override broad consensus and reshape leadership outcomes.

Such interventions underscore broader US critiques of the opaque, backroom nature of the selection process, which historically relies on informal consultations among the P5 (permanent five Security Council members) rather than open competition. The US has argued that this system favors political deal-making over merit, potentially sidelining qualified candidates from underrepresented regions or those committed to core UN functions like conflict prevention and financial efficiency. In the lead-up to the 2026 selection following António Guterres' departure, the US in October 2025 called for a "purely merit-based" process with candidates drawn from a global pool, explicitly rejecting reliance on regional rotation conventions that would prioritize Latin America. This stance challenges entrenched diplomatic norms, as evidenced by US advocacy for enhanced transparency, including public hearings and structured evaluations, to ensure the next leader prioritizes Charter-mandated duties over expansive agendas like climate initiatives outside traditional peacekeeping.

These positions reflect ongoing US efforts to leverage its influence for accountability, amid criticisms that prior selections have elevated figures tolerant of bureaucratic inefficiencies or ideologically divergent from Western interests in security and governance. For instance, US officials have emphasized selecting a Secretary-General who would "reject initiatives that fall outside the Charter's founding purpose," signaling wariness of candidates advancing non-core priorities. While this approach has drawn accusations of unilateralism from other members, it aligns with historical patterns where US veto threats or endorsements—such as supporting Javier Pérez de Cuéllar in 1981—have steered outcomes toward perceived reformers. The 1996 veto, in particular, intensified debates over P5 dominance, prompting later General Assembly resolutions in 2015 for greater inclusivity, though implementation remains limited by Security Council gatekeeping.
