# Taxation in the United States

## Introduction

Taxation in the United States consists of compulsory levies imposed by federal, state, and local governments on income, property, consumption, and other economic activities to finance public goods and services such as defense, infrastructure, education, and social welfare programs. The modern federal income tax system originated with the ratification of the Sixteenth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution on February 3, 1913, which granted Congress the power to impose taxes on incomes without apportionment among the states or regard to census population. Prior to this, a federal income tax enacted in 1894 had been ruled unconstitutional by the Supreme Court in 1895 as a direct tax not apportioned by state population.

The federal tax system includes individual income taxes, corporate income taxes, payroll taxes for social insurance programs like Social Security and Medicare, estate and gift taxes, and excise taxes on specific goods and activities. In fiscal year 2024, federal revenues totaled approximately $5 trillion (based on projections and partial actuals), with individual income taxes comprising approximately half of that amount, underscoring their dominant role in funding the national government. Payroll taxes contributed another significant portion, primarily dedicated to entitlement programs, while corporate income taxes and excises made up smaller shares.

State and local governments impose their own taxes, including income taxes in 41 states, sales taxes, and property taxes, which together generate approximately $3 trillion in revenues based on the latest comprehensive data (2022-2023). These subnational taxes often complement federal levies, with variations in rates and bases reflecting differences in fiscal policies and economic conditions across jurisdictions. Combined federal, state, and local tax revenues for 2024 are estimated at around $8 trillion, though comprehensive official totals are not yet fully available due to data lags in state and local reporting. The overall U.S. tax burden, measured as total tax revenues relative to gross domestic product, has hovered around 25-30% in recent decades, supporting a mixed economy where government spending influences resource allocation alongside private markets. Debates over tax policy frequently center on progressivity, with the federal individual income tax featuring graduated rates from 10% to 37% for 2024, though effective rates incorporate deductions, credits, and alternative minimum calculations that affect distributional outcomes.

## Overview and Fundamental Principles

### Structure and Levels of Taxation

The United States maintains a decentralized taxation framework where federal, state, and local governments independently levy taxes to fund their operations, resulting in overlapping but distinct tax burdens on individuals and entities. This structure stems from the U.S. Constitution's allocation of taxing powers, with the federal government deriving authority primarily from Article I, Section 8, while states retain broad residual powers under the Tenth Amendment, and localities operate under state charters. In fiscal year 2023, total U.S. tax revenues across all levels approximated 25% of gross domestic product, with federal collections dominating at around 16.5% of GDP, supplemented by state and local taxes contributing the remainder.

At the federal level, the Internal Revenue Service administers the primary taxes, which include individual income taxes, corporate income taxes, payroll taxes funding Social Security and Medicare, excise taxes on specific goods and services, customs duties on imports, and estate and gift taxes. Individual income taxes constituted 49% of federal revenues in FY2023, totaling approximately $2.2 trillion, while payroll taxes accounted for about 36%, corporate income taxes 9% or $420 billion, and other sources the balance. These federal taxes apply nationwide with uniform rates and bases, though states may conform to certain federal definitions for their own purposes.

State governments, numbering 50 plus the District of Columbia, impose a variety of taxes tailored to fiscal needs, with 41 states plus DC levying individual income taxes, 45 imposing general sales taxes, and nearly all relying on selective sales or excise taxes. State tax revenues in recent quarters have hovered around $2.5 trillion annually, predominantly from sales taxes (about 33%), individual income taxes (35-40%), and property taxes where applicable, though the latter are often locally administered. States exhibit significant variation; for instance, high-income-tax states like California feature top marginal rates exceeding 13%, while no-sales-tax states like New Hampshire fund via property and income levies.

### Economic Foundations of Taxation

The economic foundations of taxation in the United States rest on the need to generate revenue for public goods, income redistribution, and macroeconomic stabilization while accounting for inevitable distortions to private incentives and resource allocation. Taxes enable funding for defense, infrastructure, and social programs that markets underprovide due to non-excludability and positive externalities, as articulated in public finance theory. However, taxation alters relative prices, leading to deadweight losses—estimated at 20-30 cents per dollar of revenue for labor income taxes based on empirical elasticities of taxable income—which reduce overall economic efficiency by discouraging labor supply, capital formation, and entrepreneurship.

A core principle is neutrality, whereby taxes should minimize interference with private decisions unless correcting market failures, as non-neutral provisions like targeted credits distort allocation toward politically favored sectors. In the US, deviations from neutrality—such as deductions for specific industries—have been shown to lower GDP growth by favoring inefficient capital use, with studies estimating that eliminating such distortions could boost long-term output by 5-10%. Simplicity and transparency further underpin efficiency, reducing compliance costs (averaging $200-500 billion annually in the US) and evasion, which empirical data link to complex codes fostering uncertainty and litigation.

Equity in taxation balances horizontal equity (equal treatment of equals) and vertical equity (progressive burdens on greater ability to pay), rooted in the ability-to-pay doctrine that dominates US policy since the 16th Amendment's ratification in 1913. Yet, this conflicts with efficiency: progressive rates, reaching 37% federally plus state averages over 5% in 2025, compress incentives at high incomes, with elasticities of 0.2-0.6 implying reduced labor participation and investment; cross-state evidence shows high-tax jurisdictions experience slower income growth. The benefits principle—taxing in proportion to received government services—offers an alternative but proves impractical for non-rival goods like national defense, leading to reliance on general revenue sources. High-income taxpayers, like others, receive indirect benefits from tax-funded public services such as infrastructure and law enforcement, as well as direct benefits from contributions to Social Security and Medicare programs upon reaching eligibility.

### Taxpayer Classifications

In U.S. federal income taxation, taxpayers are primarily classified as individuals or entities, with further subdivisions determining filing requirements, tax rates, and treatment of income. Individuals file using one of five filing statuses, which affect standard deductions, tax brackets, and eligibility for credits: single, married filing jointly, married filing separately, head of household, and qualifying surviving spouse. Single status applies to unmarried individuals without qualifying dependents as of the last day of the tax year, while married filing jointly allows spouses to combine income and deductions for generally lower overall tax liability due to wider brackets. Head of household status is available to unmarried individuals providing more than half the cost of maintaining a home for a qualifying dependent, offering higher standard deductions and lower rates than single filers to reflect additional household support burdens. Qualifying surviving spouse status permits widows or widowers with a dependent child to use joint filing benefits for two years following a spouse's death, easing transitional tax impacts.

Business entities are classified under Treasury regulations for federal tax purposes, distinguishing between per se corporations (always taxed as separate entities, such as public corporations) and eligible entities that can elect treatment as corporations, partnerships, or disregarded entities. Sole proprietorships, typically single-owner operations without formal entity formation, are disregarded entities where business income passes through to the owner's individual return via Schedule C, subjecting it to self-employment taxes on net earnings. Partnerships, including multi-member LLCs electing partnership status via Form 8832, are pass-through entities reporting income on Form 1065, with distributive shares allocated to partners' personal returns, avoiding entity-level taxation but requiring basis tracking for loss limitations.

C corporations are taxed as separate entities on worldwide income at a flat 21% rate under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017, with dividends to shareholders subject to additional individual-level tax, resulting in double taxation unless mitigated by deductions like the Section 250 deduction for foreign-derived income. S corporations, elected via Form 2553 by eligible domestic corporations with up to 100 shareholders (all U.S. residents or certain trusts), achieve pass-through treatment similar to partnerships, filing informational Form 1120-S while avoiding entity tax to prevent double taxation. Limited liability companies (LLCs) default to partnership classification for multi-member setups or disregarded for single-member, but can elect corporate status, providing flexibility to optimize tax outcomes based on ownership and income character.

## Federal Income Taxation

### Historical Development

The first federal income tax in the United States was enacted through the Revenue Act of 1861, imposing a 3 percent tax on incomes exceeding $800 to finance the Civil War effort. This tax was repealed in 1872 after the war concluded. An attempt to reinstate a permanent income tax occurred in 1894 via the Wilson-Gorman Tariff Act, which levied a 2 percent tax on incomes over $4,000, but the Supreme Court struck it down in Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co. (1895), ruling that taxes on income from property were direct taxes requiring apportionment among states.

To overcome this constitutional barrier, Congress proposed the Sixteenth Amendment in 1909, which was ratified on February 3, 1913, granting authority to levy taxes on incomes "from whatever source derived" without apportionment or regard to census. The Revenue Act of 1913 promptly implemented this power, establishing a 1 percent normal tax on net income above $3,000 for individuals ($4,000 for married couples) and a progressive surtax reaching 6 percent on incomes over $500,000.

World War I prompted sharp rate increases; by 1918, the top marginal rate had risen to 77 percent on incomes over $1 million. Rates were reduced in the 1920s, but the Great Depression and New Deal policies elevated them again, with the top rate at 63 percent by 1932. During World War II, rates escalated further, peaking at 94 percent on incomes over $200,000 in 1944, and remained high postwar at 91-92 percent through the 1950s and early 1960s.

### Core Mechanics and Rate Structure

The U.S. federal individual income tax employs a progressive marginal rate system, under which different portions of a taxpayer's taxable income are taxed at increasing rates as income rises, rather than applying a single rate to total income. This structure ensures that only the income within each bracket incurs the corresponding rate, with lower brackets fully taxed before higher ones apply. Taxable income, calculated after adjustments, deductions, and exemptions, serves as the base for this computation.

For tax year 2025, the seven statutory marginal rates remain 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35%, and 37%, as established by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 and unchanged through its scheduled expiration at the end of 2025. These rates apply to brackets adjusted annually for inflation using the chained Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (C-CPI-U), which mitigates bracket creep where nominal income growth due to inflation pushes taxpayers into higher rates without real income gains. The adjustment for 2025 increased brackets by approximately 2.8% from 2024 levels. Filing status—single, married filing jointly, married filing separately, or head of household—determines the specific bracket thresholds, with joint filers generally receiving wider brackets to account for dual earners.

### Taxable Income Determination

Taxable income represents the base amount upon which federal income tax rates are applied for individuals and entities filing under the Internal Revenue Code. It is derived by subtracting allowable adjustments, deductions, and certain credits from gross income, with exclusions applied where statutorily provided. The process ensures that only income subject to taxation after specific subtractions contributes to the tax liability calculation.

The foundational step involves determining gross income, defined in Internal Revenue Code Section 61 as "all income from whatever source derived," encompassing compensation for services, business profits, gains from property dealings, interest, rents, dividends, and other accessions to wealth unless explicitly exempted. Exclusions from gross income, such as certain life insurance proceeds, qualified scholarships, and municipal bond interest, are narrowly prescribed by law to prevent double taxation or encourage specific behaviors, with the Supreme Court affirming in Commissioner v. Glenshaw Glass Co. (1955) that gross income includes all undeniable accessions to wealth, clearly realized, over which taxpayers have complete dominion. Non-taxable items like gifts, inheritances, and welfare benefits do not enter the gross income tally.

From gross income, specific adjustments—often termed "above-the-line" deductions—are subtracted to arrive at adjusted gross income (AGI). These adjustments, detailed on Schedule 1 of Form 1040, include contributions to traditional IRAs, student loan interest (up to $2,500 annually, phased out at higher incomes), health savings account contributions, self-employment tax deductions, and educator expenses up to $300 per year for eligible teachers. AGI serves as a key threshold for eligibility in numerous tax benefits, phase-outs, and alternative minimum tax computations, reflecting a policy of standardizing income measurement before broader deductions.

### Deductions, Exemptions, and Credits

Deductions reduce a taxpayer's adjusted gross income (AGI) or taxable income, thereby lowering the amount subject to federal income tax rates, while tax credits directly reduce the tax liability on a dollar-for-dollar basis and may be refundable if they exceed the liability. Above-the-line deductions, also known as adjustments to income, are subtracted from gross income to arrive at AGI and are available regardless of whether a taxpayer itemizes or takes the standard deduction; common examples include deductible contributions to traditional IRAs, student loan interest up to $2,500 annually (phased out at higher incomes), health insurance premiums for self-employed individuals, and educator expenses up to $300 for eligible teachers.

Taxpayers may choose between the standard deduction or itemizing deductions on Schedule A to further reduce taxable income after AGI. For tax year 2025, the standard deduction is $15,750 for single filers and married filing separately, $31,500 for married filing jointly and qualifying surviving spouses, and $23,625 for heads of household. Additional amounts apply for age 65+ or blind. Itemized deductions include qualified medical expenses exceeding 7.5% of AGI, state and local taxes up to a $10,000 cap (or $5,000 for married filing separately), mortgage interest on up to $750,000 of acquisition debt for homes purchased after December 15, 2017, and charitable contributions up to 60% of AGI for cash gifts to public charities. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 (TCJA) roughly doubled the standard deduction while limiting certain itemized deductions, such as eliminating miscellaneous deductions subject to the 2% AGI floor, which reduced the incentive to itemize for many taxpayers.

Personal exemptions, which previously allowed $4,050 per taxpayer, spouse, and dependent in 2017 to reduce taxable income, were suspended under the TCJA for tax years 2018 through 2025, remaining at $0 for 2025; this provision is set to expire after 2025 unless extended by Congress, potentially reinstating exemptions adjusted for inflation.

### Business and Corporate Taxation

C corporations, the standard form of incorporated business entities under Subchapter C of the Internal Revenue Code, are subject to federal income tax at a flat rate of 21 percent on their taxable income, a rate established by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) of 2017, which reduced the previous top statutory rate of 35 percent. This taxation applies to worldwide income for domestic corporations, though the TCJA shifted the U.S. toward a territorial system by exempting most foreign dividends from taxation while introducing anti-deferral regimes such as Global Intangible Low-Taxed Income (GILTI), taxed at an effective rate of 10.5 percent after a 50 percent deduction, and Base Erosion and Anti-Abuse Tax (BEAT) at 10 percent (rising to 12.5 percent for large corporations). C corporation income faces double taxation: first at the entity level and then on distributions to shareholders as dividends, taxed at qualified dividend rates of 0 to 20 percent plus a potential 3.8 percent net investment income tax.

Taxable income for C corporations is computed similarly to individuals but includes specific business deductions, such as accelerated depreciation under Section 168(k) for qualified property—allowing 100 percent bonus depreciation for assets placed in service through 2022, phasing down to 20 percent by 2026—and interest expense limitations under Section 163(j), capping deductions at 30 percent of adjusted taxable income. Research and development (R&D) expenditures, previously expensed, must now be amortized over five years for domestic activities under post-TCJA rules enacted in 2022. Effective tax rates for profitable corporations averaged around 14 percent in the years following the TCJA, lower than the statutory rate due to credits (e.g., for renewable energy or low-income housing) and deductions, though rates vary widely by industry and profitability; for instance, a Government Accountability Office analysis found average effective rates dropped from about 23 percent pre-2018 to 15 percent in 2018 for large corporations. State corporate income taxes, averaging 4-6 percent, add to the combined rate, typically resulting in a U.S. average statutory rate of 25-27 percent.

In contrast, pass-through entities—such as partnerships, S corporations, and sole proprietorships—comprise over 95 percent of U.S. businesses by number and generate about half of private-sector output, with income reported directly on owners' personal returns and taxed at individual marginal rates up to 37 percent, avoiding entity-level taxation. The TCJA introduced the Section 199A Qualified Business Income (QBI) deduction, allowing eligible owners to deduct up to 20 percent of QBI from domestic trades or businesses, effectively reducing the top rate on such income to 29.6 percent, subject to phase-outs for high earners (above $182,100 single/$364,200 joint in 2023, inflation-adjusted) involving wage and capital limitations: the deduction is limited to the greater of 50 percent of W-2 wages or 25 percent of wages plus 2.5 percent of asset basis. Specified service trades (e.g., law, medicine) face full phase-out above income thresholds, reflecting congressional intent to favor capital-intensive over service-oriented pass-throughs. The QBI deduction applies through tax year 2025 unless extended.

### Alternative Minimum Tax and Adjustments

The Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT) operates as a parallel federal income tax system designed to ensure that higher-income individuals pay a minimum level of tax despite deductions, exclusions, and credits available under the regular income tax regime. Enacted in 1969 following revelations that 155 high-income households paid no federal income tax in 1967 due to preferential treatments, the AMT imposes a tentative minimum tax on alternative minimum taxable income (AMTI), which taxpayers compare against their regular tax liability; the AMT equals the excess, if any. The modern individual AMT structure emerged from reforms in 1978 and was significantly broadened in 1986 to recapture benefits from certain exclusions and preferences, though it applied primarily to individuals as the corporate AMT was repealed by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) of 2017 for tax years beginning after December 31, 2017.

To compute AMTI, taxpayers begin with regular taxable income and apply adjustments and tax preference items that reverse or modify certain regular tax benefits, reflecting a broader base less favorable to specific incentives. Adjustments typically arise from timing differences or alternative treatments, such as accelerated depreciation methods under the regular tax (e.g., differences between MACRS and AMT straight-line depreciation for property placed in service after 1986), the exercise of incentive stock options (adding the bargain element to income), and deductions for state and local taxes in excess of AMT limits post-TCJA. Tax preference items, by contrast, include income exclusions under the regular tax that are added back for AMT purposes, notably interest on private activity bonds (beyond a de minimis amount for governmental bonds) and excess depletion allowances for natural resources exceeding 50% of net income from the property. Other adjustments encompass items like net operating losses (limited to 90% of AMTI for AMT), health savings account contributions treated differently, and certain business expense deductions (e.g., for research or mining exploration) that receive preferential amortization under regular rules.

From AMTI, taxpayers subtract an exemption amount—$88,100 for single filers and $137,000 for married filing jointly in tax year 2025, subject to inflation adjustments—and apply AMT rates of 26% on the first $232,600 of taxable AMTI (after exemption) for singles or $1,218,700 for joint filers, with 28% on excess amounts; these phaseout thresholds and rates were enhanced by the TCJA to limit AMT exposure primarily to the top 0.1% of earners. The resulting tentative minimum tax is reduced by allowable nonrefundable personal credits (with limitations) and compared to regular tax; corporations no longer face AMT post-2017 repeal, though a new 15% corporate alternative minimum tax on adjusted financial statement income applies to large entities with average annual book income exceeding $1 billion from 2023 onward under the Inflation Reduction Act. These provisions, while curbing AMT's reach—projected to affect fewer than 200,000 returns in 2025 versus millions pre-TCJA—persist as temporary measures expiring after 2025 absent extension, potentially reverting exemptions to pre-TCJA levels and broadening incidence if not addressed.

### Withholding, Filing, and Payment Processes

Employers are required to withhold federal income tax from employees' wages under Section 3402 of the Internal Revenue Code, calculating the amount based on the employee's completed Form W-4, Employee's Withholding Certificate, which accounts for filing status, multiple jobs, dependents, and other adjustments. Employees can use the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator tool to determine appropriate withholding levels and update Form W-4 accordingly, ensuring over- or under-withholding is minimized to avoid penalties or large balances due. Withheld amounts are remitted by employers to the IRS via periodic deposits, typically monthly or semi-weekly depending on payroll size, and reported annually to employees on Form W-2, Wage and Tax Statement. Backup withholding at a 24% rate applies to certain payments, such as interest or dividends, if the recipient fails to provide a valid taxpayer identification number.

Taxpayers must file an annual federal income tax return on Form 1040 or its variants if their gross income exceeds filing thresholds, which adjust annually for inflation; for tax year 2023, these included $13,850 for single filers under age 65 and $20,800 for heads of household under 65. The standard filing deadline for calendar-year taxpayers is April 15 of the following year, such as April 15, 2025, for tax year 2024 returns, though fiscal-year filers submit by the 15th day of the fourth month after their year-end. Extensions to file, via Form 4868, extend the deadline to October 15 but do not delay payment of any tax owed, which remains due by the original date to avoid failure-to-pay penalties. Electronic filing through IRS-approved software or Free File is encouraged for faster processing and refunds, with paper returns mailed to designated IRS centers based on state of residence.

Payments of federal income tax liability are handled through withholding for wage earners, with any underpayment settled via the tax return or quarterly estimated payments for those with significant non-wage income, such as self-employed individuals. Estimated taxes, reported on Form 1040-ES, are due in four installments: April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15 of the following year, required if a taxpayer anticipates owing at least $1,000 after withholding and credits. Options for remitting payments include IRS Direct Pay from a bank account at no cost, Electronic Federal Tax Payment System (EFTPS) for scheduled transfers, electronic funds withdrawal during e-filing, credit/debit card via authorized processors (with fees), or check/money order by mail. Failure to pay on time incurs underpayment penalties calculated quarterly based on the federal short-term rate plus 3%, unless safe harbor rules are met by paying 90% of current-year tax or 100% of prior-year tax (110% for high-income filers).

## Other Federal Taxes

### Payroll and Employment Taxes

Payroll and employment taxes in the United States primarily consist of taxes imposed under the Federal Insurance Contributions Act (FICA) and the Federal Unemployment Tax Act (FUTA). FICA taxes fund Social Security and Medicare programs, while FUTA supports federal unemployment benefits administration. These taxes are distinct from federal income taxes, as they are levied specifically on wages and self-employment income to finance social insurance programs.

FICA taxes are split equally between employers and employees, with each paying 6.2% for Social Security (Old-Age, Survivors, and Disability Insurance, or OASDI) on wages up to the annual wage base limit of $176,100 for 2025, resulting in a combined rate of 12.4%. Medicare taxes apply at 1.45% each for employers and employees on all wages, with no wage base limit, for a total of 2.9%; high-income employees face an additional Medicare tax of 0.9% on wages exceeding $200,000 (single filers) or $250,000 (married filing jointly), paid solely by the employee. For 2025, the total FICA rate for most employees is thus 7.65% withheld from wages, matched by employers.

Self-employed individuals pay self-employment taxes under the Self-Employment Contributions Act (SECA), which mirrors FICA but requires payment of the full 15.3% rate (12.4% Social Security up to the wage base plus 2.9% Medicare on all net earnings), though half is deductible as an adjustment to income. The Social Security portion applies to net earnings from self-employment of $400 or more, with the same wage base limit. This structure ensures equivalent contributions to social insurance programs as wage earners, adjusted for the absence of an employer share.

### Excise and Consumption-Based Taxes

Federal excise taxes constitute indirect levies on the production, importation, distribution, sale, or use of specific goods, services, and activities, functioning as targeted consumption-based taxes. These differ from state-level general sales taxes by applying narrowly to items such as fuels, alcoholic beverages, tobacco products, and air transportation, often with revenues earmarked for related infrastructure or trust funds like the Highway Trust Fund and Airport and Airway Trust Fund. Administered by the Internal Revenue Service, excise taxes are typically reported and paid quarterly via Form 720, with liability falling on manufacturers, importers, wholesalers, or end users depending on the commodity.

In fiscal year 2023, federal excise tax collections reached $76 billion, representing about 1.7% of total federal revenues of $4.4 trillion. This marks a continued decline from historical highs, where excises comprised over 90% of federal receipts in 1913 before the introduction of the income tax, now stabilizing at roughly 0.3% of GDP amid shifts to income and payroll taxation. Tobacco excises alone generated $11.3 billion in 2022, underscoring their role in both revenue and behavioral influence, though overall excise yields remain modest due to narrow bases and elastic demand responses.

Key federal excise taxes include those on transportation fuels, which fund highway maintenance: gasoline at $0.184 per gallon and diesel at $0.244 per gallon, with additional fees for leaking underground storage tanks. Alcohol taxes, imposed per proof gallon, levy $13.50 on distilled spirits, $1.07 to $3.40 on beer barrels depending on alcohol content, and varying rates on wine. Tobacco products face $50.33 per thousand small cigarettes (approximately $1.01 per pack) and higher for large cigars or smokeless tobacco. Air travel taxes, updated annually for inflation, impose $5.20 per domestic flight segment and $22.90 for certain international departures effective January 1, 2025. Other notable excises cover medical devices at 2.3% of the sale price, indoor tanning services at 10%, and reinstated Superfund taxes on petroleum and chemicals since 2022 under the Inflation Reduction Act.

### Customs Duties and Tariffs

Customs duties and tariffs in the United States consist of taxes imposed on imported merchandise to regulate international trade, protect domestic industries, and generate federal revenue. Administered primarily by U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP), these duties are collected at the point of entry for goods crossing U.S. borders. The system operates under statutory authority from laws such as the Tariff Act of 1930 and subsequent trade acts, with the president holding delegated powers to adjust rates for national security or trade reciprocity purposes under sections like 232 and 301 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962.

The Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States (HTS), maintained by the U.S. International Trade Commission, provides the classification framework and applicable duty rates for over 10,000 categories of imported goods. Goods are classified using a 10-digit HTS code based on material composition, use, and name, enabling ad valorem duties (a percentage of the customs value, typically 0-20% under most-favored-nation rates) or specific duties (a fixed amount per unit, such as per kilogram). Preferential rates apply under free trade agreements like USMCA, reducing or eliminating duties for qualifying imports from partner countries. CBP determines the dutiable value using transaction value (invoice price plus adjustments for freight and insurance) or alternative methods if unavailable, ensuring duties reflect economic reality rather than manipulated valuations.

Recent policy shifts have elevated average tariff rates from under 2.5% at the start of 2025 to over 18% by October, driven by reciprocal tariff measures implemented in April 2025. A baseline 10% ad valorem duty on most imports took effect April 5, 2025, with higher country-specific rates (e.g., up to 125% initially on certain partners, later adjusted) applied under executive authority to address trade imbalances and unfair practices. These changes override some existing trade agreements for non-reciprocal partners but maintain exemptions for compliant allies. Additional sector-specific tariffs, such as on steel and aluminum under Section 232, persist from prior administrations, with rates of 25% and 10%, respectively.

### Estate, Gift, and Transfer Taxes

The federal estate tax is levied on the fair market value of a decedent's gross estate, minus allowable deductions, for estates exceeding the applicable exclusion amount. For decedents dying in 2025, the basic exclusion amount stands at $13.99 million per individual, adjusted annually for inflation under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017, which temporarily doubled prior exemption levels through that year. The tax rate is graduated, reaching a maximum of 40% on taxable amounts above the exemption threshold. Estates must file Form 706 if the gross estate plus adjusted taxable gifts exceeds the exclusion amount, with valuation typically based on the date of death or an alternate valuation date six months later if elected. Deductions include an unlimited marital deduction for transfers to a surviving U.S. citizen spouse and a charitable deduction for qualified bequests, while credits such as the unified credit offset tax liability up to the exclusion amount.

The federal gift tax applies to inter vivos transfers of property by gift, ensuring that lifetime gifts are integrated with estate taxation to prevent avoidance of the estate tax. Donors may exclude up to $19,000 per donee annually in 2025 without incurring tax or reducing the lifetime exemption, with married couples able to split gifts to double this to $38,000 per recipient. Gifts exceeding this annual exclusion count against the donor's unified lifetime exemption of $13.99 million, the same as for estates, with rates mirroring the estate tax up to 40%. Certain transfers, such as direct payments for medical expenses or tuition, qualify for unlimited exclusions regardless of amount. Form 709 must be filed for taxable gifts, and the tax is computed on cumulative lifetime gifts above the annual exclusion, net of prior taxes paid.

The generation-skipping transfer tax (GSTT) targets transfers to beneficiaries two or more generations below the transferor, such as grandchildren, to curb dynasty trusts that bypass intermediate estate or gift taxes. It imposes a flat 40% rate on the value of such transfers exceeding the GST exemption, which aligns with the estate and gift tax exemption at $13.99 million for 2025. Direct skips, taxable terminations (e.g., upon a child's death), and taxable distributions trigger the tax, with the annual gift exclusion of $19,000 also applying to GSTT-eligible gifts. The GST exemption is allocated by the transferor via Form 709 or automatically in some cases, and unused portions do not carry over post-2025 without legislative extension.

## State and Local Taxation

### State Income and Payroll Taxes

Forty-one states and the District of Columbia impose a broad-based individual income tax as of 2025, while nine states—Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming—do not levy such a tax on earned income. Among taxing jurisdictions, structures vary: 27 states and the District of Columbia use progressive graduated-rate systems with multiple brackets, where rates increase with income levels, while 14 states apply a flat rate to all taxable income. Top marginal rates differ significantly, reaching 13.3 percent in California (applicable to income over $1 million for single filers), 11 percent in Hawaii, and 10.9 percent in New York, compared to flat rates as low as 3 percent in Indiana (effective 2025). States without income taxes often compensate through higher reliance on sales, property, or other levies, though empirical analyses indicate no-income-tax states generally exhibit lower overall tax burdens.

State income taxes typically apply to adjusted gross income with modifications for state-specific deductions, exemptions, and credits, mirroring federal definitions but excluding certain items like state bonds or retirement income in some cases. Rates and brackets adjust annually for inflation in many states, and several have enacted reductions or reforms; for instance, between 2021 and 2025, eight states transitioned to flat taxes while providing relief. Corporate income taxes exist in 44 states, generally mirroring individual structures but at lower rates, averaging around 6 percent nominally, though effective rates decline with deductions.

Beyond income taxes, states impose payroll taxes primarily to fund unemployment insurance (UI) programs, required under federal law with states setting employer contribution rates based on experience rating systems. These taxes apply to the first $7,000 to $50,800 of wages per employee annually, varying by state wage base, with new employer rates typically ranging from 0.5 to 3.4 percent; experienced-rated employers may pay up to 6-8 percent in high-unemployment states like California or New Jersey. Five states—California, Hawaii, New Jersey, New York, and Rhode Island—also levy employee-paid disability insurance taxes, often at 0.5-1 percent of wages, to cover short-term disability benefits, while employers fund UI separately. Six states plus the District of Columbia impose additional non-UI payroll taxes, such as Alaska's health care funds or Pennsylvania's capital stock tax equivalents, though these remain minor relative to UI contributions. Workers' compensation premiums, while not taxes, function similarly as mandatory employer assessments varying by industry risk and state regulations.

### Sales, Use, and Excise Taxes

Sales taxes in the United States are imposed by 45 states and the District of Columbia, as well as numerous localities, on the retail purchase of most tangible goods and select services, functioning as a consumption tax collected at the point of sale. Alaska permits only local sales taxes without a statewide rate, while Delaware, Montana, New Hampshire, and Oregon impose no state or local general sales taxes. State-level rates as of mid-2025 range from 2.9 percent in Colorado to 7.25 percent in California, with five states at 7 percent including Indiana, Mississippi, Rhode Island, and Tennessee; combined state-local rates often exceed 9 percent in high-tax areas due to additional municipal or county levies. Sales tax bases typically exclude necessities like unprepared groceries and prescription drugs in most states, though taxable services such as repairs or lodging vary by jurisdiction, leading to effective rates influenced by exemptions and administrative thresholds for small vendors.

Use taxes complement sales taxes by applying the same rates to taxable items purchased from out-of-state sellers or for which no sales tax was charged, such as certain online or catalog purchases, with the intent of ensuring equivalent taxation regardless of vendor location. All sales-taxing states impose use taxes, but compliance relies on voluntary self-reporting by consumers or businesses via state returns, resulting in low collection rates historically estimated below 10 percent of potential liability due to underreporting. Enforcement has intensified since the 2018 Supreme Court decision in South Dakota v. Wayfair, which expanded economic nexus for remote sellers to collect sales taxes directly, reducing some use tax burdens but prompting increased state audits on non-compliant entities, including businesses accruing use tax on interstate acquisitions.

Excise taxes, distinct from general sales taxes, are selective levies on specific goods, services, or activities, often per unit or ad valorem, administered at both federal and state levels to target externalities like health costs or infrastructure use. Federally, major excise taxes include those on motor fuels (18.4 cents per gallon on gasoline as of 2025), alcohol (e.g., $13.50 per proof gallon on distilled spirits), tobacco ($1.01 per pack of cigarettes), and aviation, with total collections supporting highway trusts and general revenue; in fiscal year 2024, excise taxes contributed modestly to federal receipts amid broader reliance on income taxes. States impose parallel excises on alcohol, tobacco, and fuels, often varying widely—e.g., gasoline excises from 15 cents per gallon in Alaska to 62.4 cents in California—collectively accounting for about 7 percent of state tax revenue as of recent data, funding transportation and health programs while facing erosion from inflation and evasion.

### Property and Real Estate Taxes

Property taxes in the United States are ad valorem levies imposed primarily by local governments on the assessed value of real property, including land and improvements such as buildings. These taxes fund essential local services, including public education, police and fire protection, roads, and libraries, comprising the single largest source of revenue for state and local governments. In fiscal year 2022, property taxes accounted for 27.4 percent of total state and local tax collections.

The tax base typically encompasses all real estate within a jurisdiction, with assessments conducted by local assessors to determine fair market value or a statutory fraction thereof, often updated periodically through mass appraisals or individual inspections. The effective tax rate—total property tax payments divided by total property value—varies significantly across states; in calendar year 2023, New Jersey recorded the highest at 2.23 percent on owner-occupied housing, followed by Illinois at 2.07 percent and Connecticut at 1.92 percent, while Hawaii had the lowest at 0.29 percent. The national average effective rate stood at approximately 1.1 percent in 2024, yielding an average annual payment of about $3,910 per household.

Local taxing authorities, such as counties, municipalities, school districts, and special districts, set millage rates (tax rates per $1,000 of assessed value) through voter-approved budgets or legislative processes, with total rates often aggregating multiple overlapping jurisdictions. In 2021, local governments collected $609 billion from property taxes, representing 30 percent of their general revenue. Property owners may appeal assessments if they believe valuations exceed market conditions, a process governed by state laws that typically allow administrative reviews followed by judicial recourse.

### Local Levies, Fees, and Licenses

Local governments in the United States impose special assessments, also known as local levies, to fund targeted public infrastructure projects that directly benefit specific properties, such as road paving, sewer installations, or drainage improvements. These levies are compulsory charges apportioned among affected property owners based on the estimated increase in property value or the extent of benefit received, rather than general taxation. Unlike ad valorem property taxes, special assessments require a nexus between the expenditure and the benefiting parcels, often established through local ordinances or voter approval in certain jurisdictions. For example, municipalities may create special assessment districts to recover costs for local enhancements like streetlights or sidewalks, with payments typically collected via property tax bills over time.

User fees, distinct from taxes, are charges for specific services provided by local governments, intended to cover the direct costs of delivery rather than general revenue. Common examples include water and sewer usage fees, solid waste collection charges, parking fees, and tolls for local bridges or roads. These fees often operate on a cost-recovery basis, with rates set to reflect operational expenses plus a margin for capital maintenance. In fiscal year 2021, charges for services accounted for approximately 14 percent of state and local own-source general revenues, totaling hundreds of billions of dollars, though local shares vary by municipality type. For U.S. cities, user charges—primarily from utilities and public services—rank as the top revenue source, surpassing property taxes in aggregate for many urban areas.

Licensing fees are levied for regulatory approvals and permissions, generating revenue while ensuring compliance with local standards. These include business licenses (e.g., for retail operations or food service), occupational licenses for trades like plumbing or cosmetology, building and zoning permits, and local portions of vehicle registration fees. Fees are typically scaled by business size, risk, or service type, with examples ranging from $50 to $550 for state-influenced local business permits. In addition to fines and forfeitures, local governments derived part of their miscellaneous revenues from such licenses, contributing to overall non-tax collections that reached $13 billion combined with state sources in 2021. These mechanisms allow localities to offset administrative costs but have drawn scrutiny for disproportionately burdening small businesses and low-income individuals when fees exceed regulatory necessities.

## Administration, Compliance, and Enforcement

### Federal Tax Agencies and Operations

The Internal Revenue Service (IRS) functions as the principal federal agency tasked with administering the Internal Revenue Code, collecting taxes, and enforcing compliance to support U.S. government funding. Operating under the Department of the Treasury, the IRS derives its authority from the Secretary of the Treasury, who holds plenary power to enforce internal revenue laws and has delegated operational responsibilities to the agency. Its mission emphasizes providing taxpayer services while ensuring fair enforcement, processing over 250 million returns annually and collecting trillions in revenue as of fiscal year 2023 data extended into ongoing operations.

The IRS is led by the Commissioner of Internal Revenue, appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate for a five-year term, alongside a Deputy Commissioner who shares oversight of divisions and functions. The Commissioner maintains a balance between taxpayer assistance and tax law enforcement, directing strategic priorities such as modernization and compliance efforts. Organizational structure includes operating divisions tailored to taxpayer segments—Wage and Investment for individuals, Small Business/Self-Employed, Large Business and International, and Tax-Exempt and Government Entities—supported by functions like Appeals, Chief Counsel, and Criminal Investigation. In August 2025, the IRS implemented a restructuring into four primary chiefs: Tax Compliance Officer, Chief Operating Officer, Taxpayer Services, and Chief Information Officer, aimed at enhancing efficiency amid ongoing modernization.

Core operations encompass tax return processing, refund issuance, and revenue collection through voluntary self-assessment, supplemented by mandatory withholding and estimated payments. Enforcement activities include audits, examinations, and civil penalties for underreporting, with the Criminal Investigation division handling willful evasion cases involving fraud, money laundering, and tax-related crimes, initiating thousands of investigations yearly. Collection operations target delinquent accounts via notices, liens, levies, and installment agreements, processing billions in overdue taxes while offering temporary delays for qualifying hardships. Taxpayer services provide guidance through phone support, online tools, and free filing options like Direct File. For tax planning advice in 2026, U.S.-focused sources include the official IRS website for tax rules, inflation adjustments, and official guidance; annual planning guides from major accounting firms such as KPMG, PwC, Grant Thornton, and Deloitte; and reputable financial education sites like NerdWallet and Fidelity Investments. Personalized advice should come from a qualified CPA or tax professional, as tax laws, including changes from recent legislation, require individual application. Operations faced disruptions in October 2025 due to a government funding lapse, limiting non-essential functions while preserving core deadlines.

### State and Local Tax Administration

State and local tax administration in the United States operates through a decentralized system of agencies, with states primarily managing their own income, sales, corporate, and excise taxes via dedicated departments of revenue, while local governments handle property taxes and certain fees through county or municipal offices. These entities oversee taxpayer registration, return processing, payment collection, audits, and enforcement, often leveraging electronic filing systems and withholding mechanisms for efficiency. For instance, state departments like Georgia's Department of Revenue administer tax laws, enforce regulations on controlled substances such as alcohol and tobacco, and manage motor vehicle titling alongside tax collection. In fiscal year 2021, local governments collected $609 billion in property taxes, representing 30 percent of their general revenue, primarily funding schools, counties, municipalities, and special districts.

Property tax administration at the local level involves assessors valuing real and personal property based on market conditions, with tax rates (millage) set by elected bodies or voter-approved levies from overlapping jurisdictions like school districts and fire departments. Appeals processes allow property owners to challenge assessments through local boards, with further recourse to state tax tribunals in some jurisdictions; for example, Minnesota counties handle initial administration, while the state Department of Revenue provides oversight and refund programs. Seventeen states permit local income taxes in addition to state levies, often collected directly by municipalities or counties, though some delegate administration to the state to reduce duplication.

Interstate coordination is facilitated by the Multistate Tax Commission (MTC), an intergovernmental agency established in 1967 under the Multistate Tax Compact to promote uniform state tax policies, apportionment of multistate income, and joint audits for efficiency in taxing interstate commerce. The MTC's Joint Audit Program allows participating states to pool resources for combined examinations of large taxpayers, reducing administrative burdens and minimizing double taxation risks, with 24 states as full members as of 2023. This addresses challenges like nexus determination for remote sellers post-Wayfair (2018), where economic presence triggers filing obligations across varying state thresholds.

### Self-Assessment, Reporting, and Audits

The United States federal tax system is predicated on self-assessment, requiring taxpayers to compute their own tax liabilities, file returns, and make payments without prior government determination of amounts owed. This framework, established with the modern income tax under the Revenue Act of 1913, depends on voluntary compliance, which the IRS measures through studies like the National Research Program involving random audits of representative samples. For tax year 2021, the gross tax gap—the difference between taxes owed and those paid voluntarily and timely—was estimated at $688 billion, yielding a voluntary compliance rate of 81.7 percent after late payments and enforcement recoveries. Compliance rates vary by tax type, exceeding 95 percent for wage withholding due to third-party reporting but dropping below 55 percent for underreported nonbusiness income reliant on self-assessment.

Taxpayers with gross income meeting filing thresholds—$14,600 for single filers under 65 in tax year 2024, for instance—must submit annual returns, primarily Form 1040 or its variants, detailing income, deductions, credits, and payments. The standard deadline is April 15 following the tax year, though fiscal-year filers adhere to the 15th day of the fourth month after their year-end; extensions via Form 4868 extend filing to October 15 but not payment deadlines. For tax year 2024, the IRS accepted over 140 million individual returns starting January 2025, with electronic filing mandated for most paid preparers and encouraged for its speed and error reduction, processing 90 percent of returns digitally in recent years. Estimated tax payments are required quarterly for those without sufficient withholding, using Form 1040-ES to avoid underpayment penalties.

The IRS verifies self-assessments through audits, which examine returns for accuracy and completeness, closing 505,514 examinations in fiscal year 2024 and recommending $29 billion in additional taxes. Selection methods include the Discriminant Inventory Function (DIF) system, a statistical model assigning scores to returns based on IRS historical data on similar filings to prioritize those with elevated underpayment risk; high DIF scores trigger further review, supplemented by Unreported Income DIF for detecting omitted earnings from information returns like W-2s. Random audits under the National Research Program update DIF formulas and compliance estimates without enforcement intent. Audits vary by type: correspondence audits resolve issues via mail for simpler discrepancies; office audits involve in-person meetings at IRS facilities; and field audits occur at taxpayer locations for complex cases. Overall audit coverage remains below 1 percent of individual returns, having declined two-thirds since 2010 amid resource constraints, though rates are higher—around 2-3 percent—for incomes over $1 million and projected to reach 16.5 percent for those exceeding $10 million by 2026 under enhanced funding directives.

### Penalties, Interest, and Dispute Resolution

The Internal Revenue Service (IRS) imposes civil penalties on taxpayers for various forms of noncompliance, including failure to file returns, failure to pay taxes due, and understatements attributable to negligence or fraud. The failure-to-file penalty applies at a rate of 5% of the unpaid tax for each month or fraction thereof that the return is late, capped at 25% of the unpaid amount. The failure-to-pay penalty accrues at 0.5% per month or fraction thereof on unpaid taxes after the return due date, also capped at 25%, though this rate increases to 1% per month if the IRS issues a notice of intent to levy and payment is not made within 10 days. Accuracy-related penalties equal 20% of the underpayment portion due to negligence, disregard of rules, or substantial understatement of income tax. Civil fraud penalties, assessed when intent to evade tax is proven, reach 75% of the underpayment attributable to fraud, and cannot be combined with accuracy-related penalties. Taxpayers may seek abatement of penalties for reasonable cause, such as unavoidable circumstances, or through first-time abatement for eligible compliant taxpayers.

Interest on underpayments accrues from the original due date of the return, regardless of extensions, at a rate determined quarterly under Internal Revenue Code section 6621, which sets it equal to the federal short-term rate plus 3 percentage points (or 5 points for large corporate underpayments). This interest compounds daily and applies to both the principal tax liability and any penalties. For the fourth quarter of 2025, the underpayment rate for individuals and non-corporate entities stands at 7% per year. Overpayments, conversely, earn interest at a lower rate (federal short-term rate plus 2 points for individuals, or plus 0.5 points under GATT for certain corporate overpayments exceeding $10,000), also compounded daily, but only after the return is filed and processed. Interest cannot be abated except in cases of IRS error, and it continues until full payment, incentivizing prompt settlement to minimize compounding costs.

Tax disputes typically arise from audits or notices of deficiency, with resolution beginning through administrative channels before potential litigation. Taxpayers may protest IRS determinations by submitting a written appeal to the IRS Independent Office of Appeals, a quasi-judicial body tasked with impartial resolution without court involvement, provided the case meets eligibility criteria such as disagreement with an audit adjustment or collection action. Appeals conferences involve negotiation toward settlement, often considering hazards of litigation, and can include mediation programs for complex cases; acceptance of an Appeals decision is binding unless fraud or mutual mistake is later proven. If Appeals upholds the IRS position, taxpayers can petition the U.S. Tax Court (for deficiencies before payment, allowing trial without prepaying tax), U.S. District Courts (requiring full payment first and refund suit), or the U.S. Court of Federal Claims (also post-payment). The Tax Court, specialized in tax matters, hears cases de novo and issues precedential opinions, while District Courts and Claims Court handle broader federal claims but require jurisdictional amounts and exhaustion of administrative remedies. State-level disputes follow analogous processes through respective revenue departments and courts, varying by jurisdiction but often mirroring federal self-assessment and appeal structures.
