# Labor Movement and Industrial Conflict

## Introduction

The Gilded Age and Progressive Era (1877–1920) witnessed the bloodiest labor conflicts in American history as industrial workers fought for the eight-hour day, living wages, and the right to organize against employers wielding private police forces, state militias, and federal troops to crush strikes. The Knights of Labor reached 700,000 members by 1886 before collapsing after the Haymarket affair. The American Federation of Labor (AFL), organized by Samuel Gompers in 1886, pursued 'bread-and-butter' craft unionism focused on skilled workers — excluding Black workers, women, and most immigrants from meaningful representation.

The Great Railroad Strike of 1877 — the first nationwide strike in American history — began when B&O Railroad workers protested a 10% wage cut and spread to 14 states, paralyzing rail traffic. President Hayes deployed federal troops — the first use of federal military force to break a strike — killing at least 100 workers. The Homestead Strike of 1892 pitted Carnegie Steel workers against 300 Pinkerton agents hired by Henry Clay Frick, leaving 10 men dead on the Monongahela River. The Pullman Strike of 1894, shutting down rail traffic across 27 states, was broken when President Cleveland obtained a federal injunction under the Sherman Antitrust Act — the first use of antitrust law against a labor union — and deployed 12,000 federal troops.

## Triangle Shirtwaist and Labor Reform

### Progressive Labor Legislation

The Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire of March 25, 1911, in New York City killed 146 garment workers — mostly young immigrant women — when exits were found locked and the fire escape collapsed. The disaster produced a decade of New York labor legislation: fire codes, factory inspection laws, worker's compensation, and limits on working hours. Investigators Frances Perkins and Al Smith used the disaster as a template for the New Deal's labor standards framework two decades later. The Clayton Antitrust Act of 1914 exempted labor unions from prosecution under the Sherman Act and restricted injunctions against strikes, which AFL president Gompers called 'Labor's Magna Carta.' The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 established the federal minimum wage, the 40-hour workweek, and eliminated most child labor.
