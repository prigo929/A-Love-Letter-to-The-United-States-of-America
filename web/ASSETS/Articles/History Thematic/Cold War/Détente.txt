# Détente

## Introduction

Détente was a period of moderated hostilities and diplomatic engagement between the United States and the Soviet Union during the Cold War, roughly spanning from 1969 to 1979, marked by efforts to stabilize superpower relations through arms control, trade expansion, and mutual accommodation of strategic interests rather than direct confrontation. Initiated by U.S. President Richard Nixon and National Security Advisor Henry Kissinger, détente sought to manage the nuclear arms race and extract economic benefits from Soviet grain purchases while leveraging Sino-Soviet tensions to pressure Moscow. Key achievements included the 1972 Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT I) agreements, comprising the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty restricting defensive systems to two sites per side (later reduced to one) and an interim five-year freeze on offensive intercontinental ballistic missile and submarine-launched ballistic missile launchers. The 1975 Helsinki Final Act, endorsed by 35 European and North American states, recognized postwar frontiers, encouraged economic and scientific cooperation, and pledged respect for human rights and fundamental freedoms, though the latter provision spurred dissident movements in the Eastern Bloc without compelling Soviet behavioral change. Symbolic milestones like the 1975 Apollo-Soyuz joint space mission highlighted technical collaboration amid ongoing ideological rivalry. Critics, particularly conservatives, contended that détente masked Soviet military buildup and proxy interventions in regions such as Angola and the Horn of Africa, failing to curb expansionist tendencies rooted in communist doctrine, which ultimately contributed to its erosion after the 1979 Soviet invasion of Afghanistan.

## Definition and Conceptual Framework

### Origins and Etymology

The term détente derives from the French word détente, meaning "relaxation" or "loosening," originating from the Old French destente, a derivative of destendre ("to relax" or "to slacken"), which combines the prefix des- (indicating reversal or removal, from Latin dis-) with tendre ("to stretch," from Latin tendere). This etymological root evokes the image of releasing tension, such as slackening a bowstring after shooting an arrow, a metaphor later applied to diplomatic contexts.

In diplomatic usage, détente first appeared in the early 1900s to describe efforts by European great powers to ease pre-World War I tensions, marking its transition from general French lexicon to international relations terminology. By the mid-20th century, the concept gained prominence in the context of U.S.-Soviet relations during the Cold War, where it denoted a strategic relaxation of hostilities amid nuclear parity and economic strains, particularly from the late 1960s onward under President Richard Nixon's administration. In Russian, the equivalent term was razryadka ("discharge" or "relaxation of tension"), reflecting parallel Soviet framing of the policy as a release from escalatory pressures post-Cuban Missile Crisis in 1962. This bilingual adoption underscored the mutual interest in stabilizing superpower competition without conceding ideological ground.

### Strategic Rationale from First Principles

The advent of nuclear parity between the United States and the Soviet Union by the late 1960s rendered total military victory unattainable, as both possessed deliverable warheads sufficient for mutual societal destruction, incentivizing rational leaders to stabilize rivalry through verifiable restraints rather than perpetual escalation. This calculus derived from the immutable reality of mutually assured destruction: any attempt at unilateral dominance risked preemptive catastrophe, while unchecked competition diverted resources from internal imperatives, such as economic productivity and technological innovation. Empirical evidence of this strain included the Soviet Union's achievement of intercontinental ballistic missile equivalence, with over 1,000 launchers operational by 1969, mirroring U.S. capabilities and eliminating prior asymmetries that once permitted aggressive containment doctrines.

From the U.S. perspective, détente embodied realpolitik under Nixon and Kissinger, prioritizing balance-of-power management over ideological crusades, as Soviet nuclear equivalence demanded acceptance of coexistence on terms that preserved American interests without overextension. The Vietnam War's fiscal toll—exceeding $150 billion by 1973—and resultant inflation peaking at 11% in 1974 underscored the unsustainability of simultaneous global policing and arms buildup, prompting a shift toward linkage: tying economic and technological concessions to Soviet behavioral moderation in third-world proxies. This approach exploited the 1969 Sino-Soviet border clashes, which exposed Moscow's vulnerabilities on its eastern flank, allowing Washington to cultivate Beijing as a counterweight and compel Kremlin's concessions without direct confrontation. Kissinger articulated this as reducing "the risks that a cold war would become a hot one," emphasizing empirical verification of arms limits over trust-based disarmament.

Soviet motivations under Brezhnev similarly rooted in causal constraints: chronic economic underperformance, with agricultural shortfalls necessitating massive grain imports, and military outlays consuming up to 15-20% of GDP, threatened regime stability amid ideological commitments to global expansion. Détente offered access to Western grain and technology transfers—evident in the 1972 U.S.-USSR Trade Agreement—to alleviate domestic pressures, while diplomatic engagement countered the Sino-Soviet rift's isolation effects, preventing a U.S.-China axis that could encircle the USSR. Brezhnev viewed this as leveraging "correlation of forces" in Moscow's favor, using eased tensions to consolidate Eastern Bloc control and project predictability, thereby securing parity without the perils of adventurism. Yet, this rationale presupposed reciprocal restraint, as Soviet doctrinal adherence to revolutionary opportunities inherently clashed with U.S. stability goals, foreshadowing détente's fragility.

## Pre-Détente Context

### Escalating Tensions Post-Cuba Missile Crisis

Despite the narrow avoidance of nuclear war during the Cuban Missile Crisis of October 1962, the United States and Soviet Union rapidly expanded their strategic arsenals throughout the 1960s, driven by mutual suspicions and technological advancements. The U.S. deployed hundreds of Minuteman ICBMs equipped with multiple independently targetable reentry vehicles (MIRVs) by the late 1960s, enhancing its ability to overwhelm Soviet defenses, while the Soviet Union introduced the SS-9 Scarp ICBM in the mid-1960s, capable of carrying multi-megaton warheads and achieving rough parity in throw-weight by the decade's end. This escalation reflected a first-principles recognition that deterrence required not just equivalence but superiority in delivery systems, as each side anticipated preemptive strikes from the other.

Proxy conflicts further intensified confrontations, particularly in Vietnam, where U.S. ground forces surged from approximately 16,300 advisors in 1963 to over 536,000 combat troops by 1968, following the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution on August 10, 1964, and the initiation of Operation Rolling Thunder bombing campaigns against North Vietnam in March 1965. The Soviet Union countered by providing extensive military aid to Hanoi, including SA-2 surface-to-air missiles, MiG-21 fighters, anti-aircraft artillery, and logistical support such as petroleum and ammunition, establishing itself as the primary supplier of advanced weaponry that downed over 2,000 U.S. aircraft. This indirect superpower clash raised risks of direct intervention, as Soviet advisors trained North Vietnamese forces and Moscow issued warnings against U.S. strikes on Hanoi, heightening fears of broader escalation amid the 1968 Tet Offensive.

Additional flashpoints underscored the fragility of restraint. On January 23, 1968, North Korean forces, backed by Soviet technical assistance, seized the USS Pueblo intelligence ship in international waters off Wonsan, capturing 83 crew members and extracting coerced confessions, which prolonged the crisis for 11 months and diverted U.S. attention during peak Vietnam commitments. Later that year, on August 20, 1968, Soviet-led Warsaw Pact troops invaded Czechoslovakia to suppress the Prague Spring reforms under Alexander Dubček, deploying over 500,000 soldiers and 6,000 tanks, an action that affirmed the Brezhnev Doctrine of limited sovereignty and elicited Western condemnation, reinforcing perceptions of Soviet expansionism just as U.S. President Lyndon B. Johnson sought diplomatic openings. These events collectively amplified economic strains and public war-weariness, setting the stage for mutual interest in stabilizing relations.

### Economic and Military Pressures on Superpowers

The United States encountered significant economic strains in the late 1960s and early 1970s, exacerbated by the Vietnam War, which cost approximately $168 billion in direct expenditures from 1965 to 1975, equivalent to over $1 trillion in current dollars, including $111 billion on military operations and $28.5 billion in aid. This spending contributed to stagflation—high inflation combined with economic stagnation—fueled by fiscal deficits and resource diversion from domestic priorities. The 1973 oil embargo by OPEC nations quadrupled global oil prices, triggering an energy crisis that intensified inflation to 11% by 1974 and prompted recessionary pressures, further straining budgets amid ongoing military commitments. U.S. military expenditures hovered around 5-6% of GDP during this period, reflecting the arms race's toll but remaining far lower than the Soviet Union's share.

The Soviet Union grappled with deeper structural economic weaknesses under Leonid Brezhnev's leadership, characterized by stagnation from bureaucratic inefficiencies, resource shortfalls, and prioritization of heavy industry over consumer goods. Military spending consumed an estimated 15-20% of GDP by the 1970s—compared to the U.S. figure of about 6%—diverting resources from civilian sectors and contributing to chronic underproductivity, though CIA analyses concluded it was not the sole driver of slowdowns. Procurement and force expansions grew at over 5% annually in the late 1960s and early 1970s, sustaining parity in nuclear and conventional capabilities but at the expense of agricultural and technological innovation, leading to food shortages and declining growth rates.

These parallel pressures—nuclear arms race costs exceeding sustainable levels for both sides, proxy conflicts like Vietnam draining U.S. resources, and Soviet overcommitment to defense amid internal decay—created incentives for mutual de-escalation to avert fiscal collapse without conceding strategic advantages. Domestic economic difficulties, including U.S. inflation from war and oil shocks alongside Soviet prioritization of military over welfare, underscored the unsustainability of confrontation, prompting leaders to pursue détente as a pragmatic means to cap expenditures and stabilize relations.

## Implementation and Key Policies

### Nixon-Kissinger Strategy

The Nixon administration, upon assuming office on January 20, 1969, confronted a strategic environment marked by the escalating costs of the Vietnam War, approximate nuclear parity with the Soviet Union, and diminishing U.S. leverage amid global overextension. President Nixon and National Security Advisor Henry Kissinger adopted a realist framework prioritizing the management of power balances over ideological confrontation, recognizing that mutual nuclear vulnerability necessitated coexistence to avert catastrophic war rather than pursuit of total victory. This approach sought to impose discipline on superpower competition by linking concessions in one domain—such as arms control—to Soviet restraint in others, including Third World adventurism and support for North Vietnam, thereby restoring U.S. credibility while extracting behavioral limits from Moscow.

Central to the strategy was the doctrine of "linkage," which rejected compartmentalized negotiations in favor of holistic diplomacy tying economic incentives, technology transfers, and trade normalization to Soviet compliance across issues like Berlin access and Vietnam withdrawal facilitation. Kissinger established direct back-channel communications, known as "The Channel," with Soviet Ambassador Anatoly Dobrynin starting in 1971, circumventing bureaucratic inertia to enable candid exchanges and build tacit understandings. This facilitated preparatory groundwork for arms limitation talks, including the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT) initiated in November 1969, aimed at capping offensive missiles and defensive systems to stabilize the nuclear balance without conceding strategic inferiority.

The strategy also incorporated geopolitical triangulation, leveraging the Sino-Soviet rift—evident in border clashes from 1969—to compel Soviet concessions by demonstrating U.S. flexibility toward Beijing as a counterweight, though primary focus remained on Moscow to prevent bipolar rigidity. Nixon articulated this "era of negotiations" in his inaugural address, emphasizing pragmatic restraint over confrontation, with détente envisioned not as ideological convergence but as a code of conduct regulating competition amid mutual deterrence. Implementation emphasized verifiable agreements over unverifiable goodwill, reflecting skepticism of Soviet intentions derived from historical patterns of expansion, while domestic pressures for Vietnam extrication underscored the urgency of reorienting resources from peripheral conflicts to core superpower dynamics.

### Opening to China as Prelude

The Sino-Soviet split, which intensified after ideological divergences in the late 1950s and erupted into border clashes in March and August 1969 along the Ussuri River, created a strategic rift that the United States under President Richard Nixon sought to exploit through triangular diplomacy. National Security Advisor Henry Kissinger recognized the mutual animosity between Moscow and Beijing as an opportunity to balance Soviet influence by engaging the People's Republic of China (PRC), thereby compelling the Soviet Union to negotiate from a position of relative isolation. This approach, articulated in Nixon's 1967 Foreign Affairs article as leveraging communist rivalries, aimed to end U.S. isolation of China—rooted in the Korean War-era embargo—and reposition global power dynamics without conceding strategic ground.

Preparatory diplomacy began with informal signals, including the April 1971 "ping-pong diplomacy" where the U.S. table tennis team visited China, followed by eased trade and travel restrictions announced by Nixon in October 1971. The pivotal step occurred on July 9-11, 1971, when Kissinger undertook a clandestine trip to Beijing, departing from Pakistan to avoid detection, and met Premier Zhou Enlai to coordinate Nixon's forthcoming visit. This secret engagement secured Chinese assent for presidential-level talks, with Kissinger relaying Mao Zedong's receptivity to improved relations despite ongoing Taiwan tensions. Nixon publicly announced the invitation on July 15, 1971, shocking global observers and accelerating U.S.-PRC rapprochement.

Nixon's landmark visit to China from February 21-28, 1972, marked the first by a sitting U.S. president, involving meetings with Mao Zedong on February 21 and Zhou Enlai, culminating in the Shanghai Communiqué on February 28. The communiqué affirmed U.S. acknowledgment of a single Chinese state, opposed any Taiwan independence efforts, and committed both sides to normalizing relations through expanded contacts, though full diplomatic recognition awaited 1979. Strategically, this opening pressured the Soviet Union, which had been advancing arms talks but now faced a U.S.-China alignment; Soviet leaders accelerated détente initiatives, leading to Nixon's Moscow summit in May 1972 and the SALT I framework. By introducing multipolarity into superpower rivalry, the China prelude diluted Soviet leverage in Asia and Europe, fostering mutual U.S.-Soviet restraint without resolving underlying ideological conflicts.

### Major Summits and Diplomatic Engagements

The Moscow Summit of May 22–30, 1972, marked the first visit by a U.S. president to the Soviet Union since World War II, where President Richard Nixon met General Secretary Leonid Brezhnev to negotiate arms control and broader bilateral relations. The talks produced the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT) Interim Agreement, freezing the number of intercontinental ballistic missiles and submarine-launched ballistic missiles at existing levels for five years, alongside the Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty limiting defensive systems to two sites per side. These accords aimed to curb the nuclear arms race amid mutual recognition of strategic parity.

In June 1973, Brezhnev reciprocated with a visit to the United States for the Washington Summit, held from June 18–25, engaging Nixon in discussions on preventing nuclear war and expanding economic ties. The leaders signed the Agreement on the Prevention of Nuclear War, committing to consultations to reduce escalation risks, and advanced joint efforts in science, technology, and trade, including a pledge for most-favored-nation status contingent on Soviet emigration policies. This summit reinforced détente's momentum through over 100 hours of cumulative dialogue across Nixon-Brezhnev meetings.

President Gerald Ford continued these efforts at the Vladivostok Summit on November 23–24, 1974, meeting Brezhnev in the Soviet Far East to outline SALT II parameters. They agreed to equal aggregate limits of 2,400 strategic delivery vehicles per side, including 1,320 multiple independently targetable reentry vehicles, with a seven-year timeline for completion. The joint communiqué emphasized parity and verification, setting the stage for subsequent negotiations despite unresolved details.

## Achievements and Tangible Outcomes

### Arms Control and Limitation Treaties

The Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT) represented the cornerstone of arms control efforts during détente, initiating formal negotiations in November 1969 between the United States and the Soviet Union to curb the nuclear arms race. SALT I culminated in two agreements signed on May 26, 1972, by President Richard Nixon and General Secretary Leonid Brezhnev in Moscow: the Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty and the Interim Agreement on offensive strategic weapons.

The ABM Treaty prohibited the deployment of sea-based, space-based, or air-based ABM systems and limited ground-based systems to two fixed sites per party, with each site covering no more than 150 launchers and interceptors, later amended to one site in 1974. This restriction aimed to preserve mutual assured destruction by preventing either side from achieving a defensive shield against retaliatory strikes. The Interim Agreement froze the number of intercontinental ballistic missile (ICBM) and submarine-launched ballistic missile (SLBM) launchers for five years at existing levels: the United States at 1,054 ICBMs and 656 SLBMs, while the Soviet Union maintained higher totals of approximately 1,618 ICBMs and 950 SLBMs, reflecting disparities in force structures but halting quantitative expansion.

Building on SALT I, negotiations continued into SALT II, which sought deeper limits on strategic offensive arms. A breakthrough occurred at the Vladivostok Summit on November 24, 1974, where President Gerald Ford and Brezhnev agreed to a framework capping total strategic delivery vehicles at 2,400 per side, with no more than 1,320 multiple independently targetable reentry vehicle (MIRV)-equipped missiles. The full SALT II Treaty was signed on June 18, 1979, in Vienna by President Jimmy Carter and Brezhnev, further reducing ceilings to 2,250 delivery vehicles by 1981 and imposing sub-limits on MIRVed systems, bombers, and cruise missiles, while banning new fixed ICBM silo construction and limiting missile modernization.

### Scientific and Cultural Exchanges

Scientific and cultural exchanges expanded significantly during the period of détente, serving as mechanisms to foster mutual understanding and reduce hostilities between the United States and the Soviet Union. These initiatives built upon earlier frameworks like the 1958 Lacy-Zarubin Agreement but intensified in the 1970s through bilateral protocols and commissions aimed at cooperation in non-military fields. Exchanges often prioritized areas such as space exploration, health research, and performing arts, though Soviet authorities tightly controlled content to align with ideological goals, limiting exposure to dissenting voices.

The Apollo-Soyuz Test Project (ASTP) in July 1975 exemplified scientific collaboration, marking the first joint U.S.-Soviet crewed space mission. Launched on July 15, 1975, the Soviet Soyuz 19 spacecraft carried cosmonauts Alexei Leonov and Valery Kubasov, while the U.S. Apollo module, commanded by Thomas Stafford with Vance Brand and Donald "Deke" Slayton, lifted off shortly after; the vehicles docked on July 17, enabling crew transfers and joint experiments in orbit for two days. This docking, dubbed a "handshake in space," involved 28 technical experiments, including biological studies on plant growth and microbial behavior, and demonstrated compatible spacecraft docking systems despite technological asymmetries favoring Soviet heavy-lift capabilities. The project, initiated under a 1972 space agreement signed during the Nixon-Brezhnev summit, cost the U.S. approximately $250 million and symbolized détente's potential for practical cooperation, though critics noted it provided the Soviets with insights into American engineering without reciprocal technological depth.

Beyond space, joint commissions facilitated exchanges in biomedical and environmental sciences. The U.S.-U.S.S.R. Joint Committee on Health, established in 1972, enabled researcher visits and collaborative studies on cardiovascular diseases, with over 100 American scientists traveling to the Soviet Union by 1976 for workshops and data sharing. Similar efforts in environmental protection addressed shared concerns like ocean pollution, yielding protocols for joint monitoring signed in 1976. These interactions, while yielding modest advancements, disproportionately benefited Soviet access to Western methodologies, as U.S. participants often encountered restricted Soviet facilities and censored publications.

### Resolution of Regional Flashpoints

The Four Power Agreement on Berlin, signed on September 3, 1971, by the United States, Soviet Union, United Kingdom, and France, secured unimpeded civilian access from West Germany to West Berlin via transit routes and airspace, while affirming the city's status outside the Federal Republic of Germany but guaranteeing allied rights. This accord alleviated a longstanding flashpoint by reducing East German interference and Soviet threats, contributing to stabilized Central European relations amid détente negotiations.

The 1975 Helsinki Final Act, concluded by 35 European and North American states including the superpowers, formalized acceptance of post-World War II borders, thereby diminishing irredentist claims and the risk of territorial disputes escalating into East-West conflict. By prioritizing "inviolability of frontiers" alongside confidence-building measures like prior notification of military maneuvers, the accords fostered a framework for European security that tempered superpower rivalry over divided nations like Germany.

In the Middle East, following the 1973 Yom Kippur War, U.S. Secretary of State Henry Kissinger's shuttle diplomacy yielded the first Egyptian-Israeli disengagement agreement on January 18, 1974, establishing a buffer zone monitored by UN forces, and a Syrian-Israeli accord on May 31, 1974, which withdrew forces from the Golan Heights' purple line. These pacts, achieved without direct Soviet mediation despite initial Arab-Soviet alignment, prevented further superpower entanglement by sidelining Moscow's resupply role and averting a broader crisis that could undermine détente. Overall, such resolutions contained proxy escalations, prioritizing de-escalation over ideological competition.

## Criticisms and Failures

### One-Sided Benefits to Soviet Union

Critics of détente argued that it provided disproportionate advantages to the Soviet Union by alleviating economic pressures and enabling military expansion without requiring reciprocal internal reforms or restraint in foreign policy. During the 1970s, the USSR benefited from access to Western grain and technology, which bolstered its economy and industrial base amid chronic agricultural shortfalls and technological lags, while the United States faced domestic shortages and higher food prices as a result. The 1972 U.S.-Soviet grain agreement, often termed the "Great Grain Robbery," saw the Soviets purchase approximately 10 million metric tons of American wheat at below-market prices—equivalent to one-third of the U.S. exportable surplus—exploiting undisclosed Soviet crop failures from poor weather, which contributed to a 30% rise in global food prices and decimated U.S. stockpiles. This deal, negotiated under détente's spirit of economic cooperation, allowed the USSR to feed its population and livestock without addressing systemic inefficiencies in collectivized agriculture, imposing no equivalent concessions from Moscow.

Technological transfers further tilted benefits toward the Soviets, as U.S. policies under détente relaxed export controls, facilitating the flow of dual-use high-technology in electronics, metallurgy, aircraft design, and computing to the USSR, directly enhancing its military-industrial capabilities. For instance, reforms in the 1969 Export Administration Act encouraged such trade with Soviet bloc nations, enabling Moscow to acquire Western innovations that compensated for domestic R&D shortcomings and supported advancements in strategic weapons systems. Soviet acquisition efforts during this period, including through joint ventures and licensing, were estimated to contribute significantly to military modernization, with U.S. intelligence assessing that such transfers under détente could yield major gains in Soviet war-making potential without comparable U.S. access to Soviet technology. This one-way dynamic persisted despite concerns that détente's emphasis on cooperation masked Soviet exploitation for unilateral advantage.

Militarily, the Soviet Union undertook an unprecedented buildup during the détente era, increasing strategic forces without interruption, while arms control agreements like SALT I (signed May 26, 1972) effectively froze U.S. advantages in submarine-launched ballistic missiles and permitted Soviet parity in intercontinental ballistic missiles, allowing Moscow to close technological gaps. From 1972 onward, Soviet defense spending rose steadily, with no evidence of reductions in overall military strength; by 1974, Western analysts noted significant expansions in naval, air, and ground forces, interpreting this as evidence that the USSR viewed détente as compatible with aggressive militarization rather than mutual de-escalation. The Interim Offensive Arms Agreement under SALT I limited both sides to existing missile launchers but disadvantaged the U.S. by codifying Soviet numerical superiority in land-based ICBMs (approximately 1,618 Soviet versus 1,054 U.S. by 1972), enabling continued Soviet deployment of multiple independently targetable reentry vehicles (MIRVs) that evaded the treaty's silos-only focus. This asymmetry was compounded by the USSR's exploitation of détente to pursue interests without fear of U.S. countermeasures, as evidenced by persistent high force levels and investments that outpaced economic growth. Overall, these elements substantiated claims that détente functioned as a period of Soviet consolidation, granting economic relief and strategic breathing room at minimal cost to an expansionist regime.

### Domestic Opposition from US Conservatives

Prominent figures within the Republican Party and aligned conservative intellectuals mounted significant opposition to Détente during the Nixon and Ford administrations, arguing that the policy naively equated the democratic United States with the expansionist Soviet regime and failed to deter Moscow's military buildup. Critics contended that arms control agreements like SALT I, signed on May 26, 1972, permitted the Soviets to maintain numerical superiority in intercontinental ballistic missiles (6,000 to the U.S.'s 1,054) while ignoring qualitative advancements in Soviet weaponry, effectively rewarding aggression rather than achieving parity. This perspective gained traction amid revelations of Soviet violations of earlier understandings and their support for proxy wars, leading conservatives to view Détente as a strategic concession that eroded U.S. deterrence.

Ronald Reagan emerged as a leading voice against Détente during his 1976 Republican presidential primary challenge to incumbent Gerald Ford, decrying the policy for fostering an illusion of cooperation while the Soviets pursued unilateral gains. In speeches and campaign rhetoric, Reagan portrayed Détente as morally equivocal, insisting it overlooked the fundamentally ideological nature of Soviet communism and its drive for global dominance, rather than treating it as a benign great-power rivalry. His critique resonated with the emerging New Right, who pressured Ford to distance himself from the term "Détente" during the campaign, reflecting internal GOP divisions that weakened support for the policy. Reagan's stance, echoed by figures like Senator Barry Goldwater, framed Détente not as pragmatic diplomacy but as a dangerous relaxation of vigilance that invited Soviet adventurism.

Senator Henry M. Jackson (D-WA), though a Democrat, forged alliances with conservatives in opposing Détente's core elements, particularly its arms control and trade components, which he saw as decoupled from Soviet human rights abuses and emigration restrictions. Jackson labeled SALT I a "bum deal" akin to pre-World War II appeasement, arguing it locked in Soviet advantages without verifiable compliance mechanisms or constraints on multiple independently targetable reentry vehicles (MIRVs). His advocacy for the Jackson-Vanik Amendment, enacted on January 3, 1975, conditioned most-favored-nation trade status on freedom of emigration, directly challenging Détente's economic détente by prioritizing moral leverage over commercial incentives; the amendment passed the Senate 72-4 and House 323-91, signaling bipartisan hawkishness.

### Ignoring Soviet Human Rights Abuses and Expansionism

During the period of détente, U.S. policymakers under Presidents Nixon and Ford largely subordinated concerns over Soviet human rights violations to the pursuit of strategic arms control and bilateral stability, a stance exemplified by the handling of dissident Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn. Following the 1973 publication of The Gulag Archipelago, which documented the Soviet system's extensive network of forced-labor camps holding millions since the 1930s, Solzhenitsyn was arrested and deported in February 1974 after losing his citizenship. Secretary of State Henry Kissinger advised President Ford against meeting Solzhenitsyn, arguing that such an encounter would undermine U.S.-Soviet relations by endorsing views perceived as advocating the overthrow of the Soviet regime. Kissinger similarly downplayed Jewish emigration from the USSR as irrelevant to American foreign policy objectives, despite reports of systemic discrimination and refusenik persecution affecting tens of thousands. This approach reflected a broader realpolitik framework that treated internal Soviet repression— including ongoing psychiatric abuse of dissidents and suppression of religious freedoms—as secondary to geopolitical bargaining, even as Western broadcasts like Voice of America faced internal pressures to limit coverage of such issues to avoid provoking Moscow.

The 1975 Helsinki Final Act, signed by Ford on August 1, further highlighted this prioritization, as its third "basket" provisions on human rights and freer movement of people and ideas were accepted by the U.S. as a minor concession to secure Soviet recognition of post-World War II European borders, despite foreseeable non-compliance. Soviet authorities promptly ignored these commitments, arresting over 100 individuals for monitoring violations by the newly formed Moscow Helsinki Watch Group in 1976 and intensifying KGB operations against activists like Andrei Sakharov, who faced internal exile in 1980. U.S. responses remained muted, with Ford defending the accords against domestic critics who argued they legitimized Soviet domination of Eastern Europe without enforcing accountability for abuses, such as the continued imprisonment of political prisoners estimated at 10,000–15,000 in psychiatric hospitals alone by the mid-1970s.

Parallel to this, détente entailed overlooking Soviet expansionist activities in the Third World, where Moscow exploited proxy conflicts to extend influence without direct superpower confrontation. In Angola's 1975 civil war, the Soviet Union airlifted over 36,000 tons of arms to the Marxist MPLA faction and facilitated the deployment of 36,000 Cuban troops, tipping the balance against U.S.-backed opponents; the Ford administration provided covert CIA funding (about $32 million) to rivals but refrained from escalation to preserve SALT negotiations, despite congressional opposition from figures like Senator Henry Jackson who viewed it as rewarding aggression. Similarly, in the 1977–1978 Ogaden War, the Soviets orchestrated a shift of $1 billion in aid and 15,000 Cuban forces from Somalia to Ethiopia, enabling the Marxist Derg regime to conquer the region and topple Somalia's pro-Western government; U.S. protests were diplomatic only, with no military countermeasures, allowing Moscow to consolidate a foothold in the Horn of Africa amid détente's emphasis on mutual restraint in Europe. Critics, including conservative coalitions, contended that such leniency—coupled with Soviet naval expansions and violations of the 1972 Biological Weapons Convention—eroded Western deterrence, as Moscow interpreted U.S. forbearance as acquiescence to Brezhnev Doctrine-style interventions under the guise of anti-colonial support. This pattern of selective blindness contributed to perceptions of détente as asymmetrically beneficial, prioritizing short-term accords over confronting the ideological and territorial imperatives driving Soviet behavior.
