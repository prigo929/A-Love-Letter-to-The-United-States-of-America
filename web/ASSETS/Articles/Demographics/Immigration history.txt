# Immigration history

## Introduction

Immigration to the United States involves the entry and settlement of foreign nationals, legally or otherwise, transforming the country's population from predominantly European colonists to a diverse society with a foreign-born share reaching 15.8 percent, or 53.3 million individuals, as of January 2025. Early policies from the 18th and 19th centuries encouraged open entry to populate the frontier and fuel economic expansion, with federal regulation emerging in 1891 amid rising arrivals that exceeded 14.5 million between 1900 and 1920. Subsequent restrictions, including the 1920s national origins quotas, curbed inflows until the 1965 Immigration and Nationality Act prioritized family ties and skills over ancestry, diversifying sources from Europe to Latin America and Asia. Empirical analyses consistently find immigration bolstering long-term economic growth through labor augmentation, innovation, and wage gains for most native workers, though short-term fiscal burdens and assimilation challenges have fueled policy debates and enforcement efforts.

## Historical Overview

### Colonial and Early Republic Period

Immigration to the British American colonies began with the establishment of Jamestown in 1607 by English settlers seeking economic opportunities under the Virginia Company. By 1641, approximately 300 ships had transported around 20,000 settlers to the colonies, predominantly from England, driven by land acquisition and trade prospects. The Great Migration to New England from 1629 to 1640 brought over 20,000 English Puritans fleeing religious persecution, establishing communities focused on self-governance and agriculture. Throughout the 17th century, the majority of European immigrants originated from England, comprising the principal demographic component of the colonial population, which doubled every generation due to high birth rates and continued inflows.

A significant portion of white immigrants arrived as indentured servants, with estimates indicating 96,600 indentured laborers and 66,300 free immigrants between 1607 and 1699, often exchanging four to seven years of labor for passage and basic provisions. Indentured servitude accounted for nearly two-thirds of British settlers in the 17th century, particularly prevalent in the Chesapeake colonies where labor demands for tobacco cultivation were high. Britain also transported about 50,000 convicts to the colonies before the Revolution, despite colonial opposition to the practice as a form of punishment. By 1775, cumulative European immigration, including later arrivals from Scotland, Ireland, Germany, and the Netherlands, had contributed to a colonial population exceeding 2.5 million, though exact inflows are estimates due to limited records.

In the early Republic, following independence in 1776, the United States maintained an open immigration policy, encouraging settlement to populate western territories and bolster the economy, with no federal restrictions on entry until the late 19th century. The Naturalization Act of 1790 established the first uniform rule, granting citizenship to any "free white person" of good character after two years' residency and an oath of allegiance, explicitly limiting eligibility to those of European descent and excluding non-whites. This framework reflected the framers' intent to preserve a homogeneous republic, as articulated in congressional debates favoring immigrants aligned with republican values. Subsequent acts in 1795 extended residency to five years amid concerns over rapid influxes, while the 1798 Alien and Sedition Acts authorized deportation of non-citizens deemed dangerous during wartime tensions with France, though rarely enforced. Immigration records commenced in 1820, capturing the onset of increased European arrivals post-1815.

### 19th-Century Mass Immigration

The 19th century marked the onset of mass immigration to the United States, with federal records beginning systematic tracking in 1820 under the Steerage Act, which required captains to report passenger manifests. Between 1820 and 1860, approximately 5 million immigrants arrived, primarily from northern and western Europe, including Britain, Ireland, and Germany; this influx accelerated after the Civil War, culminating in nearly 12 million arrivals between 1870 and 1900, mostly Europeans entering through ports like New York, Philadelphia, and Boston. By 1900, the foreign-born population had swelled to about 10.3 million, comprising 13.6% of the total U.S. population, up from 2.2 million (9.7%) in 1850.

Key drivers included push factors in Europe—such as crop failures, land scarcity, and political upheavals—and pull factors in the U.S., including rapid industrialization demanding low-wage labor for factories, railroads, and mining, alongside abundant land opportunities in the expanding frontier. The Irish Potato Famine (1845–1852) exemplified acute push pressures, devastating Ireland's potato-dependent subsistence economy and causing over 1 million deaths from starvation and disease; in response, roughly 500,000 Irish, predominantly Catholic and unskilled, emigrated to the U.S. during those years alone, often arriving in overcrowded "coffin ships" with high mortality rates en route. German immigration surged in the 1840s–1850s, with over 1.5 million arrivals fleeing the failed revolutions of 1848 and seeking economic stability; many settled in Midwestern farming communities or urban trades. These migrants filled labor shortages amid the U.S. Industrial Revolution, contributing to infrastructure projects like the transcontinental railroad and urban growth, though they faced exploitation in tenement housing and hazardous jobs.

Immigration policy remained largely open, with no numerical quotas or literacy tests until the late century; the federal government focused on minimal regulation, such as head taxes, while states handled some arrivals until 1891 federal oversight. This era saw rising nativist backlash, particularly against Irish Catholics perceived as culturally incompatible and economically competitive; the Know-Nothing Party, peaking in the 1850s, advocated restrictions and fueled anti-immigrant violence in cities like Philadelphia (1844 riots). Despite tensions, empirical evidence indicates immigrants boosted long-term economic growth, with studies showing positive effects on local incomes and urbanization from 1860–1920 inflows. The shift toward southern and eastern European sources intensified after 1880, setting the stage for early 20th-century debates, but northern Europeans dominated the century's total.

### Early 20th-Century Restrictions and Quotas

The Immigration Act of 1917 introduced the first federal literacy requirement, mandating that immigrants over age 16 demonstrate the ability to read in any language, alongside raising the head tax to $8 and expanding grounds for exclusion such as polygamists and those with certain diseases. It also established the "Asiatic Barred Zone," prohibiting immigration from most of Asia south of the 60th parallel north, excluding Japan, the Philippines, and certain Pacific islands, reflecting escalating restrictions on non-European sources amid concerns over labor competition and cultural integration. These measures built on earlier precedents like the 1907 Gentlemen's Agreement, an informal U.S.-Japan pact where Japan pledged to deny passports to laborers intending to emigrate to the continental U.S., in exchange for ending San Francisco's segregation of Japanese schoolchildren, thereby curbing Japanese influx without formal legislation.

Post-World War I anxieties over economic strain, urban overcrowding, and radical ideologies fueled the Emergency Quota Act of May 19, 1921, imposing the nation's first numerical caps at 3% of each nationality's U.S. population per the 1910 census, allowing roughly 350,000 immigrants annually but prioritizing northwestern Europeans. This temporary law responded to a 1920-1921 surge exceeding 800,000 arrivals, aiming to preserve wage levels for native workers and mitigate perceived threats from unassimilated groups linked to anarchism and Bolshevism during the Red Scare.

The Immigration Act of 1924, signed by President Calvin Coolidge on May 26, formalized and tightened these restrictions through a "national origins" quota system, limiting total admissions to 164,667 annually—about 2% of each nationality's count from the 1890 census—explicitly favoring British, German, and Irish origins while capping southern and eastern Europeans at minimal levels, such as 5,802 for Italians and 2,874 for Poles. It extended the Asian exclusion by revoking naturalization eligibility for Japanese and other Asians, reducing overall immigration by over 80% from pre-war peaks and stabilizing the foreign-born share of the population at around 11-12% through the 1930s.

### Post-World War II to 1965 Reforms

Following World War II, U.S. immigration policy continued to operate under the restrictive National Origins Formula established by the Immigration Act of 1924, which capped annual admissions at approximately 150,000 visas and allocated quotas based on the national origins of the U.S. population in 1920, heavily favoring immigrants from Northern and Western Europe while severely limiting those from Southern and Eastern Europe, Asia, and Africa. This system resulted in average annual immigration of about 250,000 persons from 1946 to 1965, with roughly two-thirds originating from Europe, though total inflows remained modest compared to earlier waves due to the quotas and postwar economic recovery in Europe reducing emigration pressures.

In response to the humanitarian crisis of displaced persons in Europe, Congress passed the Displaced Persons Act on June 25, 1948, authorizing the admission of up to 205,000 refugees outside the quota system, primarily ethnic Germans, Poles, and other Europeans uprooted by the war, with subsequent amendments in 1950 raising the total to over 400,000 by prioritizing those in displaced persons camps and requiring U.S. sponsorship and medical clearance. The Act's implementation favored certain groups, such as Baltic and Ukrainian refugees, over others, including some Jewish survivors, due to lobbying by agricultural interests seeking labor and Cold War concerns over communist infiltration, admitting about 400,000 individuals by 1952 and marking the first explicit federal refugee policy.

The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952, known as the McCarran-Walter Act, comprehensively revised immigration law while retaining the national origins quota system, setting an overall Eastern Hemisphere cap of 154,000 visas annually and ending the total exclusion of Asian immigrants by granting them a small quota of 100 persons per country, alongside provisions for naturalization regardless of race. President Truman vetoed the bill citing its discriminatory quotas and potential to exclude needed talent amid Cold War competition, but Congress overrode the veto on June 27, 1952; the Act also introduced preferences for skilled workers and family members but maintained ideological exclusions for subversives, reflecting anti-communist priorities. From 1952 to 1965, actual admissions exceeded quota projections, reaching about 3.5 million due to unused quotas reallocated and ad hoc refugee measures like the 1953 Refugee Relief Act admitting 214,000 more Europeans and others.

### Immigration Since 1965

The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965, also known as the Hart-Celler Act, abolished the national origins quota system established in the 1920s, which had favored immigrants from Northern and Western Europe, and replaced it with a preference system emphasizing family reunification and skilled labor. The law set an overall annual cap of 290,000 visas for the Eastern Hemisphere (with a per-country limit of 20,000) and 120,000 for the Western Hemisphere, intending to promote non-discriminatory admissions while maintaining modest levels similar to the pre-1965 era of about 250,000-300,000 immigrants per year. However, the family-based preferences enabled chain migration, where new immigrants could sponsor relatives, leading to exponential growth beyond initial projections.

This shift fundamentally altered immigrant origins, reducing the European and Canadian share from 84% in 1960 to 68% by 1970 and 42% by 1980, while Latin America rose to 49% and Asia to 27% of inflows in the post-1965 wave, with Mexico becoming the dominant source country. U.S. Census Bureau and Pew Research Center data illustrate the evolving composition of the foreign-born stock: in 1960, 9.7 million (5.4% of the population), with ~75-85% from Europe/Canada, ~10-15% from Latin America/Caribbean, and <5% from Asia; by 1970, 9.6 million (4.7%), ~70% Europe/Canada, ~20% Latin America/Caribbean, ~10% Asia; 1980, 14.1 million (6.2%), ~50% Europe/Canada, ~35% Latin America/Caribbean, ~15% Asia; 1990, 19.8 million (7.9%), ~35% Europe/Canada, ~45% Latin America/Caribbean, ~20% Asia; 2000, 31.1 million (11.1%), ~25% Europe/Canada, ~50% Latin America/Caribbean, ~25% Asia; 2010, 40.0 million (12.9%), ~20% Europe/Canada, ~55% Latin America/Caribbean, ~25% Asia; and by 2020/2022, ~46 million (13.8-14%), ~15-20% Europe/Canada, ~50% Latin America/Caribbean, ~30% Asia. These changes, driven by the family-based system's self-reinforcing chain migration, contributed to the Hispanic share of the U.S. population rising from ~4% in 1965 to 19% in 2020 and the Asian share from <1% to ~6-7%. The foreign-born population, which stood at 4.7% of the U.S. total (9.6 million) in 1970, climbed to 6.2% (14.1 million) by 1980, 8.0% (18.9 million) by 1990, 11.1% (31.1 million) by 2000, and reached 14.3% (47.8 million) by 2023, driven primarily by sustained legal admissions averaging over 1 million annually in recent decades alongside unauthorized entries.

Legal permanent resident admissions expanded through subsequent reforms, including the Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986, which legalized about 3 million unauthorized immigrants while introducing employer sanctions and border enhancements that proved unevenly enforced. The Immigration Act of 1990 raised the annual cap to 700,000, adding diversity visas and expanding employment-based categories, further boosting family-sponsored entries that comprised 65-70% of green cards in most years. Unauthorized immigration surged post-1965, partly due to the termination of the Bracero Program (which had managed seasonal Mexican labor) and pull factors from family networks; estimates indicate growth from negligible levels in the early 1970s to 3.5 million by 1990, peaking at around 12 million in 2007 before stabilizing near 11 million through 2021, then rising to 14 million by 2023 amid increased border encounters from diverse regions including Venezuela, India, and China.

## Legal and Policy Framework

### Constitutional and Foundational Principles

The U.S. Constitution grants Congress the explicit authority under Article I, Section 8, Clause 4 to "establish an uniform Rule of Naturalization" throughout the United States, centralizing the process of conferring citizenship on non-citizens and ensuring consistency across states. This clause addresses citizenship acquisition but does not directly enumerate powers over immigration entry or exclusion, which the Supreme Court has interpreted as an inherent federal prerogative derived from national sovereignty, foreign affairs authority, and implied powers under the Necessary and Proper Clause.

The plenary power doctrine, affirmed by the Supreme Court since the late 19th century, holds that Congress possesses broad, largely unreviewable authority to regulate the admission, exclusion, and removal of non-citizens, particularly at the border, as an exercise of sovereign control over external relations. This doctrine distinguishes immigration from domestic matters, limiting judicial scrutiny to procedural due process for those already within U.S. territory while deferring to political branches on substantive policy. Early exercises of this power, such as the Naturalization Act of 1790, implemented the constitutional mandate by restricting eligibility to "free white person[s]" of good character who had resided in the U.S. for two years and declared intent to become citizens, reflecting an initial emphasis on cultural and moral compatibility for integration into the republic.

Foundational principles articulated by the Framers underscored immigration's role in bolstering population and economic vitality while prioritizing assimilation to preserve republican virtues and social cohesion. Thomas Jefferson viewed immigrants as a source of national strength and a refuge from tyranny, yet advocated for their adoption of American principles to avoid importing monarchical habits that could undermine self-government. George Washington and others similarly encouraged settlement to populate the frontier but insisted on oaths of allegiance and moral qualifications, implying a sovereign right—and obligation—to restrict inflows that threatened cultural unity or public order. These views informed early policies favoring European migrants presumed assimilable, establishing precedents for selective admission over unrestricted entry.

### Major Legislation and Reforms

The Naturalization Act of 1790 established the first uniform federal rule for granting U.S. citizenship, limiting eligibility to "free white persons" of good character who had resided continuously in the United States for two years, renounced foreign allegiances, and demonstrated attachment to the Constitution. This law reflected early republican priorities of assimilating European settlers while excluding non-whites, slaves, and indentured servants from naturalization.

Subsequent 19th-century laws introduced restrictions on entry rather than just naturalization. The Immigration Act of 1882 imposed a 50-cent head tax on immigrants and barred entry to those deemed "idiots, lunatics, convicts, or persons likely to become a public charge," marking the federal government's initial role in screening arrivals for economic self-sufficiency. The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 prohibited Chinese laborers from immigrating, the first law to target a specific nationality, driven by labor competition concerns on the West Coast amid economic downturns. The Immigration Act of 1891 created the Office of the Superintendent of Immigration under the Treasury Department, formalizing federal oversight of admissions and deportations, and expanded exclusions to include polygamists and those with contagious diseases.

Early 20th-century reforms shifted toward numerical limits based on national origins. The Emergency Quota Act of 1921 capped annual immigration at 3% of each nationality's U.S. population per the 1910 census, reducing total entries from over 800,000 in 1920 to about 350,000, primarily to curb post-World War I inflows from Southern and Eastern Europe. The Immigration Act of 1924 (Johnson-Reed Act) tightened this to 2% of the 1890 census population, favoring Northern and Western Europeans, and established the U.S. Border Patrol while banning most Asian immigration via the "Asiatic Barred Zone." These quotas reflected eugenics-influenced concerns over cultural and genetic dilution, reducing immigration to under 150,000 annually by the 1930s.

### Visa Categories and Admission Processes

The United States distinguishes between immigrant visas, which permit permanent residency (lawful permanent resident status, or LPR), and nonimmigrant visas, which allow temporary stays for purposes such as tourism, study, or temporary work. Immigrant visas are numerically limited under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), with annual caps set by Congress, subject to per-country limits of 7% of the total family-sponsored and employment-based visas combined. These categories prioritize family reunification, economic contributions, humanitarian needs, and diversity, but backlogs often exceed available visas, particularly for high-demand countries like Mexico, India, China, and the Philippines.

Family-based immigrant visas constitute the largest share of admissions, accounting for over 60% of new LPRs in recent fiscal years. Immediate relatives of U.S. citizens—spouses, unmarried children under 21, and parents of citizens aged 21 or older—face no numerical cap, allowing unlimited issuance based on approved petitions. Family preference categories, capped at 226,000 visas annually, include: F1 for unmarried sons and daughters (21 or older) of U.S. citizens (23,400 visas); F2A for spouses and unmarried children under 21 of lawful permanent residents; F2B for unmarried sons and daughters (21 or older) of lawful permanent residents (114,200 combined for F2); F3 for married sons and daughters of U.S. citizens (23,400 visas); and F4 for siblings of U.S. citizens and their minor children (65,000 visas). These require a U.S. citizen or LPR sponsor to file Form I-130, with priority dates determining visa availability amid demand-driven waits often exceeding a decade for F4 from certain countries.

Employment-based visas, limited to 140,000 annually (plus unused family preference visas that may spill over), target skilled labor and investment. EB-1 covers priority workers with extraordinary ability in sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; outstanding professors or researchers; and multinational executives or managers (approximately 40,000 visas, no labor certification required). EB-2 applies to professionals with advanced degrees or exceptional ability, often requiring a job offer and labor certification unless waived for national interest; EB-3 includes skilled workers (two years training), professionals (bachelor's degree), and other workers (less than two years training), with the latter capped at 10,000. EB-4 addresses special immigrants such as religious workers, certain international organization employees, and U.S. government retirees; EB-5 requires foreign investors to commit at least $800,000 in targeted employment areas or $1.05 million standard, creating 10 full-time U.S. jobs (set-asides for rural/high-unemployment areas). Sponsors file Form I-140, with labor certification from the Department of Labor typically needed for EB-2 and EB-3 to verify no qualified U.S. workers are available.

### Enforcement Mechanisms and Agencies

U.S. immigration enforcement is primarily coordinated by the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), which oversees operations to prevent unauthorized entry, apprehend violators, and execute removals. Key mechanisms include border patrols, port inspections, interior arrests, detention facilities, and removal proceedings, with authority derived from statutes like the Immigration and Nationality Act.

U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP), established in 2003, enforces immigration laws at and between the 328 official ports of entry, managing over 7,000 miles of land borders and coastal approaches. Its Office of Field Operations (OFO) conducts inspections at ports, screening travelers and cargo using biometric systems, radiation detectors, and risk-based targeting to identify inadmissible aliens. The U.S. Border Patrol (USBP), a CBP component with approximately 19,500 agents as of fiscal year (FY) 2024, patrols between ports, employing vehicle barriers, sensors, cameras, and aerial surveillance to detect and apprehend crossers. In FY 2024, USBP recorded nationwide encounters totaling about 2.5 million, including apprehensions between ports that decreased 25% from prior years due to enhanced deterrence measures.

U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE), also under DHS since 2003, focuses on interior enforcement through its Enforcement and Removal Operations (ERO) directorate, which identifies, arrests, detains, and removes noncitizens subject to deportation. ERO operates 25 field offices and manages a network of over 40 detention centers with a daily population averaging 30,000-40,000 detainees, prioritizing threats like criminals and recent border crossers via the DHS enforcement pyramid. In FY 2024, ICE conducted 113,431 administrative arrests and removed 271,484 noncitizens, contributing to DHS's total of roughly 700,000 removals and returns—the highest since 2010. ICE's Homeland Security Investigations (HSI) complements this by targeting transnational crimes linked to immigration violations, such as smuggling and document fraud.

## Contemporary Immigration Patterns

Global surveys, such as those conducted by Gallup, indicate that the United States is the top desired immigration destination, with approximately 170 million adults worldwide naming it as their preferred future residence—far exceeding aspirations for any individual European country—due to perceptions of high-salary opportunities and the American Dream. However, Europe as a whole is also highly attractive, sometimes surpassing the US in appeal for quality of life, safety nets, and welfare benefits.

### Legal Immigration Trends

Legal immigration to the United States encompasses the granting of lawful permanent resident (LPR) status, issuance of nonimmigrant visas for temporary stays, and naturalizations converting LPRs to citizens. Annual LPR admissions have averaged approximately 1 million since the 1990s, with fluctuations tied to economic conditions, policy changes, and global events. In fiscal year (FY) 2023, 1.17 million individuals obtained LPR status, marking a 15.2% increase from the prior year and reflecting post-pandemic recovery in processing. Family-sponsored admissions remain the largest category, comprising about 65% of LPR grants in recent years, followed by employment-based (around 15%), diversity visas (5%), and refugees/asylees (with adjustments).

Nonimmigrant admissions, which include temporary workers, students, and visitors, have exceeded 100 million entries annually in recent pre-COVID years, though these figures count multiple entries by individuals and do not represent unique migrants. Issuances of key categories like H-1B specialty occupation visas and F-1 student visas peaked in the 2010s but showed declines in FY 2024-2025 amid heightened scrutiny and processing delays, with F-1 issuances down 12% in early 2025 compared to 2024.

Naturalization rates have trended upward recently, with 818,500 individuals naturalized in FY 2024, a 12% increase over the 2010-2019 annual average of 730,100. This rise follows a dip during the COVID-19 pandemic, driven by eligible LPRs applying amid policy incentives and backlog reductions, though the median age of new citizens remains around 42 years, with over 37% aged 30-44. Overall, legal immigration inflows contribute significantly to population growth, accounting for nearly all U.S. population increase in recent estimates.

### Unauthorized Immigration and Border Crossings

Unauthorized immigration refers to the presence of foreign nationals in the United States without legal authorization, primarily through unlawful entry across borders or overstaying authorized visas. Estimates of the total unauthorized immigrant population reached 14 million in 2023, marking a record high driven by increased inflows from non-Mexican countries. The Department of Homeland Security (DHS) reported a lower figure of 11 million as of January 2022, reflecting methodological differences in accounting for recent arrivals and departures. Overstays contribute significantly to this population; DHS data indicate approximately 500,000 nonimmigrants overstayed visas in fiscal year (FY) 2024, with an overall overstay rate declining to about 4% from higher pre-pandemic levels. Suspected in-country overstay rates for B-1/B-2 tourist visas were around 2-3% in FY 2023-2024 for most categories, though higher for certain nationalities.

The majority of unauthorized entries occur via unlawful border crossings, predominantly along the 1,954-mile U.S.-Mexico southwest land border, which accounts for over 90% of U.S. Border Patrol (USBP) apprehensions nationwide. Northern and coastal borders see far fewer incidents, with USBP encounters there typically under 10,000 annually. Methods include foot crossings through remote desert or river areas, often facilitated by smuggling networks, and occasional maritime or vehicular attempts. U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) measures activity through "encounters," encompassing USBP apprehensions between ports of entry and Office of Field Operations inadmissibles at ports. In FY 2023, USBP recorded 2,063,692 southwest border encounters, reflecting a surge linked to policy shifts post-Title 42 expulsions. This declined to approximately 1.53 million USBP encounters in FY 2024 amid executive actions restricting asylum claims.

Estimates of "got-aways"—migrants evading detection—add to unauthorized inflows, with CBP internal sources citing over 1.7 million known got-aways at the southwest border in FY 2023. The Congressional Budget Office estimated 860,000 got-aways for that year, based on probabilistic modeling of sensor data and agent observations. These figures represent undetected entries contributing to the unauthorized population, though net migration accounts for outflows via voluntary departure or removal. Enforcement outcomes vary: pre-2023, many encounters resulted in releases pending hearings under catch-and-release policies; Title 42 enabled rapid expulsions until its end in May 2023.

### Recent Developments (2016–2025)

During the Trump administration's first term (2017–2021), immigration enforcement intensified through executive actions, including the expansion of expedited removal, implementation of the Migrant Protection Protocols (MPP, or "Remain in Mexico") in January 2019 requiring asylum seekers to await hearings in Mexico, and construction of approximately 450 miles of border barriers. These measures contributed to a decline in southwest border apprehensions from 408,870 in fiscal year (FY) 2016 to 303,916 in FY2017, with further reductions amid Title 42 public health expulsions starting March 2020, which expelled over 1.8 million migrants by the policy's end in May 2023. Refugee admissions fell to historic lows, averaging under 20,000 annually compared to over 70,000 pre-2017, and travel restrictions targeted nationals from several Muslim-majority countries, later upheld in modified form by the Supreme Court.

The Biden administration (2021–2025) reversed many prior policies, terminating MPP in June 2021, suspending border wall construction, and expanding humanitarian parole for over 1 million migrants from countries including Cuba, Haiti, Nicaragua, and Venezuela (CHNV program, launched January 2023), alongside programs for Afghans, Ukrainians, and others. Southwest border encounters surged, reaching 1.7 million in FY2021, 2.2 million in FY2022, 2.0 million in FY2023, and 2.9 million in FY2024, driven by releases into the U.S. interior via notices to appear or parole rather than immediate returns. An executive order in June 2024 restricted asylum processing when daily encounters averaged over 2,500, but encounters remained elevated until late 2024.

Following Donald Trump's reelection and inauguration in January 2025, the second term prioritized rapid enforcement, reinstating MPP on day one, pausing asylum applications at the southern border, and suspending the U.S. refugee program temporarily. The Laken Riley Act, signed January 29, 2025, mandated detention of certain noncitizens charged with theft-related offenses, aiming to facilitate deportations. Encounters plummeted, with southwest border apprehensions at 8,347 in February 2025, total encounters at 25,243 in June 2025 (the lowest monthly figure in CBP history), and 4,600 in July 2025—a 91.8% drop from July 2024—attributed to policy signals and Mexican cooperation on interdictions. Interior enforcement expanded, targeting over 1 million deportations annually, though logistical constraints like detention capacity limited scale initially. These enforcement measures contributed to negative net migration in 2025, the first such occurrence in at least 50 years, with Brookings Institution estimates indicating net outflows ranging from 10,000 to 295,000 people, driven by a steep decline in new arrivals and increases in removals and voluntary departures.

### Primary Source Countries and Destinations

In 2023, Mexico remained the leading source country for the U.S. foreign-born population, with 10.9 million Mexican-born immigrants representing 23% of the total 47.8 million immigrants. India followed with 2.9 million (6%), China with 2.4 million (5%), and the Philippines, El Salvador, Cuba, Vietnam, Dominican Republic, Guatemala, and Colombia each accounting for around 3%. These figures reflect the cumulative stock of immigrants, where Mexico's dominance stems from historical waves of both legal and unauthorized migration peaking in the 1990s and 2000s, though its share has declined from 29% in 2010 due to reduced birth rates, improved Mexican economic conditions, and stricter U.S. enforcement. Immigration from Europe, representing a small share of total U.S. immigration dominated by Asia and Latin America, has experienced stable to slightly increasing permanent flows recently (approximately 61,000 in FY 2021 to 82,000 in FY 2023), partly due to humanitarian admissions from Ukraine; EU countries comprise the majority (often 70–80% in non-crisis years), with EU-specific flows averaging 40,000–60,000 per year, amid a long-term relative decline compared to other regions; inflows are influenced by humanitarian programs (e.g., Uniting for Ukraine for displaced persons) and adjustments from temporary visas to permanent status, with non-EU European countries (e.g., United Kingdom, Ukraine, Russia) contributing significantly to broader European totals.

For new legal permanent residents (LPRs) in fiscal year 2023, Mexico accounted for 15% of the 1.2 million approvals, followed by Cuba and India at 7% each, the Dominican Republic at 6%, and China at 5%; the top 10 countries comprised 54% of total LPRs. This flow highlights a divergence: family-based and diversity visa pathways favor Latin American countries like Mexico and Cuba, while employment-based visas drive inflows from India (often in tech sectors) and China. Unauthorized immigration, estimated at 14 million in 2023—a record high—continues to draw primarily from Mexico, Guatemala, Honduras, and El Salvador, though Venezuela's unauthorized population surged to 650,000 amid political instability and U.S. policy expansions like humanitarian parole programs admitting over 500,000 Venezuelans since 2022.

## Demographic Profile

### Scale and Growth of Immigrant Population

The foreign-born population in the United States, encompassing naturalized citizens, lawful permanent residents, temporary visa holders, and unauthorized immigrants, numbered 53.3 million as of January 2025, representing 15.8 percent of the total U.S. population of approximately 337 million. Of these, approximately 37.9 million had legal status, excluding unauthorized immigrants. This marked the largest absolute number and one of the highest shares in U.S. history, surpassing previous peaks from the early 20th century when the foreign-born share reached about 15 percent around 1910. The Current Population Survey data for January 2025 indicated this figure, reflecting adjustments for unauthorized residents.

Historically, the immigrant population grew modestly from 1850, when it comprised about 10 percent of the populace, to a peak of 13.6 percent (10.3 million) in 1900 amid waves of European migration. The share then declined sharply due to restrictive quotas in the 1920s and the Great Depression, bottoming out at 4.7 percent (9.6 million) in 1970 following the 1924 Immigration Act's national origins formula. The 1965 Immigration and Nationality Act abolished these quotas, initiating steady growth: from 11.1 percent (31.1 million) in 2000 to 12.9 percent (40 million) in 2010, and accelerating to 13.5 percent (44.8 million) by 2020.

Recent decades have seen the fastest expansion since the early 1900s, driven by family reunification, employment-based visas, humanitarian admissions, and unauthorized entries. Immigration has been the primary driver of U.S. population growth since the end of the Cold War, contributing over 70 percent in recent decades. Between 2022 and 2023, the foreign-born population increased by 1.6 million, a 3.6 percent annual growth rate—the largest since at least 2000. Net international migration contributed 2.8 million to population growth in fiscal year 2023-2024, accounting for 84 percent of the overall U.S. population increase of 3.3 million. This surge elevated the foreign-born share to near-historic highs, though preliminary estimates indicate a decline of over 1 million by June 2025—the first since the 1960s—potentially due to increased deportations, voluntary returns, and reduced border crossings following policy changes.

### Origins, Age, Education, and Skills Composition

The regional origins of the foreign-born population have shifted markedly since 1960, when Europe accounted for 74%, Latin America 9%, and Asia 5% of the immigrant stock; by 2010, these figures had changed to 12% Europe, 53% Latin America, and 28% Asia, driven by post-1965 policy reforms favoring family reunification and diversity over national-origin quotas. As of January 2025, the foreign-born population in the United States, totaling approximately 53.3 million individuals, originates predominantly from Latin America and Asia. Mexico remains the leading source country, accounting for 22% of immigrants or about 11 million people, followed by India (6%, or 3.2 million), China (6%, or 3 million), the Philippines (4%, or 2.1 million), and Cuba (3%, or 1.7 million). Overall, Latin American countries contribute 52% of the immigrant stock (26.7 million), while Asian origins make up 27% (14 million); these patterns reflect historical migration waves from Mexico and Central America alongside recent increases in high-skilled inflows from India and China via employment-based visas.

The age distribution of the foreign-born population skews toward working-age adults, with 77.1% aged 16 to 64 in recent estimates, compared to 60.9% among the native-born. The median age for foreign-born individuals stands at 46.1 years, higher than the native-born median of approximately 37 years, largely due to the accumulation of earlier cohorts aging in place offset by younger recent arrivals. This structure supports labor force participation but contributes to a relatively older overall profile as integration progresses.

Educational attainment among immigrants aged 25 and older reveals a bimodal distribution: 24% lack a high school diploma, exceeding the 7% rate among U.S.-born adults, while 36% hold a bachelor's degree or higher, matching the native rate. Recent arrivals since 2010 show elevated qualifications, with 45.2% possessing a bachelor's or advanced degree versus 38% of natives, driven by selective visa categories favoring skilled workers from Asia. However, the overall immigrant profile includes substantial low-education segments from Latin America, where Central American immigrants attain bachelor's degrees at only 11%.

### Second-Generation Outcomes and Assimilation Metrics

Second-generation immigrants, defined as U.S.-born children of foreign-born parents, demonstrate substantial assimilation across key metrics, including language proficiency, educational attainment, and economic mobility, though outcomes vary significantly by parental origin and selectivity. By the second generation, English language acquisition is nearly universal, with over 90% reporting fluency as their primary language, facilitating integration into broader societal structures. This linguistic shift correlates with declining ethnic identification; for instance, among those of Hispanic origin, only 55% of the second generation self-identify primarily as Latino, compared to higher rates among first-generation immigrants. The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine's 2015 comprehensive review of post-1965 immigration found that second-generation individuals generally attain educational levels and earnings comparable to or exceeding those of native-born Americans, with convergence occurring within two generations for most cohorts. However, this pattern holds more robustly for offspring of higher-skilled or European/Asian-origin immigrants, while those from less selective Latin American streams exhibit persistent gaps, attributable to factors like parental human capital and cultural transmission rather than discrimination alone.

Educational outcomes illustrate both progress and disparities. Second-generation immigrants enroll in higher education at rates surpassing their parents—32% of immigrant-origin students comprised U.S. college enrollees in 2022, up from 20% in 2000—and achieve higher completion rates than first-generation cohorts, often matching or exceeding natives in STEM fields for Asian-origin groups. A 2023 analysis of longitudinal earnings data revealed that second-generation men from diverse origins experience occupational advancement akin to natives, with median household incomes for second-generation adults reaching approximately $70,000 in recent Census estimates, surpassing first-generation figures by 20-30%. Yet, children of immigrants face elevated low-income risks (42% vs. 33% for native children), particularly in employment quality, where second-generation workers from certain origins remain overrepresented in elementary occupations due to network effects and skill mismatches. These variances underscore causal influences like parental arrival age and origin-country human capital, which predict second-generation performance more than U.S.-specific policies.

Intermarriage rates serve as a proxy for cultural assimilation, with second-generation individuals exhibiting markedly higher out-group unions than their parents—26% for Hispanics and 23% for Asians in recent Pew analyses, compared to under 10% for first-generation counterparts. Over half of second-generation immigrants marry outside their parental ethnic group, accelerating identity convergence toward American norms. A 2024 empirical study spanning multiple cohorts confirmed that second-generation descendants contribute to economic assimilation by adopting native-like labor participation and innovation patterns, though full parity lags for groups with lower parental education. Challenges persist in segmented assimilation, where second-generation youth from high-poverty, low-skill immigrant backgrounds face elevated risks of underachievement, as evidenced by persistent poverty correlations in 2020s data. Overall, while aggregate metrics indicate successful integration for many, origin-specific cultural and selection effects drive heterogeneous trajectories, with empirical evidence favoring policies enhancing parental skills for optimal second-generation results.

## Economic Effects

### Contributions to Growth, Innovation, and Labor Markets

Immigrants have made disproportionate contributions to U.S. innovation, particularly through high-skilled channels such as H-1B visas. Empirical analysis indicates that immigrants, comprising about 16% of U.S.-based inventors from 1990 to 2016, authored 23% of patents during that period, accounting for 32% of aggregate innovative output when adjusted for productivity spillovers. High-skilled immigrants enhance firm-level innovation; for instance, firms receiving H-1B workers experience faster growth and higher patenting rates, with a 10% increase in H-1B share linked to a 2% rise in product reallocation as a measure of creative destruction. These effects stem from immigrants' overrepresentation in STEM fields, where they collaborate with natives to generate novel ideas, though benefits are concentrated in tech hubs like Silicon Valley.

In entrepreneurship, immigrants drive business formation at rates exceeding their population share. As of 2024, immigrants founded over 20% of new U.S. businesses despite representing 14-15% of the population, with their firms contributing to job creation in high-growth sectors. Immigrants accounted for 24% of entrepreneurs in 2019, up from 19% in 2007, and are linked to more than 40% of Fortune 500 companies through founding or leadership roles. Native-immigrant collaborative firms outperform immigrant-only ones, securing 117% more patents, highlighting synergies that amplify economic dynamism.

Immigration supports overall economic growth by expanding the labor force and countering demographic stagnation. Post-pandemic surges in immigration from 2021-2024 boosted population growth, thereby increasing GDP through higher labor supply and consumption, with estimates suggesting unauthorized inflows alone added measurable output before recent declines. Meta-analyses confirm positive productivity effects via workforce rejuvenation and specialization, where immigrants enable natives to shift toward higher-skill tasks. However, these gains vary by skill level; high-skilled inflows yield stronger long-term growth than low-skilled, which primarily fill immediate shortages without equivalent innovation spillovers.

### Wage, Employment, and Fiscal Impacts on Natives

Empirical research on the wage effects of immigration on native-born U.S. workers reveals conflicting findings, largely attributable to methodological differences in defining labor market competition. Studies employing a national skill-based approach, which aggregates workers by education and experience levels, indicate that increases in low-skilled immigrant labor supply depress wages for comparable native workers, particularly mass low-skilled unauthorized immigration that suppresses wages for working-class Americans in construction, service jobs, and manual labor, with studies estimating drops of 3-10% for high school dropouts competing directly; while the overall economy may gain from cheap labor benefiting corporations, costs hit the bottom rung hardest, widening inequality. Economist George Borjas estimates that a 10 percentage point rise in the immigrant share of the labor force reduces wages for low-skilled natives by 3 to 4 percent, with effects concentrated among high school dropouts experiencing declines of up to 9 percent over the 1980–2000 period. In contrast, spatial econometric studies examining local labor markets often report negligible or positive wage impacts, as immigrants may complement natives through specialization or stimulate demand; however, these approaches have been critiqued for underestimating effects due to native internal migration in response to inflows. A 2025 meta-analysis of 88 studies underscores the ongoing contention, finding average short-term wage reductions for natives but heterogeneity by skill level and immigrant type, with low-skilled natives facing the most consistent downward pressure.

On employment, the preponderance of evidence suggests immigration does not systematically displace native workers at the aggregate level. National-level analyses show no significant correlation between immigrant inflows and native unemployment rates, as labor markets adjust through occupational shifts and economic expansion. Longitudinal data from 2000–2023 indicate that recent immigration has coincided with rising native-born employment, particularly in high-skill sectors, with immigrants often filling roles that enable natives to move into supervisory or complementary positions. Short-term localized displacement may occur among low-skilled natives during rapid surges, such as the post-2021 border encounters, but these effects dissipate as markets equilibrate, and overall native labor force participation has not declined in response to immigration.

Fiscal impacts on natives vary markedly by immigrant education, legal status, and generational accounting. The 2017 National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine report concludes that first-generation immigrants impose a net present-value fiscal cost of approximately $279,000 per low-skilled arrival (excluding descendants), driven by higher utilization of education, Medicaid, and welfare programs relative to tax contributions, though high-skilled immigrants yield a surplus of $259,000. Including U.S.-born children shifts the balance toward neutrality or slight positivity over 75 years, but state and local governments bear disproportionate burdens from K-12 schooling and means-tested benefits, estimated at $1,600–$2,000 annual net cost per low-skilled immigrant household. Recent analyses confirm these patterns: a 2025 Manhattan Institute update finds legal immigrants reduce the federal deficit by $68,000 lifetime on average, but unauthorized immigrants increase it by $116,000, factoring in remittances and enforcement costs; low-skilled legal arrivals remain net drains absent high economic mobility. The 2021–2024 immigration surge added $9.2 billion in direct state-local costs in 2023 alone, net of federal aid, primarily from shelter and education expenditures. These costs translate to higher taxes or reduced services for natives, particularly in high-immigration states like California and Texas.

### Long-Term Net Benefits vs. Short-Term Costs

The economic impacts of immigration to the United States exhibit a pattern where short-term costs, particularly fiscal and labor market strains, often outweigh immediate benefits, while long-term gains emerge through innovation, demographic vitality, and intergenerational contributions, contingent heavily on immigrants' skill levels. A 2017 analysis by the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine found that first-generation immigrants and their dependents generated an average annual net fiscal deficit of $57.4 billion from 2011 to 2013, driven by greater consumption of education, welfare, and healthcare services relative to tax payments, with low-skilled immigrants imposing the highest burdens. Harvard economist George Borjas estimates that influxes of low-skilled immigrants depress wages for comparable native-born workers by 3 to 5 percent, as increased labor supply competes directly in sectors like construction and agriculture, exacerbating short-term employment displacement for high school dropouts. These costs are amplified for unauthorized immigrants, who contribute limited taxes while accessing emergency services and education for U.S.-born children, leading to localized strains on state and local budgets in high-immigration areas.

In contrast, long-term benefits accrue from high-skilled immigration and the assimilation of subsequent generations, fostering productivity gains and fiscal surpluses over decades. The Congressional Budget Office projected in 2024 that the 2021–2026 immigration surge would increase federal revenues by $0.9 trillion over the 2024–2034 period through expanded labor force participation and taxable income, while adding $0.7 trillion in mandatory spending and debt interest, yielding a net deficit reduction when accounting for economic growth effects. High-skilled immigrants, such as H-1B visa holders, drive disproportionate innovation: studies indicate they boost firm-level job creation for natives by generating complementary roles and patents, with one additional high-skilled immigrant raising native employment by 2 to 3 workers over five years. Second-generation immigrants, per the National Academies' modeling over 75 years, reverse first-generation deficits, contributing a net present value surplus of up to $259,000 per person for those arriving as adults, through higher earnings and taxes post-assimilation.

The net assessment hinges on composition: low-skilled immigration yields persistent fiscal drags, with a 2025 Manhattan Institute update estimating that immigrants without high school diplomas impose lifetime costs exceeding $300,000 per household due to welfare dependency and low tax bases, whereas college-educated arrivals deliver surpluses of over $500,000. Overall, selective policies favoring skills could enhance long-term GDP growth by 0.5 to 1 percent annually via demographic replenishment and entrepreneurship, but mass low-skilled inflows risk amplifying short-term costs without commensurate offsets, as evidenced by Borjas' findings of sustained wage gaps for natives persisting beyond initial decades. Empirical consensus holds that while immigration expands aggregate output—potentially by 1 to 2 percent of GDP over decades—the per capita benefits for natives remain modest or negative in the near term without reforms prioritizing human capital.
