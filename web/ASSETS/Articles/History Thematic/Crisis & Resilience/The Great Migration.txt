# The Great Migration

## Introduction

The Great Migration refers to the large-scale relocation of approximately six million African Americans from the rural Southern United States to urban centers in the Northeast, Midwest, and West, spanning roughly from 1916 to 1970. This demographic shift reduced the proportion of the black population residing in the South from 89 percent in 1910 to 53 percent by 1970, fundamentally altering the geographic distribution of African Americans across the country.

The migration unfolded in two main waves: the first, from about 1910 to 1940, drew migrants primarily to industrial cities like Chicago, Detroit, and New York amid World War I labor demands and agricultural disruptions such as the boll weevil infestation; the second, from 1940 to 1970, expanded to Western destinations and was accelerated by World War II economic opportunities. Push factors included pervasive racial violence, including thousands of lynchings, and the oppressive Jim Crow system enforcing segregation and economic disenfranchisement in the South, while pull factors encompassed higher wages in Northern factories and relatively greater legal protections against discrimination.

This exodus facilitated urbanization among African Americans, with over 80 percent living in cities by 1970, fostering cultural developments such as the Harlem Renaissance but also contributing to residential segregation, overcrowded housing, and racial tensions in Northern cities that erupted in riots. Economically, migrants often experienced initial gains in income and occupational status compared to Southern counterparts, though competition in labor markets and persistent discrimination limited long-term convergence with white outcomes. The Great Migration's legacy includes a transformed black urban underclass in the North, with downstream effects on family structure, crime rates, and political mobilization, underscoring the interplay of opportunity and entrenched barriers in American racial dynamics.

## Causes and Motivations

### Push Factors in the South

The primary push factors driving African Americans from the South during the Great Migration included pervasive racial violence, legal and social segregation under Jim Crow laws, and economic exploitation through systems like sharecropping and debt peonage. These conditions created an environment of systemic oppression that limited opportunities and threatened lives, prompting mass exodus starting around 1916.

Racial terror, particularly lynchings, instilled widespread fear among black Southerners. Between 1882 and 1968, 3,446 black individuals were lynched in the United States, with the vast majority occurring in the South; by the late 1920s, 95 percent of lynchings took place there. The Equal Justice Initiative documented over 4,000 racial terror lynchings in Southern states from 1877 to 1950, many of which targeted African Americans for perceived economic competition or minor infractions, continuing into the migration era despite a decline from peak years in the 1890s.

Jim Crow laws enforced strict racial segregation and disenfranchisement, denying African Americans access to public facilities, voting rights, and fair legal protections. These state-mandated discriminations, upheld by the Supreme Court in Plessy v. Ferguson (1896), permeated daily life and reinforced white supremacy, making social mobility nearly impossible in the South.

### Pull Factors in the North and West

Labor shortages in northern industries, exacerbated by World War I enlistments and European immigration restrictions, created demand for African American workers in factories, particularly in steel, meatpacking, and automobile manufacturing sectors starting around 1916. Northern employers actively recruited southern migrants through labor agents who traveled to black communities, promising steady employment amid wartime production needs. These opportunities contrasted sharply with southern agricultural labor, drawing over 400,000 African Americans northward by 1920.

Wages in northern urban factories were substantially higher than in the South; for instance, around 1916, a factory wage in the North was typically three times that of black agricultural workers in the South, with daily earnings reaching up to five dollars compared to fifty cents to two dollars in southern fields. By 1940, the average black worker in the North earned nearly three times more than counterparts remaining in the South, incentivizing continued migration. This economic disparity, amplified by letters from early migrants and advertisements in black newspapers, amplified the allure of industrial work over sharecropping or tenant farming.

Perceptions of improved social conditions, including access to education and urban amenities, further pulled migrants, as northern cities offered public schools and cultural institutions unavailable or severely restricted in the rural South under Jim Crow laws. During the Second Great Migration, wartime labor demands extended these pulls to western states like California, where shipbuilding and defense industries provided jobs, though educational gains were often offset by de facto segregation. Overall, these factors resulted in a net exodus of approximately 1.6 million African Americans from the South between 1910 and 1940, with destinations concentrated in cities like Chicago, Detroit, and New York.

## First Great Migration (1910–1940)

### Origins and Initial Waves

The First Great Migration began in earnest around 1916 due to World War I labor demands and encouragement from newspapers like the Chicago Defender. The initial waves were precipitated by World War I's disruption of European immigration to the United States starting in 1914, which generated severe labor shortages in Northern factories amid surging demand for wartime production. Industrial employers in cities like Chicago and Detroit responded by dispatching labor agents southward to recruit African American workers, offering wages far exceeding those in Southern agriculture—often double or more for unskilled labor. This recruitment intensified from 1916 onward, as Northern steel mills, meatpacking plants, and railroads sought to fill vacancies left by the war's impact on immigrant inflows, with an estimated 400,000 to 500,000 African Americans departing the South between 1916 and 1918 alone.

Agricultural crises in the South compounded these pull factors, notably the boll weevil infestation that spread rapidly after 1915, destroying up to 50% of cotton yields in affected regions like Georgia and Alabama and displacing thousands of black sharecroppers dependent on the crop. Combined with chronic low earnings under sharecropping systems—where net income for black farmers averaged under $100 annually in many areas—and episodic racial violence, these conditions prompted rural Southern blacks to seek stability northward. Black-owned newspapers, particularly the Chicago Defender, amplified the movement through editorials and success stories, while chain migration was fueled by letters from early arrivals touting steady paychecks and escape from peonage-like debt traps.

From 1910 to 1920, net out-migration of African Americans from the South reached approximately 454,000, marking the onset of urban demographic shifts as migrants concentrated in industrial hubs: Chicago's black population significantly increased, with at least 50,000 southern migrants arriving between 1916 and 1920—for instance, growing from 44,000 in 1910 to over 109,000 by 1920. These pioneers, predominantly young men and families from the Black Belt states of Mississippi, Alabama, and Georgia, traveled primarily via segregated rail lines, arriving to fill roles in manufacturing that paid $2–$3 daily compared to $0.75–$1 in Southern fields. Though initial flows tapered post-1918 armistice due to returning soldiers and economic slowdowns, the decade established patterns of family reunification and community formation that sustained the migration into the 1920s.

### Tensions, Violence, and Migrant Responses

The influx of African American migrants into northern industrial cities during the 1910s generated acute tensions with white residents, primarily over economic competition and residential encroachment. Black workers, often recruited as strikebreakers during World War I labor shortages, displaced white union members in sectors like meatpacking and steel, fostering resentment amid postwar job scarcity. In Chicago, the black population surged 148% from 1916 to 1919, exacerbating overcrowding and pushing migrants into adjacent white neighborhoods, where white homeowners enforced segregation through restrictive covenants, bombings of black properties, and vigilante actions.

These frictions erupted into widespread violence, culminating in the East St. Louis riots of July 1–3, 1917, where white mobs, enraged by black strikebreaking and demographic shifts, killed at least 39 African Americans, injured hundreds, and destroyed homes and businesses in black districts. The "Red Summer" of 1919 saw similar white-initiated attacks across at least 26 U.S. cities, driven by fears of black economic advancement and postwar social upheaval, resulting in dozens of black deaths nationwide. In Chicago's July 27–August 3 riot, triggered by the drowning of black teenager Eugene Williams after a white beachgoer threw a rock at him, mobs burned black neighborhoods, yielding 38 fatalities (23 black, 15 white), 537 injuries (342 black), and over 1,000 black families homeless.

African American migrants responded with unprecedented armed self-defense, particularly by World War I veterans who rejected passive victimhood. In Chicago, black residents formed armed patrols and snipers fired from windows to protect communities, confronting police and repelling white gangs in street battles. W.E.B. Du Bois, in The Crisis, explicitly endorsed such resistance, arguing that returning soldiers must defend their rights by force if necessary, marking a shift toward assertive "New Negro" militancy. Organizations like the NAACP documented atrocities to galvanize federal attention, while black newspapers urged migrants to arm themselves and remain vigilant, sustaining the flow northward despite northern perils, as southern lynchings persisted at higher rates.

## Second Great Migration (1940s–1970)

### World War II Labor Demands and Expansion

World War II mobilization created acute labor shortages in the United States defense industries, as roughly 16 million Americans entered military service between 1940 and 1945, necessitating the recruitment of additional workers for factories, shipyards, and munitions plants. Northern and Western employers, facing production demands for the war effort, increasingly turned to African Americans in the South to fill these roles, particularly after traditional labor pools were depleted. This demand restarted and intensified the Great Migration, shifting patterns toward urban centers with wartime contracts.

A pivotal development occurred on June 25, 1941, when President Franklin D. Roosevelt issued Executive Order 8802, banning racial discrimination in federal defense jobs and establishing the Fair Employment Practice Committee (FEPC) to oversee compliance. Prompted by threatened marches organized by labor leader A. Philip Randolph, the order opened semi-skilled and skilled positions previously reserved for white workers, enabling African Americans to secure employment in auto plants converted to tank production and aircraft factories. Although enforcement was inconsistent due to limited resources and resistance from some employers and unions, the policy reduced formal barriers and signaled expanded opportunities, directly fueling migration by alleviating fears of outright exclusion.

The scale of this expansion was substantial: between 1940 and 1950, approximately 1.5 million African Americans departed the South, with over 1 million migrating northward and westward specifically during the war years to take industrial jobs. This influx included significant movement to Western states like California, where shipbuilding and aviation industries absorbed tens of thousands of Black workers amid labor demands for Pacific theater support. Occupationally, the share of Black men in semi-skilled roles rose from 15% in 1940 to higher levels by war's end, reflecting gains in manufacturing and mechanics tied to migration-driven workforce entry. These shifts not only met immediate wartime needs but also laid the groundwork for sustained demographic changes in receiving cities.

### Postwar Scale and Urban Influx

The Second Great Migration expanded significantly in the postwar era, with migration rates sustaining high levels through the 1950s before tapering in the 1960s. In the 1940s, approximately 1.5 million African Americans left the South, driven by lingering wartime industrial opportunities and postwar economic expansion. From 1940 to 1970 overall, over four million African Americans migrated to non-Southern regions, accounting for roughly 80% of national Black population gains during that period. This outflow was facilitated by mechanization in Southern agriculture, which displaced sharecroppers and tenant farmers, alongside persistent racial violence and legal discrimination.

Postwar migrants overwhelmingly targeted urban industrial centers, concentrating in central cities rather than suburbs or rural areas. The Black population share in Northern and Western cities increased from 4% in 1940 to 16% by 1970, reflecting rapid demographic shifts in metropolises like Chicago, Detroit, New York, and Los Angeles. Western destinations saw a marked upsurge, with 339,000 African Americans relocating there in the 1940s alone, compared to just 49,000 in the prior decade, fueled by defense-related jobs in California and other states. This urban influx strained housing and infrastructure in established Black neighborhoods, exacerbating overcrowding while enabling access to unionized manufacturing and service sector employment.

U.S. Census data underscore the scale of these transformations, showing that between 1940 and 1970, the proportion of African Americans residing outside the South rose dramatically, with urban hubs absorbing the bulk of newcomers. By 1970, cities that had previously hosted smaller Black communities experienced population doublings or more, altering electoral politics, labor markets, and social dynamics in the North and West.

## Migration Patterns and Demographics

### Primary Destinations and Concentrations

The primary destinations for African American migrants during the Great Migration were industrial urban centers in the Northeast, Midwest, and emerging Western cities, driven by labor demands in manufacturing, steel, and automotive sectors. Cities such as Chicago, Detroit, New York, Philadelphia, Cleveland, and Pittsburgh absorbed the largest influxes, with over six million individuals relocating from the South between 1910 and 1970. In the First Great Migration (1910–1940), the focus was predominantly on Northeastern and Midwestern hubs, while the Second Great Migration (1940–1970) saw increased movement to Western locales like Los Angeles. These patterns reflected targeted recruitment by Northern industries seeking low-wage labor amid labor shortages. While California's cities, such as Los Angeles and the San Francisco Bay Area, saw the largest Western influxes due to massive World War II shipbuilding, aircraft manufacturing, and defense industries, other Western destinations also attracted significant numbers of African American migrants. In the Mountain West, Denver, Colorado emerged as a notable hub. As a major railroad terminus, Denver drew Black workers from the South through railroad employment opportunities, which persisted into the postwar era alongside some wartime defense industries. Chain migration helped establish and grow vibrant communities, particularly in the Five Points neighborhood—known as the "Harlem of the West"—which featured jazz venues (including the historic Rossonian Hotel), Black-owned businesses, and strong cultural institutions.

Denver stood out relative to other areas in the Rockies and Great Plains, which generally lacked large-scale industrial jobs, major rail hubs, or established Black communities prior to the migration. This resulted in sparser Black populations across much of the interior West, with population maps from the period showing relative concentration in the Denver metro area compared to surrounding regions until the larger West Coast migrations dominated later patterns.

Chicago and Detroit exemplified the scale of urban transformation, with Chicago's African American population rising from about 2 percent of the city's total in 1910 to 33 percent by 1970, accommodating hundreds of thousands of newcomers. Detroit experienced a more than 600 percent increase in its black population between 1910 and 1920 alone, fueled by automotive industry expansion. New York City also saw substantial growth, particularly in Manhattan, where migrants bolstered the black population amid wartime and postwar economic booms; U.S. Census data indicate significant percentage-point rises in black shares across these metros from 1910 to 1940 and beyond. Philadelphia and Cleveland similarly hosted large communities, with black populations expanding rapidly due to steel and manufacturing opportunities.

### Statistical Data and Trends

Between 1910 and 1970, approximately 6 million African Americans migrated from the South to other regions of the United States, marking one of the largest internal demographic shifts in the nation's history. This movement reduced the proportion of the Black population residing in the South from 89% in 1910 to about 53% by 1970.

The first phase of the migration, spanning 1910 to 1940, involved roughly 1.6 million individuals, primarily young adults seeking industrial employment amid World War I labor shortages. By 1940, the Black population in Southern states had declined noticeably, with net out-migration contributing to percentage-point increases in Black shares of major Northern and Midwestern cities, such as Detroit (from 1.4% to 9.2%) and Chicago (from 2.0% to 8.2%).

The second phase, from 1940 to 1970, accelerated dramatically, with an estimated 5 million migrants, driven by World War II and postwar economic expansion; this period accounted for about 80% of the total Black population gains outside the South. Urbanization intensified, as over 80% of African Americans lived in cities by 1970, up from rural dominance earlier in the century.

## Economic Outcomes

### Gains in Wages and Employment Opportunities

Migrants from the American South to Northern and Midwestern cities during the Great Migration experienced marked wage premiums relative to those who remained in the South, driven primarily by the shift from low-productivity agriculture to higher-paying industrial and manufacturing roles. In 1940, for instance, the average Black worker in the North earned nearly three times the wage of their counterparts in the South, reflecting the pull of labor demand in expanding urban economies.

Empirical analyses using matched census records confirm substantial economic returns for migrants, with gains estimated at 60 to 70 log points in log wages after accounting for positive selection on observables like literacy and prior occupation. These returns contributed to narrowing the Black-White income gap, as migration enabled access to mechanized industries that rewarded skills more efficiently than Southern sharecropping or tenant farming systems. Such gains were particularly pronounced in the First Great Migration (1910–1940), when World War I labor shortages prompted Northern firms to recruit Southern Black workers for semi-skilled positions previously dominated by European immigrants.

Employment opportunities expanded beyond agriculture, with Black migrants increasingly entering manufacturing, transportation, and service sectors in destination cities like Chicago and Detroit. By 1940, census data indicated that Northern Black men were overrepresented in industrial occupations compared to Southern Blacks, who remained tied to farm labor yielding annual earnings often below $300 in the 1910s. The Second Great Migration (1940–1970) amplified these trends, as wartime production under Executive Order 8802 opened defense-related jobs, boosting Black employment in unionized factories and yielding real wage growth even amid postwar economic adjustments. Overall, these shifts elevated occupational status and lifetime earnings for migrants, though returns varied by cohort and destination due to local labor market conditions.

### Persistent Challenges and Opportunity Gaps

Despite initial wage improvements from southern agricultural labor to northern industrial jobs, African American migrants encountered persistent racial wage disparities in northern cities, where the black-white wage gap narrowed only modestly by 15 log points between 1940 and 1970. This gap was exacerbated by labor market competition, particularly from European immigrants, which disproportionately lowered wages for less-skilled black workers compared to whites with similar education and experience. Black unemployment rates consistently exceeded those of whites, reaching more than twice the white rate for males in the early 1950s and maintaining a ratio often above 2:1 through the 1970s, reflecting barriers to stable employment in unionized and skilled sectors.

Housing segregation, intensified by restrictive covenants and redlining during the migration era, confined migrants to overcrowded urban enclaves, inflating rents by up to 30-50% above comparable white areas and eroding opportunities for homeownership and wealth accumulation. These patterns limited economic mobility, as segregated neighborhoods correlated with reduced intergenerational income gains, with one standard deviation increase in segregation linked to about 5% lower adult incomes for black residents. Postwar public housing projects, intended as solutions, often perpetuated isolation in high-poverty areas, hindering access to quality jobs and networks.

Educational opportunities improved marginally, with black children of migrants gaining approximately 0.8 years of schooling on average by relocating northward as of 1940, and high school enrollment rising from under 50% in 1950 to nearly two-thirds by 1970. However, persistent gaps in attainment relative to whites—stemming from underfunded segregated schools and family disruptions—constrained career advancement, maintaining racial disparities in occupational mobility even after escaping southern Jim Crow. These challenges contributed to intergenerational opportunity gaps, where subsequent black cohorts in migration destinations experienced diminished economic prospects compared to earlier migrants.
