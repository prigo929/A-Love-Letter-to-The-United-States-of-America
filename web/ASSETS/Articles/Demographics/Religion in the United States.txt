# Religion in the United States

## Introduction

Religion in the United States encompasses a broad spectrum of beliefs and practices safeguarded by the First Amendment, which prohibits government establishment of religion and protects free exercise thereof, fostering exceptional religious pluralism compared to many nations. As of 2023-2024, 62% of U.S. adults identify as Christians—40% Protestant, 19% Catholic, and 3% other Christian denominations—while 29% report no religious affiliation, with Judaism at 2% and Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, and other faiths each comprising about 1%. This diversity stems from colonial Protestant roots, waves of immigration, and constitutional disestablishment, enabling the growth of non-Christian communities and secularism.

Historically, religion has profoundly shaped American society through events like the First Great Awakening in the 1730s–1740s, a revival movement that emphasized personal conversion and challenged established clergy, promoting evangelicalism and indirectly influencing revolutionary ideals of individual liberty. Subsequent awakenings in the 19th and 20th centuries spurred social reforms, missionary expansion, and denominational proliferation, while the absence of a national church allowed for competition among sects, contributing to high voluntary adherence rates. The U.S. maintains greater religious participation than other industrialized countries, with 25% of U.S. adults attending religious services in person at least once a week, though absolute church membership has declined amid broader cultural shifts.

Key characteristics include religion's intersection with politics and culture, evident in evangelical influence on policy debates over life issues, education, and family structure, alongside ongoing tensions between free exercise claims and state interests in areas like public accommodations and healthcare mandates. Christian affiliation has fallen from over 90% in the early 1990s to current levels, stabilizing post-2019, reflecting causal factors such as generational change, scandals, and rising skepticism rather than mere demographic replacement. Despite secular trends, recent surveys indicate growing perceptions of religion's societal influence, underscoring its enduring, if contested, role in national identity.

## Historical Foundations

### Colonial Era: Religious Diversity Amid Protestant Dominance

The thirteen British colonies in North America, established between 1607 and 1732, were overwhelmingly populated by Protestant settlers fleeing religious persecution or seeking economic opportunity, with Protestantism forming the cultural and institutional backbone of colonial society. By 1776, recorded congregations numbered around 3,228, dominated by larger Protestant denominations such as Congregationalists, Anglicans, Presbyterians, and Baptists, comprising approximately 98 percent of the Christian population. Eight of the colonies maintained established churches—typically Anglican in the South or Congregationalist in New England—enforcing taxes for church support and often restricting non-adherents from full civic participation.

Regional differences highlighted Protestant dominance while allowing pockets of variation. In New England, Puritan settlers founded Plymouth Colony in 1620 and Massachusetts Bay Colony in 1630, enforcing strict Congregationalist orthodoxy that tolerated little deviation, leading to the banishment of dissenters like Roger Williams and Anne Hutchinson. Southern colonies like Virginia, settled in 1607, established the Anglican Church as the official faith, with laws mandating attendance and suppressing Catholic and Quaker practices. The Middle Colonies exhibited greater pluralism, with Pennsylvania chartered in 1681 as a haven for Quakers under William Penn, alongside Dutch Reformed in New York and diverse German Lutheran and Moravian communities, fostering voluntary associations over state enforcement.

Catholicism represented a notable minority, primarily in Maryland, founded in 1634 by Lord Baltimore as a refuge for English Catholics amid Protestant ascendancy in England. The Maryland Toleration Act of 1649 granted freedom of worship to all Trinitarian Christians, prohibiting persecution for beliefs in Jesus as the son of God, though it punished denial of the Trinity or blasphemy and reflected Protestant influence in the assembly by then. Rhode Island, established in 1636 by exiled Baptist Roger Williams, pioneered broader separation of church and state, welcoming Jews, Quakers, and others, but such experiments were exceptions amid widespread Protestant hegemony. Small Jewish communities, numbering fewer than 2,000 by the 1770s, existed in ports like Newport and New York, engaging in trade without formal establishment.

### Revolutionary and Founding Period: Religion's Role in Independence and Governance

During the American Revolution, Protestant clergy provided significant moral and rhetorical support for independence, framing resistance to British rule as a defense of divinely ordained liberties. Sermons from the pulpits often invoked biblical precedents of covenant-breaking tyrants and the duty to resist unjust authority, with ministers like Jonas Clark at Lexington urging armed defense against British troops on April 19, 1775. The so-called "Black Robe Regiment" referred to these patriot pastors, who not only preached rebellion but in some cases fought in militias, earning British derision for their influence on public opinion. Approximately two-thirds of colonial ministers endorsed the Patriot cause, contributing to the Revolution's ideological foundation by portraying King George III as a violator of natural rights derived from God.

The Declaration of Independence, adopted on July 4, 1776, explicitly rooted American rights in theistic principles, appealing to the "Laws of Nature and of Nature's God" and attributing unalienable rights to their "Creator," while concluding with reliance on "divine Providence" and the judgment of the "Supreme Judge of the world." This language drew from Enlightenment natural law traditions fused with Protestant covenant theology, justifying separation as a response to grievances seen as breaches of moral order. In contrast, the U.S. Constitution of 1787 omitted direct references to God or Christianity, reflecting a deliberate federal neutrality on religion; Article VI prohibited religious tests for office, and the document preserved states' existing religious establishments without imposing a national one.

The Founding Fathers held diverse personal beliefs, with many exhibiting orthodox Christian practices—George Washington served as an Episcopal vestryman and emphasized religion's role in military discipline and civic virtue, while John Adams identified as a churchgoer who viewed Christianity as essential for morality—yet key figures like Thomas Jefferson and Benjamin Franklin leaned toward deism, prioritizing reason over dogma. Jefferson, author of the Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom enacted in 1786, advocated strict separation to prevent coercion, arguing that "compulsion in religion" corrupted both faith and state. This diversity informed governance: while states like Massachusetts retained Congregational establishments until 1833, federal disestablishment avoided a single denomination's dominance, promoting voluntary religion as a bulwark against tyranny.

### 19th Century: Expansion, Awakenings, and Sectarian Growth

The Second Great Awakening, spanning roughly 1800 to 1835, marked a period of widespread religious revivals across the United States, particularly in frontier regions like Kentucky and Tennessee, where Presbyterians, Methodists, and Baptists led camp meetings that drew thousands. These events emphasized personal conversion, moral reform, and emotional preaching, contrasting with the more formal Calvinist traditions, and resulted in church membership roughly doubling nationwide during this era. The movement's influence extended to social causes, including temperance and abolitionism, though it also deepened denominational divisions as participants sought "purer" forms of faith.

Evangelical denominations like Methodists and Baptists experienced explosive growth, capitalizing on the Awakening's momentum and the circuit-riding preachers who reached remote settlements. By the mid-19th century, Methodists and Baptists together comprised about 70 percent of American Protestants, with Methodists alone expanding from fewer than 300,000 members in 1800 to over 4 million by 1860 through aggressive evangelism and organizational efficiency. Baptists similarly surged, appealing to individualistic doctrines of believer's baptism and congregational autonomy, which resonated in the democratizing West. This sectarian proliferation reflected broader societal shifts, including disestablishment of state churches in remaining holdout states like Massachusetts (1833), fostering a competitive religious marketplace.

Westward expansion intertwined with religious dissemination, as settlers carried Protestant voluntarism into new territories, often viewing territorial acquisition as divinely ordained under concepts akin to Manifest Destiny. Missionaries and denominations established congregations amid migration, with the Second Awakening's fervor fueling institutions like Bible societies and seminaries to sustain growth. Concurrently, new sects emerged from revivalist fringes: the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, founded by Joseph Smith in Fayette, New York, on April 6, 1830, grew rapidly amid claims of restored primitive Christianity, prompting westward treks to Missouri and Illinois before the 1847 exodus to Utah under Brigham Young, where membership reached over 250,000 by Utah's 1896 statehood.

### 20th Century: Immigration Waves, Wars, and Early Secular Challenges

The early 20th century witnessed substantial waves of immigration from Southern and Eastern Europe, fundamentally diversifying the predominantly Protestant religious landscape of the United States. From 1901 to 1910 alone, over 8.8 million immigrants arrived, with the majority hailing from Italy, Poland, Russia, and the Austro-Hungarian Empire—regions dominated by Catholicism, Judaism, and Eastern Orthodoxy. This influx included an estimated 3.5 million Catholics between 1900 and 1920, swelling the U.S. Catholic population from roughly 12 million in 1900 to 18 million by 1930 and establishing ethnic parishes that preserved linguistic and cultural ties. Jewish immigration, peaking at about 2 million from Eastern Europe between 1880 and 1924 amid pogroms and economic hardship, expanded the American Jewish community to approximately 4.2 million by the late 1920s, with many arriving as secular or Reform Jews who integrated into urban intellectual life. These newcomers challenged Protestant cultural hegemony, prompting the construction of thousands of synagogues, Catholic cathedrals, and Orthodox churches, while fostering urban religious pluralism amid rural Protestant resistance.

The Immigration Act of 1924, enacting strict national-origin quotas favoring Northern Europeans, curtailed this flow—reducing annual arrivals from over 800,000 in the early 1920s to under 300,000 by decade's end—and reflected nativist fears of "un-American" faiths eroding social cohesion. Organizations like the revived Ku Klux Klan, peaking at 4-5 million members in the mid-1920s, explicitly targeted Catholics and Jews as threats to Protestant values, blending religious prejudice with anti-immigrant sentiment. Despite such tensions, immigration spurred institutional growth: Catholic dioceses proliferated, Jewish federations formed for communal aid, and interfaith dialogues tentatively emerged in cities, laying groundwork for later pluralism.

World War I and World War II integrated religion into national mobilization, emphasizing a shared Judeo-Christian ethic while exposing faiths to scrutiny. In WWI, Protestant, Catholic, and Jewish leaders framed the conflict as a crusade against Prussian militarism, with groups like the YMCA and Knights of Columbus providing welfare to troops and promoting voluntary interdenominational chapels; however, the war's 116,000 American deaths fueled postwar disillusionment and a surge in spiritualism as grieving families sought contact with the dead. WWII amplified pluralism, as the military commissioned over 12,000 chaplains from diverse denominations—including 40% Protestant, 30% Catholic, and growing Jewish representation—to serve 16 million personnel, standardizing multi-faith services and eroding sectarian barriers through shared sacrifice. Conscientious objectors from pacifist sects like Quakers and Mennonites numbered about 50,000, testing free exercise claims amid public suspicion of disloyalty. These wars reinforced religion's patriotic role but also highlighted causal strains: mass mobilization disrupted local congregations, and exposure to global ideologies sowed seeds of doubt.

### Post-1945 to Present: Cultural Shifts, Declines, and Signs of Resurgence

Following World War II, religious affiliation and practice in the United States reached historic highs, with church membership rising from 57% of the population in 1950 to 63.3% by the end of the decade, outpacing population growth. Weekly church attendance peaked at 49% in the mid-to-late 1950s, reflecting a cultural emphasis on faith amid Cold War anxieties over atheism in communism. This era saw institutional additions like "under God" to the Pledge of Allegiance in 1954 and "In God We Trust" as the national motto in 1956, embedding religious symbolism in civic life.

The 1960s marked a pivotal cultural shift toward secularization, driven by the countercultural revolution, which challenged traditional moral norms on sexuality, authority, and family structures. This period initiated a "shock and aftershock" in religious adherence, with rising skepticism toward institutional religion amid social upheavals like the Vietnam War and civil rights struggles. Church membership, which had held above 70% from 1937 through the 1970s, began a gradual erosion, falling to an average of 68% in the 1980s and 1990s. By the 1970s, the religiously unaffiliated ("nones") emerged as a growing category, comprising about 5% of adults, up from near zero in the 1950s.

The decline accelerated into the late 20th and early 21st centuries, with Christian identification dropping from about 90% of adults in the early 1990s to roughly two-thirds by 2022. The nones expanded to 28% of U.S. adults by 2023, including atheists, agnostics, and those claiming "nothing in particular," particularly among younger generations disaffiliating from childhood faiths. Church membership fell below 50% for the first time in Gallup polling by 2020, down from 70% in the mid-20th century, with weekly attendance stabilizing around 36% but skewed lower among millennials and Gen Z. Evangelical Protestantism saw temporary growth in the 1970s–1980s through movements like the Moral Majority, yet overall trends reflect institutional scandals, cultural liberalization, and perceived irrelevance to modern life.

## Legal and Constitutional Framework

### First Amendment: Establishment and Free Exercise Clauses

The First Amendment's religion clauses, ratified on December 15, 1791, as part of the Bill of Rights, state: "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof." These provisions emerged from debates during the Constitutional Convention and ratification, where Anti-Federalists feared federal overreach into religious matters, prompting assurances that the national government would neither impose a church nor interfere with state-level religious arrangements. James Madison, who introduced the amendment's language in the First Congress on June 8, 1789, shaped it based on Virginia's disestablishment struggles, including his successful opposition to a 1784 bill for state-funded Christian teachers and his authorship of the 1785 Memorial and Remonstrance Against Religious Assessments, which argued that coerced support for religion violated natural rights and that faith thrives without government compulsion.

The Establishment Clause originally meant to bar Congress from enacting laws that formally designate a national religion, compel taxpayer funding of religious institutions, or mandate religious observance, drawing from colonial experiences with Anglican establishment and state assessments while permitting non-preferential accommodations like legislative chaplains or military exemptions—practices contemporaneous with ratification. Historical evidence from ratification debates and state constitutions indicates the clause targeted federal coercion or exclusivity, not incidental government benefits to religion or symbolic acknowledgments, as evidenced by early congressional actions such as hiring chaplains on June 1, 1789, and issuing Thanksgiving proclamations. This understanding contrasts with later strict-separation interpretations, which some scholars attribute to 20th-century judicial expansions rather than 1789-1791 public meaning, potentially overlooking the Founders' view of religion as a voluntary moral foundation for republican governance.

The Free Exercise Clause, proposed by Madison as a safeguard for "the full and free right of conscience," prohibited laws directly targeting or burdening religious beliefs or practices, rooted in natural rights philosophy and ratification-era concerns over federal suppression of dissenters like Baptists and Quakers. Originally, it allowed generally applicable laws to incidentally restrict religious conduct if neutral and non-discriminatory—such as Sunday closing laws upheld in early cases—without requiring exemptions, reflecting the era's emphasis on public order over absolute exemptions. Madison's drafts evolved from broader protections against congressional "intermeddling with the distinct and independent provinces" of religion to the final text, underscoring intent to shield exercise from federal prohibition while not elevating religious claims above civil authority.

### Key Supreme Court Cases Shaping Religious Liberty

In Cantwell v. Connecticut (1940), the Supreme Court incorporated the Free Exercise Clause to the states via the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause, striking down a statute requiring a license for religious solicitation that granted officials discretionary power to deny permits based on content, as it enabled prior restraint on protected speech and solicitation. The decision emphasized that neutral licensing must not target religious proselytizing, establishing early protections against state interference with door-to-door evangelism.

Everson v. Board of Education (1947) extended the Establishment Clause to the states, upholding a New Jersey law reimbursing parents for bus fares to parochial schools under the "child benefit" theory, while articulating a "wall of separation" between church and state derived from Jefferson's metaphor, though permitting indirect aid that did not foster government religion. This 5-4 ruling rejected absolute separation, allowing accommodations where public welfare programs benefited religious institutions incidentally, influencing subsequent aid-to-religion cases.

The Sherbert v. Verner (1963) test imposed strict scrutiny on government burdens on religious practice, ruling that South Carolina's denial of unemployment benefits to a Seventh-day Adventist fired for refusing Saturday work violated free exercise, as the state lacked a compelling interest overriding sincere beliefs and failed to show minimal infringement. This framework required proving substantial burden, compelling governmental interest, and least restrictive means, prioritizing individual exemptions from neutral laws with incidental religious impacts.

### Modern Tensions: Balancing Pluralism and Tradition

In recent decades, tensions have arisen in the United States between maintaining religious pluralism—accommodating diverse faiths and secular perspectives—and preserving traditional religious norms, particularly those rooted in Christianity, which has historically shaped American public life. A 2024 Pew Research Center survey found that 80% of U.S. adults believe religion's influence on American life is shrinking, with 49% viewing this decline as negative, reflecting concerns among traditionalists that secularization erodes moral foundations derived from Judeo-Christian ethics. This perception aligns with empirical trends: religious "nones" rose from 16% in 2007 to 29% in 2021, correlating with cultural shifts toward individualism and away from communal religious observance, though a 2025 Gallup poll indicated a slight rebound, with only 59% seeing religion's societal influence as decreasing, down from 75% in prior years.

Legal flashpoints exemplify these frictions, often pitting free exercise rights against mandates for inclusive pluralism. In 303 Creative LLC v. Elenis (2023), the Supreme Court ruled 6-3 that a Christian web designer could not be compelled under Colorado's anti-discrimination law to create content affirming same-sex marriages, prioritizing First Amendment protections for traditional religious views on marriage over state-enforced neutrality in commercial speech. Similarly, Masterpiece Cakeshop v. Colorado Civil Rights Commission (2018) upheld a baker's refusal to design a cake for a same-sex wedding, citing hostility toward religion in enforcement as violating neutrality principles. These decisions highlight causal tensions: laws promoting pluralism, such as those expanding civil rights to include sexual orientation and gender identity, frequently conflict with traditional doctrines defining marriage and human nature, leading to exemptions that critics argue undermine equality while proponents defend as essential to pluralism's core—protecting minority religious dissent. Pending 2025 cases, including challenges to transgender policies in schools and religious foster care agencies, further test this balance, with the Court leaning toward accommodating faith-based objections.

Public discourse reveals deepening divides, fueled by immigration-driven religious diversity and secular advocacy. Pew data from 2024 shows 48% of Americans perceive conflict between their religious beliefs and mainstream culture, particularly on issues like abortion and education, where traditionalists advocate for policies reflecting biblical anthropology against pluralistic expansions of rights. Evangelical and Catholic coalitions have mobilized against perceived erosions, such as school curricula incorporating gender fluidity or restrictions on public prayer, arguing these prioritize ideological uniformity over historic traditions. Conversely, pluralists, including growing Muslim, Hindu, and unaffiliated populations, push for accommodations like prayer spaces or halal options in public institutions, straining resources and sparking backlash from those viewing such expansions as diluting Christianity's foundational role. A 2025 analysis notes that while 59% now see religion's public role as positive, partisan gaps persist—Republicans overwhelmingly favor greater religious influence (72% positive), versus Democrats (38%)—underscoring how electoral politics amplifies these tensions without resolving underlying causal incompatibilities between relativist pluralism and absolutist traditions.

## Current Demographic Composition

### Overall Statistics on Affiliation and Trends

As of the Pew Research Center's 2023-24 Religious Landscape Study, 62% of U.S. adults identify as Christians, comprising 40% Protestants, 19% Catholics, and 3% members of other Christian groups such as Orthodox Christians or Mormons. Religiously unaffiliated adults, often termed "nones," constitute 29% of the population, including atheists, agnostics, and those identifying as "nothing in particular." The remaining 9% adhere to non-Christian religions, such as Judaism (approximately 2%), Islam (1%), Hinduism (1%), Buddhism (1%), and smaller shares of other faiths including Sikhism and folk religions.

These figures reflect self-reported affiliation rather than active practice, with surveys indicating lower rates of regular worship attendance; for instance, Gallup's 2023 polling found only 32% of Americans attending religious services weekly or nearly weekly, down from 42% in 2000. Variations across surveys highlight methodological differences: the Public Religion Research Institute's 2024 Census reported 65% Christian identification, while Gallup's 2023 average yielded 68%, potentially due to differences in sampling frames and question wording that affect how respondents categorize marginal or cultural affiliations.

Christian affiliation has declined steadily but shows signs of stabilization. Pew data tracks the Christian share from 78% in 2007 to 71% in 2014 and 62% in 2023-24, a slowdown from the earlier pace of decline. Conversely, the unaffiliated share rose from 16% to 23% to 29% over the same periods, though recent analyses suggest this growth is decelerating, particularly among younger cohorts where switching away from religion has plateaued. Non-Christian religious groups have grown modestly from 4% to 9%, driven by immigration and conversions, but remain a small minority.

### Christianity as the Foundational Majority Faith

Christianity formed the religious bedrock of the American colonies, with the majority of European settlers arriving as Protestants seeking to practice their faith free from European state interference. Many British North American colonies, such as those in New England, were explicitly established for religious purposes by groups like the Puritans, who aimed to create communities governed by biblical principles. The Founding Fathers, predominantly orthodox Christians or deists informed by Christian ethics, incorporated Judeo-Christian concepts of natural rights and moral order into foundational documents; for instance, the Declaration of Independence invokes the "Creator" as endowing unalienable rights. While the Constitution avoided establishing a national church to prevent sectarian strife, Christianity's influence permeated early state constitutions, laws, and public life, reflecting its status as the de facto majority faith.

This predominance persisted through the 19th and 20th centuries, as waves of Protestant revivalism and Catholic immigration reinforced Christianity's cultural and demographic dominance, embedding it in institutions like public education and civil ceremonies. Christian assumptions undergirded legal frameworks on marriage, charity, and ethics, even as religious pluralism grew. By the mid-20th century, surveys consistently showed over 90% of Americans identifying as Christian, underscoring its role in national identity amid industrialization and global conflicts.

In contemporary demographics, Christianity remains the majority religion, with 62% of U.S. adults identifying as Christian in the 2023-2024 period: 40% Protestant, 19% Catholic, and 3% other denominations including Orthodox and Latter-day Saints. This figure reflects stabilization after a decline from 78% in 2007, holding steady between 60% and 64% since 2019, potentially indicating a plateau amid broader secularization trends. Regional variations persist, with higher concentrations in the South and Midwest, where evangelical Protestantism predominates, sustaining Christianity's foundational imprint on American values despite challenges from urbanization and cultural shifts.

### Non-Christian Abrahamic and Eastern Religions

Judaism represents the largest non-Christian Abrahamic faith in the United States, with approximately 5.8 million adults identifying as Jewish in 2021, comprising about 2.4% of the U.S. adult population when including those with Jewish background or ancestry alongside religious identification. Of these, 4.2 million identify primarily by religion, concentrated in states like New York and California. Jewish communities trace origins to the colonial era, with the first synagogue, Touro in Newport, Rhode Island, established in 1763, and significant immigration waves from Central and Eastern Europe between 1880 and 1924 bolstering numbers to over 4 million by mid-20th century. Post-Holocaust arrivals and secular retention patterns have sustained presence, though intermarriage rates exceeding 50% among non-Orthodox Jews contribute to gradual decline in strict religious affiliation.

Islam, the second-largest Abrahamic minority faith, accounts for roughly 1% of U.S. adults, or about 3.5 million Muslims as of recent estimates, with growth driven primarily by immigration since the 1965 Immigration and Nationality Act amendments, which lifted national-origin quotas favoring Asian and Middle Eastern entrants. Early Muslim presence dates to enslaved Africans in the 16th-19th centuries, estimated at 10-15% of imports practicing Islam, though assimilation eroded distinct communities until 20th-century revivals like the Nation of Islam, founded in 1930, which later aligned with orthodox Sunni Islam under influences like Malcolm X. Contemporary adherents, over half foreign-born, show higher fertility rates and conversion inflows, projecting potential doubling by 2050 absent policy shifts.

Eastern religions, encompassing Hinduism, Buddhism, Sikhism, and smaller traditions like Jainism, collectively represent about 2-3% of the population, with rapid expansion tied to post-1965 Asian immigration. Hinduism claims around 2.5 million adherents, or 0.7-1% of Americans, largely Indian-origin professionals in tech hubs like Silicon Valley, where temple construction surged from under 100 in 1980 to over 1,000 by 2020. Buddhism, at approximately 1% or 3 million, draws from East Asian immigrants and Western converts via Zen and Tibetan lineages popularized since the 1950s Beat Generation, with highest concentrations in California and Hawaii. Sikhism, originating from Punjab, numbers 200,000-500,000, centered in California and New York agribusiness communities since early 20th-century arrivals, marked by the 1912 Stockton Gurdwara as the first U.S. temple. These groups exhibit high retention due to cultural insularity and family structures, contrasting broader secular trends.

### Indigenous Traditions and Native Spiritualities

Indigenous spiritual traditions in the United States encompass a diverse array of practices among over 570 federally recognized tribes, each maintaining distinct beliefs, rituals, and cosmologies shaped by specific geographies, histories, and oral traditions rather than a singular pan-Indian religion. These traditions typically emphasize animism, wherein natural elements, animals, and landscapes possess spiritual agency, and reverence for a Creator or Great Spirit manifesting in various forms. Ceremonies such as vision quests, sweat lodges for purification, and communal rites like the Sun Dance among Plains tribes involve physical endurance, prayer, and communal reciprocity to foster harmony with the cosmos.

European colonization from the 16th century onward systematically suppressed these practices through missionary efforts, forced relocations like the Trail of Tears in the 1830s, and government policies including the 1883 Code of Indian Offenses that criminalized dances and healers, alongside boarding schools that prohibited native languages and rituals until the mid-20th century. The American Indian Religious Freedom Act, signed into law on August 11, 1978, by President Jimmy Carter, marked a pivotal recognition of these rights, declaring that federal policies should protect the inherent right of American Indians to practice their religions, including access to sacred sites and use of peyote in the Native American Church. Amendments in 1994 further addressed peyote sacramental use and eagle feathers for ceremonies, though enforcement has faced challenges from land development and resource extraction conflicting with tribal claims to sites like the San Francisco Peaks.

Contemporary adherence defies simple categorization, as many of the approximately 5.2 million self-identified Native Americans blend traditional spiritualities with Christianity—evident in syncretic practices—or identify as unaffiliated, with Pew Research indicating less than 1% explicitly affiliate with "Native American religions" in surveys, though this undercounts informal, community-based observances. PRRI data from 2020 shows 28% of Native Americans as religiously unaffiliated, yet qualitative studies highlight persistent engagement in sacred site pilgrimages, healing rituals, and revitalization movements post-1978, particularly among reservation communities and through organizations like the Native American Church, which integrates peyote visions with monotheistic elements. Urban migration has adapted practices, fostering pan-tribal gatherings, while legal victories under the Religious Freedom Restoration Act of 1993 have upheld access to eagle parts for over 50 tribes via the National Eagle Repository established in 2000. These traditions prioritize ecological stewardship and intergenerational transmission, countering historical erasure through language revitalization and ceremonial resurgence documented in ethnographic records since the 1990s.

## Internal Diversity and American-Origin Movements

### Protestant Denominations and Evangelical Influences

Protestantism constitutes the largest segment of Christianity in the United States, encompassing approximately 34% of adults as of 2023-2024, divided primarily between evangelical Protestants at 23% and mainline Protestants at 11%. This includes diverse denominations such as Baptists, Methodists, Lutherans, Presbyterians, and Pentecostals, with the Southern Baptist Convention remaining the largest single Protestant body, claiming around 13 million members in 2023 despite recent declines from schisms and membership losses. The United Methodist Church, a mainline denomination, reported about 5.7 million U.S. members in 2023 following a split over theological differences on issues like same-sex marriage, which led to the formation of the more conservative Global Methodist Church with over 100,000 members by 2024. Other significant groups include the Evangelical Lutheran Church in America (mainline, ~3.3 million adherents) and the Assemblies of God (Pentecostal evangelical, ~3 million).

Evangelicalism, a trans-denominational movement emphasizing personal conversion, biblical authority, and evangelism, has profoundly shaped American Protestantism since the First Great Awakening in the 1730s-1740s, led by figures like Jonathan Edwards and George Whitefield, which spurred widespread revivals and denominational growth. Its influence peaked politically in the late 20th century through organizations like the Moral Majority, founded in 1979 by Jerry Falwell, which mobilized evangelicals—then about 25% of the electorate—around social conservatism, contributing to the election of Ronald Reagan in 1980 and subsequent Republican dominance on issues like abortion restrictions post-Roe v. Wade. Today, evangelicals maintain cultural sway via megachurches, media empires like those of Joel Osteen or Franklin Graham, and voter blocs, comprising roughly 40 million adherents who prioritize doctrinal orthodoxy amid broader Protestant decline. Black Protestant churches, often evangelical in theology, represent a distinct tradition with about 7% of the population, influencing civil rights movements through leaders like Martin Luther King Jr. while sustaining high religiosity in African American communities.

Tensions between mainline and evangelical wings reflect theological divergences, with mainline denominations adopting progressive stances on social issues—such as the Episcopal Church's ordination of women since 1976 and affirmation of LGBTQ+ clergy—contrasting evangelical commitments to traditional doctrines, resulting in membership shifts and schisms. Evangelical growth in non-denominational churches, now numbering over 35,000 congregations with 12-13 million attendees, underscores a trend toward independent, Bible-centered fellowships that bypass hierarchical structures, fostering innovation but also fragmentation. Despite overall Protestant disaffiliation rates mirroring national trends—driven by secularization and generational shifts—evangelical retention remains higher among youth due to emphasis on experiential faith and community engagement.

### Catholicism and Orthodox Christianity

Catholicism arrived in the territory of the present-day United States through early European explorations, with Spanish missionaries establishing missions in Florida as early as 1565 and French Jesuits in the Great Lakes region by the 17th century. A deliberate Catholic settlement occurred in Maryland in 1634 under Cecil Calvert, Lord Baltimore, where Jesuits accompanied settlers and constructed the first church and school. The faith's growth accelerated in the 19th century via mass immigration, particularly from Ireland following the 1840s famine, increasing the Catholic population from about 5% of the U.S. total in 1850 to 17% by 1906, driven by waves from Ireland, Germany, Italy, Poland, and later Latin America.

As of 2025, approximately 19-20% of U.S. adults self-identify as Catholic, representing stability since 2014 after a decline from 24% in 2007, with net losses from disaffiliation partially offset by Hispanic immigration. The demographic profile shows 54% White, 36% Hispanic, 4% Asian, and 2% Black Catholics, with immigrants comprising 29% and their children 14% of the total. Catholics tend to be older than the general population, with weekly Mass attendance around 25% in recent decades, down from over 50% in the 1970s, reflecting broader trends in religious practice amid secularization and internal challenges like clergy abuse scandals.

The U.S. Catholic Church operates through 176 Latin-rite dioceses and archdioceses, plus Eastern Catholic jurisdictions, overseen by the United States Conference of Catholic Bishops (USCCB), with significant institutions including over 6,000 elementary and secondary schools and 600 colleges and universities as of recent counts. Cultural influence persists in areas like education, healthcare (operating about one-sixth of U.S. hospitals), and social services, though retention rates lag, with only about one-third of those raised Catholic remaining active adherents into adulthood.

### Judaism, Islam, and Other Imported Faiths

Judaism arrived in the United States with the first Jewish settlers in the 17th century, including a group of 23 Sephardic Jews fleeing persecution in Brazil who landed in New Amsterdam in 1654. Subsequent waves of immigration, particularly from Central and Eastern Europe between 1820 and 1924, swelled the population to over 3 million by the early 20th century, driven by pogroms and economic hardship. Today, the U.S. Jewish population stands at approximately 7.5 million, or 2.4% of the total population, encompassing those identifying by religion, ancestry, or culture, with about 5.8 million adults self-identifying as Jewish by religion. This figure reflects stability amid high rates of intermarriage and disaffiliation, as roughly 25% of those raised Jewish no longer identify as such in adulthood. Concentrations remain in urban areas like New York and Los Angeles, where Orthodox communities have grown through higher birth rates, comprising about 10% of the total Jewish population.

Islam's presence in the U.S. traces to enslaved Africans in the 16th to 19th centuries, with estimates suggesting 10-50% of slaves were Muslim, though most lost their faith under oppression. Significant immigration began in the late 19th century from the Ottoman Empire and South Asia, accelerating after the 1965 Immigration Act, which ended national-origin quotas and facilitated entry from Muslim-majority countries. The current Muslim population is estimated at 3.5 to 4.5 million, representing about 1.1% of the U.S. population, with growth primarily from immigration and higher fertility rates. Roughly 73% are Sunni, 16% Shia, and the rest from other sects or movements; about 20% are converts, though retention challenges persist, with 23% of raised Muslims leaving the faith. Muslims are diverse ethnically, with significant South Asian, Arab, and African American communities, concentrated in states like Michigan, New York, and California.

Other imported faiths, primarily from Asia, have expanded through post-1965 immigration. Hinduism claims around 3.6 million adherents in North America as of 2020, mostly Indian immigrants and their descendants, equating to about 1% of the U.S. population, with growth tied to professional migration in technology and medicine sectors. Buddhism numbers approximately 3 million, blending immigrant traditions from East and Southeast Asia with convert communities drawn to Zen and Tibetan practices since the mid-20th century. Sikhism, imported via Punjabi laborers in the early 1900s and later professionals, has an estimated 200,000 to 500,000 followers, or less than 0.2% of the population, centered in California and New York, known for gurdwaras like the historic Stockton temple established in 1915. Smaller groups include Jains (around 150,000, largely Gujarati professionals) and Baha'is (under 200,000), reflecting niche immigration patterns rather than mass conversion. These faiths collectively represent under 3% of Americans but contribute to urban religious pluralism, with temples and centers proliferating in immigrant hubs.

### U.S.-Born Movements: Mormons, Adventists, and Pentecostalism

The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, commonly known as the Mormon church, was founded on April 6, 1830, in Fayette, New York, by Joseph Smith, who reported visions directing him to ancient records translated as the Book of Mormon, published that same year. Distinctive doctrines include the belief in ongoing revelation through living prophets, additional scriptures beyond the Bible, and practices such as baptism for the dead and eternal marriage in temples. As of December 31, 2024, the church reports 17,509,781 members worldwide, with approximately half residing in the United States, concentrated in Utah and neighboring states where it exerts cultural and political influence.

The Seventh-day Adventist Church arose from the Millerite movement, which anticipated Christ's return based on interpretations of biblical prophecy, culminating in the Great Disappointment on October 22, 1844, when the expected event failed to materialize. This led to doctrinal refinements emphasizing the investigative judgment in heaven and Sabbath-keeping on Saturday, with the church formally organized on May 21, 1863, in Battle Creek, Michigan. Ellen G. White, a co-founder, contributed over 100,000 pages of writings viewed by adherents as inspired counsel on health, education, and eschatology, including advocacy for vegetarianism and opposition to alcohol and tobacco. By December 31, 2024, membership reached 23,684,237 globally, with strong growth in Africa and Latin America, though U.S. retention faces challenges from secular trends.

Pentecostalism emerged as a distinct movement in the United States around 1901, when Charles Parham in Topeka, Kansas, taught that speaking in tongues evidenced baptism in the Holy Spirit, but it exploded through William J. Seymour's Azusa Street Revival in Los Angeles, beginning April 9, 1906, in a former stable that drew diverse crowds for weeks of worship marked by glossolalia, healings, and prophecy. The revival, lasting until about 1915, spread Pentecostalism's emphasis on direct spiritual experiences and miracles, diverging from cessationist views in mainstream Protestantism, and birthed denominations like the Assemblies of God, organized in 1914. Today, Pentecostal and charismatic adherents number over 600 million worldwide, representing rapid expansion especially in the Global South, while in the U.S., groups like the Assemblies of God claim millions of members and influence evangelical politics through prosperity theology and worship styles. These movements, rooted in American soil, exemplify restorationist and experiential innovations that have reshaped global Christianity by prioritizing personal revelation, health codes, and supernatural empowerment over traditional creeds.

## The Unaffiliated and Secular Currents

### Composition of "Nones": Atheists, Agnostics, and Humanists

The religiously unaffiliated, often termed "nones," comprise individuals who do not identify with any organized religion, including self-described atheists, agnostics, and those selecting "nothing in particular" in surveys. According to the Pew Research Center's 2023-24 Religious Landscape Study, 29% of U.S. adults fall into this category, with atheists constituting 5% of the overall population (approximately 17% of nones), agnostics 6% (about 21% of nones), and the remaining 19% (roughly 62% of nones) identifying as "nothing in particular." This breakdown reflects a consistent pattern where explicit non-theism (atheism and agnosticism) represents a minority within the unaffiliated group, while the larger "nothing in particular" segment often includes individuals with residual spiritual leanings or indifference rather than outright rejection of supernatural concepts.

Atheists explicitly reject belief in any gods or deities, often grounding their worldview in empirical evidence and naturalism. In the same Pew data, this subgroup is distinguished by higher rates of certainty in their non-belief, with 93% of U.S. atheists affirming that there is no God, compared to varying degrees of doubt among others. Agnostics, by contrast, express uncertainty about the existence of God or deities, frequently citing insufficient evidence for affirmation or denial; Pew's analysis shows they share similar demographic traits with atheists, such as younger age and urban residency, but report lower absolute rejection rates (e.g., only 50% firmly stating no God exists). Public Religion Research Institute (PRRI) data from 2023 aligns closely, estimating 5% of adults as atheists and 5% as agnostics within a 27% unaffiliated total, underscoring the relative stability of these proportions across major polling organizations despite methodological differences in question wording.

Humanists, who emphasize secular ethics, reason, and human welfare without reliance on supernatural authority, form a smaller, less distinctly captured subset within the nones. Self-identification as humanist is rare in broad surveys, often subsumed under "nothing in particular" or atheist categories; for instance, Pew's 2023 data does not isolate humanists as a separate group, but organizational estimates from groups like the American Humanist Association suggest they number in the low hundreds of thousands, representing under 1% of the population. This diffuse presence stems from humanism's philosophical rather than strictly religious framing, with adherents prioritizing evidence-based morality over metaphysical claims, though surveys indicate overlap with atheists (e.g., 78% of humanists in niche polls affirm no God). Gallup's 2024 tracking, while not breaking out humanists, notes 5% of adults as atheists and 6% as agnostics within a 21% no-religion group, implying humanists contribute marginally to the explicitly secular core.

### "Spiritual but Not Religious" Phenomenon

The "spiritual but not religious" (SBNR) designation refers to individuals who affirm a sense of spirituality—often involving belief in a higher power, personal meaning, or transcendent experiences—while explicitly rejecting affiliation with organized religion or institutional doctrines. This self-identification has gained prominence since the early 2000s, reflecting a broader detachment from traditional religious structures amid rising individualism and skepticism toward ecclesiastical authority.

Prevalence data vary by survey methodology, but recent national polls indicate SBNR comprises a significant portion of the U.S. population. A 2023 Pew Research Center survey found 22% of American adults identifying as SBNR, defined as those who describe themselves as spiritual but not religious, often within the religiously unaffiliated ("nones") category, which reached 28% overall. In contrast, Gallup polls from 2023 and 2024 reported higher figures, with 33% of respondents selecting "spiritual but not religious" when offered that option alongside "religious" or "neither," up from 20% in 1999, signaling a steady increase correlated with declining church membership. These individuals differ from outright atheists or agnostics, who constitute about 5-6% of adults; among nones, SBNR outnumber strict nonbelievers, with 37% of unaffiliated expressing spiritual leanings versus 42% claiming neither spirituality nor religion.

Demographically, SBNR Americans skew younger, with higher concentrations among those under 30, and are more likely to be white, politically liberal, and less educated than the general population. They exhibit lower rates of regular religious practices, such as weekly attendance at services (under 10%), but engage in alternative pursuits like meditation (49% meditate regularly), yoga, or nature-based rituals, viewing spirituality as a personal, eclectic endeavor rather than communal or doctrinal. Belief patterns include a majority (58%) affirming God's existence with some certainty, though less emphatically than religious adherents, alongside openness to phenomena like reincarnation (35%) or spiritual energy in objects (42%), but with pronounced distrust of organized religion—42% believe it causes more harm than good, citing institutional scandals, hypocrisy, and rigidity as deterrents.

### Factors Driving Disaffiliation and Retention Patterns

A significant driver of religious disaffiliation in the United States is the loss of belief in core religious teachings, with 67% of those who have left their childhood religion citing this as the primary reason according to a 2023 PRRI survey. Similarly, Pew Research Center data from 2023 indicates that two-thirds of religiously unaffiliated adults question religious teachings or express disbelief in God. This intellectual shift often correlates with higher education levels and exposure to scientific perspectives, though empirical studies emphasize personal reevaluation over institutional pressure. Disaffiliation rates vary demographically: Democrats (75%) and political independents (68%) are more likely than Republicans (38%) to cite disbelief, while LGBTQ individuals (73%) disproportionately mention negative religious stances on sexual orientation and gender identity compared to non-LGBTQ individuals (41%).

Institutional factors also contribute substantially, including perceptions of organizational flaws and moral failings. In the same PRRI survey, 47% of disaffiliates pointed to negative teachings or treatment of LGBTQ persons—a figure that doubled from 29% in 2016—while 31% referenced clergy sexual abuse scandals, up from 19% previously, with former Catholics particularly affected at 45%. Pew data aligns, showing 47% disliking religious organizations and 30% reporting negative experiences with religious individuals. Political entanglement ranks lower but is notable, with 17% overall and up to 23% in some analyses attributing departure to congregations becoming overly politicized, often linked to evangelical alignments with conservative politics alienating younger or progressive members. These patterns accelerated post-2010, with 35% of U.S. adults—approximately 90 million—having switched or dropped religious identities by 2025, predominantly from Christianity.

Retention patterns reveal stark group differences, with upbringing emerging as the strongest predictor: those raised in a faith tradition are far more likely to maintain it, as evidenced by 73% of Christians raised in the faith remaining so into adulthood per 2025 Pew analysis. Black Protestants exhibit the highest retention at 82%, followed by Jewish Americans at 77% and white evangelical Protestants at 76% (an improvement from 66% in 2016), often tied to cultural identity, communal solidarity, and resistance to assimilation pressures. Hispanic Catholics retain at 68%, buoyed by ethnic ties, while mainline Protestants and white Catholics see lower rates around 60-62%, reflecting weaker doctrinal emphasis or internal liberalization.
