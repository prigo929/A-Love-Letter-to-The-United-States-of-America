# The Great Depression

## Introduction

The Great Depression in the United States was a severe and extended economic contraction that originated with the Wall Street stock market crash of October 1929 and persisted until roughly 1941, when the industrial mobilization for World War II ended the downturn. It featured a cascade of bank failures exceeding 9,000 institutions, a monetary contraction that reduced the money supply by about one-third, and a deflationary spiral that eroded asset values and consumer purchasing power. Real gross domestic product plummeted by approximately one-third between 1929 and 1933, while unemployment rates climbed to nearly 25 percent of the civilian labor force by 1933, inflicting widespread hardship including mass homelessness, farm foreclosures, and acute poverty.

Initial responses under President Herbert Hoover involved limited federal interventions such as the Reconstruction Finance Corporation to stabilize banking, but the crisis deepened amid policy shifts like the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1930, which exacerbated international trade contraction. Upon assuming office in 1933, President Franklin D. Roosevelt launched the New Deal, a series of programs expanding federal authority over labor markets, agriculture, and infrastructure to combat deflation and unemployment, though empirical studies indicate these measures, including wage controls and cartelization under the National Industrial Recovery Act, likely retarded recovery by distorting price signals and discouraging investment. Recovery gained traction only with wartime production demands, which restored full employment but masked underlying structural issues unresolved until the 1950s. The episode underscores debates over monetary policy failures—particularly the Federal Reserve's inaction against banking panics and money supply shrinkage—as primary causal drivers, rather than mere market excesses.

## Economic Causes

### Federal Reserve Policies and Credit Expansion

Following the sharp recession of 1920-1921, during which the Federal Reserve raised its discount rate to 7 percent to combat postwar inflation, the central bank lowered rates significantly and maintained them at low levels to support recovery. From 1922 to mid-1927, the New York Federal Reserve Bank's discount rate averaged approximately 4 percent, with brief adjustments to 3.5 percent in periods of slack, encouraging member banks to borrow reserves at favorable terms. This accommodative stance persisted amid growing economic output, as the Fed prioritized stability over preemptive tightening despite emerging signs of excess in financial markets.

The Fed's discounting policies facilitated an expansion of bank credit, particularly through loans to brokers collateralized by stock market securities, which member banks funded partly by rediscounting eligible paper at Federal Reserve banks. Although the Federal Reserve Act aimed to channel credit toward commercial and agricultural needs, oversight proved inadequate, allowing banks to circumvent guidelines by relending borrowed funds into speculation; brokers' loans, which totaled under $2 billion in 1922, ballooned to over $8 billion by mid-1929 as nonbank lenders and banks alike poured resources into the call loan market. The Fed issued warnings in 1926 and raised the discount rate to 5 percent in 1928 to signal restraint, but enforcement relied on voluntary compliance, and direct borrowing for speculative purposes continued unchecked until new board guidelines in early 1929 prohibited member banks from accessing the discount window if engaged in stock loans—a measure too late to reverse the momentum.

This credit expansion outstripped genuine savings and productive investment opportunities, distorting resource allocation toward asset speculation rather than sustainable capital formation. The M2 money stock grew at an average annual rate of 5 percent from 1921 to 1929, reflecting multiple expansion of deposits through bank lending, while real GNP increased by about 4.2 percent per year over the broader 1920-1929 period (with faster recovery growth post-1921). Low real interest rates, held below market-clearing levels by Fed accommodation, signaled abundant capital availability, prompting malinvestment—overexpansion in durable goods, real estate, and equities unsupported by consumer demand or underlying productivity gains, as firms and households deferred consumption in favor of yield-chasing in financial assets. Such imbalances, rooted in the artificial prolongation of credit beyond voluntary savings, amplified sectoral distortions and vulnerability to reversal when credit conditions inevitably tightened.

### Asset Bubbles and Speculative Excess

The U.S. stock market in the 1920s exhibited classic signs of an asset bubble, with equity prices detaching from corporate earnings and dividends due to rampant speculation. From a post-World War I low of 63.90 in August 1921, the Dow Jones Industrial Average climbed steadily, accelerating in the latter half of the decade to reach 381.17 on September 3, 1929—a gain of over 500% in less than eight years. Price-to-earnings ratios for leading stocks exceeded 30 by 1929, far above historical norms of 10-15, signaling overvaluation as investors extrapolated perpetual growth amid low interest rates and optimistic forecasts.

Margin buying amplified this distortion, enabling investors to leverage purchases with minimal equity down payments, typically 10-20% of the stock's value, borrowed at call from brokers backed by bank loans. Outstanding brokers' loans peaked at roughly $8.5 billion in summer 1929, representing about 10% of the era's gross national product and fueling a feedback loop where rising prices justified further borrowing. Herd behavior dominated, with even conservative institutions and individuals speculating under the illusion of low risk, as easy credit masked underlying fragilities like uneven profit growth across sectors. This leverage distorted price signals, directing capital toward unproductive speculation rather than genuine investment, setting the stage for a sharp correction when margin calls mounted.

Consumer installment credit extended similar dynamics beyond stocks to durable goods, blurring lines between productive consumption and overextension. By 1929, outstanding installment debt for items like automobiles and household appliances approached $7 billion, up from negligible levels pre-war, as retailers and banks offered deferred payments to boost sales amid rising incomes. This credit expansion, while stimulating short-term demand, heightened household vulnerability to economic shocks, mirroring the leverage risks in financial markets and contributing to broader imbalances.

### Structural Imbalances in Key Sectors

The U.S. agricultural sector exhibited deep structural imbalances by the 1920s, rooted in wartime expansion and postwar overproduction. During World War I, farmers increased output to meet Allied demand, borrowing extensively for land and equipment, which drove mortgage debt per acre up 135% from 1910 to 1920. Postwar recovery of European agriculture created global surpluses, causing U.S. commodity prices to plummet starting in 1920 and decline at an average annual rate of 1.54% through 1929. Debt-laden farmers intensified production to offset falling prices, worsening overproduction and trapping the sector in a cycle of declining incomes and rising foreclosures, with average county debt-to-value ratios reaching 28.9% by 1920.

Manufacturing sectors, notably automobiles and construction, developed overcapacity that heightened economic fragility. Automobile output expanded rapidly from 2.3 million vehicles in 1920 to 5.3 million in 1929, but by mid-1929, market penetration neared saturation, resulting in accumulating inventories of new and used cars. Residential construction boomed in the early 1920s, absorbing loanable funds through real estate bonds and innovations easing credit access, yet permits and activity fell 40.4% from 1925 to 1929, signaling excess supply relative to underlying demand.

These sectoral weaknesses intertwined with escalating debt burdens across households and farms, rendering the economy vulnerable to contraction. Farm debt-to-asset ratios surpassing 20% correlated with surging bankruptcies in the 1920s, as legacy wartime loans proved unmanageable amid price deflation. Mortgage debt nearly tripled from 1921 to 1929, while household consumer debt-to-income ratios doubled to 9.3% by 1929, constraining consumption and amplifying deleveraging risks upon any shock.

### International Constraints and Trade Disruptions

The United States emerged from World War I as the world's largest creditor, with European allies owing approximately $10 billion in principal war debts, structured under repayment plans that depended on German reparations and ongoing American loans. This interlinked financial system created vulnerabilities, as the 1929 stock market crash halted U.S. capital outflows to Europe, disrupting debt servicing and triggering banking strains abroad. The failure of Austria's Creditanstalt bank on May 11, 1931, exemplified this contagion, leading to withdrawals of short-term credits from Central European banks and heightened demands on U.S. financial institutions for liquidity.

Adherence to the gold standard imposed rigid constraints on monetary policy, requiring the Federal Reserve to prioritize gold convertibility over domestic expansion. In response to 1931 gold outflows and sterling's devaluation, the Fed raised discount rates to 3.5% in October 1931, defending reserves but exacerbating credit contraction; the U.S. money supply subsequently fell by nearly 30% from late 1930 to early 1933. Although U.S. gold holdings ultimately rose from $3.9 billion in 1929 to $4.2 billion by 1933 due to inflows from gold-standard abandonments elsewhere, the system's rules prevented proactive monetary easing, transmitting deflationary pressures globally.

Early protectionist measures further disrupted trade flows, with the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act—signed June 17, 1930—elevating average duties on dutiable imports to nearly 60% and affecting over 20,000 goods. Retaliatory tariffs from Canada, Europe, and other partners reduced U.S. export markets, contributing to a roughly 60% drop in exports from $5.2 billion in 1929 to $2.1 billion in 1932, before stabilizing lower. These international frictions compounded liquidity shortages and sectoral imbalances, as diminished foreign demand hit U.S. agriculture and manufacturing hardest, with farm exports falling 65% by 1932.

## Precipitating Events and Early Contraction

### Stock Market Crash of 1929

The stock market crash of 1929 unfolded over several days in late October, culminating in massive sell-offs on the New York Stock Exchange that erased billions in market value. Following a peak in the Dow Jones Industrial Average of 381.17 on September 3, 1929, early signs of strain appeared as trading volumes increased and prices became volatile. On October 24, known as Black Thursday, panic selling drove a record 12.9 million shares traded, surpassing the prior high of 8.2 million set in March. The Dow fell approximately 9 percent intraday, reflecting forced liquidations from margin calls as brokers demanded repayment on leveraged positions.

Prominent bankers, led by figures such as Albert Wiggin of Chase National Bank and coordinated through the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, intervened by purchasing large blocks of stock at above-market prices to restore confidence and halt the decline. This effort temporarily stabilized the market, with the Dow closing down only about 2 percent for the day after an initial plunge. However, the underlying pressures persisted, as high leverage— with margin loans estimated at $8.5 billion against a market value of around $50 billion—amplified selling when asset values dropped.

The respite proved short-lived. On October 28, Black Monday, the Dow declined nearly 13 percent amid renewed heavy volume, followed by Black Tuesday, October 29, when it fell another nearly 12 percent with over 16 million shares traded. These sessions resulted in a combined market value loss of roughly $16 billion in October alone, equivalent to about 18 percent of the month's starting capitalization. By mid-November, the Dow had lost nearly half its peak value, closing around 198 by the end of Black Tuesday before a partial rebound.

### Banking Panics and Financial Contagion

The banking panics that afflicted the United States from late 1929 through 1933 stemmed largely from eroding depositor confidence, which triggered mass withdrawals and exposed underlying illiquidity in the fractional-reserve banking system rather than fundamental insolvency across most institutions. The failure of the Bank of United States on December 11, 1929—the nation's largest bank collapse up to that point, with $285 million in deposits—ignited initial contagion as depositors questioned the stability of similar urban institutions, prompting runs that forced rapid asset sales at depressed prices. This event amplified liquidity strains, as banks reliant on short-term interbank lending found correspondent networks strained, spreading fear beyond New York to regional systems.

In 1930, panics clustered in rural and agricultural states, where banks heavily concentrated loans in farm mortgages amid plummeting crop prices and land values; over 1,350 banks suspended operations that year, with failures radiating from isolated rural runs to broader regional networks via interconnected holdings. Contagion accelerated through correspondent relationships, as seen in the November collapse of the Caldwell banking empire in the South, which controlled dozens of institutions and held significant deposits, leading to cascading withdrawals in Tennessee, Kentucky, and adjacent areas. Agricultural regions like the Midwest and Plains states suffered disproportionately, as commodity deflation—wheat prices fell over 50% from 1929 levels—eroded collateral values, prompting depositors to withdraw funds preemptively amid rumors of insolvency.

The 1931–1933 waves escalated into nationwide turmoil, with bank suspensions peaking in early 1933; cumulatively, more than 9,000 banks failed between 1930 and 1933, erasing roughly one-third of the total banking system. These runs, fueled by a "contagion of fear" among depositors, converted potentially solvent banks into illiquid ones overnight, as demands for cash exceeded reserves—typically 10–13% of deposits—forcing fire sales of assets and credit contraction. The resulting loss of approximately $7 billion in deposits directly curbed money creation, with the broader money supply (M1) contracting by nearly one-third from late 1929 to early 1933, as failed banks ceased lending and surviving ones hoarded reserves. This financial contagion deepened economic contraction by restricting credit to businesses and households, independent of underlying solvency issues in many cases.

### Deflationary Pressures and Economic Spiral

Following the stock market crash, consumer prices in the United States began a sustained decline, with the Consumer Price Index falling by nearly 25% between 1929 and 1933. Wholesale prices experienced even steeper drops, declining by approximately 37% over the same period as measured by the all-commodities index. This deflation amplified the real burden of existing debts, which were denominated in fixed nominal dollars; as prices fell, the purchasing power required to service loans and mortgages increased, prompting widespread defaults, foreclosures, and business failures that further eroded economic activity.

The deflationary dynamic intertwined with rigid nominal wages, which adjusted downward more slowly than prices due to contractual rigidities, union pressures, and worker resistance to cuts. Real wages thus rose relative to output prices, making labor more expensive for firms already facing collapsing demand, which compelled employers to reduce hiring, hours, and production—exacerbating unemployment and output contraction. Concurrently, expectations of further price declines encouraged cash hoarding by households and businesses, diminishing money velocity and effectively contracting the money supply's impact on spending, which intensified the downward pressure on prices and demand.

These forces propelled a self-reinforcing spiral: falling prices signaled lower future prices, delaying consumer purchases and investment; reduced spending triggered inventory gluts and production cuts; and curtailed output reinforced price declines. Real gross domestic product contracted by about 30% from 1929 to 1933, while industrial production halved, reflecting the depth of this feedback loop in core economic sectors.

## Government Policy Responses

### Hoover Administration Interventions

President Herbert Hoover initially responded to the emerging economic crisis with a philosophy of voluntarism, encouraging private sector cooperation and local initiatives rather than direct federal intervention. He organized conferences of business leaders to maintain wages and production levels voluntarily and urged state and local governments to address relief needs. However, as the contraction deepened in 1931 and 1932, Hoover shifted toward more active government measures, establishing mechanisms for federal lending and adjusting trade and fiscal policies, though these efforts remained limited in scope compared to later expansions.

A key intervention was the creation of the Reconstruction Finance Corporation (RFC) on January 22, 1932, authorized to provide loans to banks, railroads, and other financial institutions to prevent failures and stabilize credit. By the end of Hoover's term, the RFC had authorized approximately $951 million in loans, primarily to banks and railroads, aiming to inject liquidity into strained sectors without direct aid to individuals or businesses. While intended to avert systemic collapse, the RFC's selective support to large entities arguably prolonged inefficient operations and failed to stimulate broad recovery, as loan approvals favored politically connected institutions and did little to restore consumer confidence or aggregate demand.

The Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act, signed into law on June 17, 1930, raised average import duties to nearly 60% on dutiable goods, ostensibly to protect domestic industries amid falling prices. This protectionist measure prompted swift retaliation from trading partners, including Canada and European nations, which imposed counter-tariffs that reduced U.S. exports by over 60% from 1929 to 1932, exacerbating deflationary pressures and industrial contraction. Empirical evidence links the tariff's disruption of global trade flows to a deepened spiral, as foreign demand for American goods—particularly agricultural products—plummeted, contributing to farm foreclosures and rural distress without offsetting gains in protected sectors.

### Roosevelt's New Deal Initiatives

Following his inauguration on March 4, 1933, President Franklin D. Roosevelt launched the New Deal, a series of programs enacted primarily during the "First Hundred Days" from March to June 1933, aimed at providing relief to the unemployed, promoting economic recovery, and enacting reforms to prevent future crises. These initiatives included emergency banking legislation, the creation of federal agencies for job creation, agricultural stabilization, and industrial regulation.

The Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC), established on March 31, 1933, employed over 3 million young men from relief families in conservation projects such as reforestation, soil erosion control, and park development, offering them $30 per month with most wages sent home to families. The Agricultural Adjustment Act (AAA), signed on May 12, 1933, sought to raise farm prices by paying farmers to reduce production through crop acreage reduction and livestock slaughter, including the destruction of approximately 6 million hogs and sows at a cost exceeding $30 million, alongside plowing under cotton fields despite widespread hunger.

The National Industrial Recovery Act (NIRA), enacted on June 16, 1933, suspended antitrust laws to allow industries to draft "codes of fair competition" that set minimum wages, maximum hours, production quotas, and price floors, effectively enabling cartel-like arrangements to stabilize prices and employment. These codes, numbering over 600 by mid-1934, were criticized for reducing competition and enforcing wage rigidities that prevented necessary labor market adjustments, as higher mandated wages discouraged hiring and prolonged unemployment.

### Monetary Policy Experiments

On April 5, 1933, President Franklin D. Roosevelt issued Executive Order 6102, which prohibited the hoarding of gold coin, bullion, and certificates exceeding $100 in value (approximately 5 troy ounces), requiring their delivery to Federal Reserve Banks by May 1, 1933, in exchange for $20.67 per ounce. This measure, enacted amid the banking crisis, effectively suspended domestic gold convertibility and centralized gold holdings to expand the monetary base, with the administration intending to combat deflation by increasing currency issuance. Compliance yielded over 2,600 metric tons of gold, though evasion occurred, and penalties included fines up to $10,000 or ten years imprisonment.

The Gold Reserve Act, signed on January 30, 1934, nationalized all gold held by the Federal Reserve and authorized the president to devalue the dollar by revaluing gold to $35 per ounce, a 69% increase from the prior $20.67 rate. This devaluation generated approximately $2.8 billion in seigniorage profits for the Treasury (equivalent to about 5% of 1934 GDP), which funded monetary expansion and aimed to raise commodity prices to pre-Depression levels, such as restoring agricultural indices to 1926 parity. The policy boosted the monetary base by permitting greater issuance of currency and deposits backed by the inflated gold reserves, yet wholesale prices, which had fallen 33% from 1929 to 1933, rose only modestly initially, with deflationary pressures persisting due to weak demand and banking sector caution.

Complementing gold measures, the Silver Purchase Act of June 19, 1934, mandated Treasury purchases of silver until it comprised one-third of the combined gold and silver monetary stock or until market prices stabilized at 80% of the gold-silver ratio. Intended to inflate prices via bimetallism and appease silver-producing states, the program drove silver prices from $0.45 per ounce in 1934 to peaks near $0.77 by 1937, but diverted resources from gold-based expansion and exacerbated deflation abroad, such as in China, without proportionally lifting U.S. commodity indices.

### The 1937-1938 Recession

The recession of 1937–1938 marked a sharp contraction within the broader recovery from the Great Depression, with real gross domestic product (GDP) declining by approximately 10 percent from peak to trough and unemployment rising from 14.3 percent in 1937 to around 20 percent by mid-1938. Industrial production fell nearly 30 percent, and the downturn lasted about 13 months, reversing much of the gains achieved since 1933. This episode demonstrated the fragility of the economic rebound, as policy-induced tightening disrupted monetary and fiscal supports that had previously stabilized the system.

Monetary policy shifts by the Federal Reserve and Treasury played a central role in precipitating the contraction. In 1936–1937, the Fed doubled member bank reserve requirements in three stages—August 1936, March 1937, and May 1937—aiming to curb perceived excess reserves and potential inflation, which reduced the money multiplier and led to a contraction in bank lending and the money supply. Concurrently, the Treasury began sterilizing gold inflows in late June 1936, preventing the automatic expansion of the monetary base that inflows would otherwise trigger under the gold standard regime; this policy sterilized over $300 million in gold by mid-1937, flattening monetary growth despite continued gold accumulation. Empirical analysis indicates that gold sterilization alone accounted for a significant portion of the monetary contraction, with the money stock declining by about 15 percent in 1937, exacerbating deflationary pressures and discouraging investment.

Fiscal measures under the Roosevelt administration compounded the monetary tightening. Concerned with persistent budget deficits, the administration reduced federal spending growth and introduced new payroll taxes for Social Security in January 1937, which increased labor costs and dampened consumer spending; net government contribution to income fell sharply, with federal outlays dropping by over 10 percent in real terms during 1938. These actions reflected a shift toward balancing the budget prematurely, as officials misinterpreted the recovery's strength and feared inflationary overheating despite underlying vulnerabilities like high debt levels and incomplete banking reforms. The combined effect undermined fragile private sector confidence, leading to inventory liquidation, reduced capital expenditures, and a credit crunch that halted the momentum of prior expansionary efforts.

## Private and Social Adaptations

### Business and Philanthropic Responses

In the immediate aftermath of the 1929 stock market crash, President Hoover convened business leaders in November 1929, securing pledges from major industrialists to maintain existing wage rates and avoid widespread layoffs to mitigate deflationary pressures and sustain consumer purchasing power. These commitments aligned with Hoover's economic philosophy emphasizing voluntary cooperation over government mandates, with some firms, such as those in manufacturing, initially adhering by implementing work-sharing arrangements—reducing weekly hours rather than dismissing workers outright, thereby preserving a portion of the labor force amid declining output. However, adherence proved uneven; nominal wages across industries fell by roughly 25-30% from 1929 to 1933 peaks, as market forces compelled adjustments to falling demand, though hourly rates in surviving jobs often held steadier than total pay due to shortened workweeks.

Philanthropic initiatives emphasized localized, voluntary aid, coordinated through entities like the President's Emergency Committee for Employment (PECE), formed on October 21, 1930, to rally private donations, community drives, and employer-led job creation without direct federal funding. PECE and its successor, the President's Organization on Unemployment Relief, facilitated campaigns that raised funds from corporations and affluent individuals, supplementing soup kitchens, shelters, and temporary employment projects run by charities such as the Red Cross, which allocated $5 million for Depression-era assistance by 1931. Industrialists contributed notably; Henry Ford sponsored two youth work camps to provide skills training and shelter for unemployed boys, while John D. Rockefeller Jr. advanced private funding for Rockefeller Center's construction, one of the era's few large-scale non-government projects, employing thousands during 1931-1934.

Early Depression relief relied predominantly on private and local sources, with 1932 expenditures from combined public and private channels totaling $317 million—equivalent to about $26 per unemployed person among the 12.5 million jobless—before federal programs scaled up post-1933. These efforts reflected a tradition of self-reliance, drawing on community networks and business philanthropy to address immediate needs, though their capacity strained as unemployment surpassed 20% by 1931, highlighting limits in sustaining aggregate demand without broader structural adjustments. Foundations like Rockefeller's continued targeted grants for public health and administration amid fiscal constraints, but overall private giving declined with donor incomes, underscoring philanthropy’s role as a bridge rather than a comprehensive solution.

### Labor Unrest and Unionization Efforts

The wave of labor unrest intensified in the mid-1930s amid partial economic recovery, as workers sought higher wages and job security in industries like trucking and steel. In 1934, the number of work stoppages reached 1,856, involving nearly 1.5 million workers and marking the highest annual total since systematic records began, driven by grievances over pay cuts and layoffs during the Depression. Notable was the Minneapolis Teamsters Strike, beginning May 16, 1934, where over 400 drivers demanded union recognition and better conditions from trucking firms; clashes culminated in "Bloody Friday" on July 20, when police fired on picketers, killing two strikers—John Bergh and Henry Ness—and injuring dozens, highlighting the era's violent confrontations between labor and authorities.

The strike peaked in 1937 with 4,740 work stoppages nationwide, involving about two million workers, as union organizing accelerated in mass-production sectors. The Little Steel Strike, launched March 13, 1937, by the Steel Workers Organizing Committee against U.S. Steel subsidiaries like Republic Steel, aimed at recognition and wage hikes; it turned deadly in the Memorial Day Massacre on May 30 in Chicago, where police killed ten unarmed strikers and wounded around 100 others outside a Republic plant, contributing to at least 18 total deaths and hundreds of injuries across the action, which ultimately failed to unionize Little Steel firms despite widespread shutdowns. These events underscored how unrest often escalated into physical confrontations, with strikers facing armed opposition from police and company guards, eroding public sympathy in some cases.

The National Labor Relations Act of 1935, enforced by the newly created National Labor Relations Board (NLRB), protected workers' rights to organize and bargain collectively, prohibiting employer interference like firings for union activity. NLRB rulings facilitated rapid union expansion, with membership rising from approximately 3 million in 1933 to over 9 million by 1939, tripling in key industries such as auto and steel through certified elections and back-pay awards. This growth enabled wage increases—evidenced by a structural shift linking higher industrial concentration to elevated pay scales around 1936–1937—but also correlated with rigid labor markets that some analyses link to slower hiring and prolonged unemployment, as elevated union wages exceeded marginal productivity in a depressed economy, prioritizing incumbent workers over new entrants. Critics, drawing from economic models of the era, argue such dynamics fostered cartel-like bargaining that hampered flexibility, though empirical studies affirm short-term gains in compensation for organized labor at the cost of broader employment fluidity.

### Migration, Homelessness, and Survival Strategies

The Dust Bowl, encompassing severe droughts and soil erosion in the Great Plains from 1930 to 1936, displaced hundreds of thousands of farmers and laborers from states including Oklahoma, Texas, Kansas, and Arkansas, driving them westward in search of work. Between 1935 and 1940, over 250,000 migrants from the Southwest, with a plurality from Oklahoma—derisively termed "Okies"—arrived in California, often traveling by overcrowded vehicles or hitchhiking along Route 66 to labor in fruit and vegetable fields. These migrants encountered low wages, poor living conditions in squatter camps, and local hostility, as California officials sought to restrict their influx amid existing labor surpluses.

In June 1932, approximately 43,000 World War I veterans and their families converged on Washington, D.C., as the Bonus Army, erecting encampments to protest the denial of early bonus payments authorized under the 1924 World War Adjusted Compensation Act. The group, largely peaceful and self-policed, symbolized broader displacement as unemployed ex-servicemen rode rails or walked from across the country, only to be evicted by U.S. Army forces on July 28 under orders from President Hoover, resulting in fires that destroyed their shelters.

Hoovervilles, sprawling shantytowns constructed from scrap materials like cardboard and tin, proliferated in urban peripheries, providing shelter for the homeless before significant federal relief emerged. The largest, in St. Louis, Missouri, accommodated 3,000 to 5,000 residents from 1930 to 1936, featuring rudimentary self-governance, including elected leaders and rules against crime, supplemented by private donations from churches and businesses. Similar settlements in Seattle and other cities relied on communal labor for sanitation and security, underscoring residents' adaptive organization amid municipal neglect.

## Societal Impacts

### Unemployment, Poverty, and Human Costs

Unemployment in the United States reached its nadir during the Great Depression, peaking at 24.9% of the civilian labor force in 1933, equivalent to approximately 12.8 million individuals out of work. This figure represented a sharp rise from pre-Depression levels, with joblessness surging due to industrial contraction and banking failures that curtailed credit and investment. Nominal wage rigidity exacerbated the crisis, as downward inflexibility in wages prevented necessary adjustments to restore employment equilibrium amid falling demand and prices.

Poverty manifested in widespread deprivation, with urban and rural households facing acute shortages of food and shelter; soup kitchens and makeshift encampments became common as private charity strained under the volume of need. Malnutrition affected significant portions of the population, particularly children, though aggregate mortality rates paradoxically declined overall due to reduced traffic accidents, workplace injuries, and possibly lower infectious disease transmission from curtailed social activity—except for suicides, which rose notably during the period. Suicide rates increased from about 17 per 100,000 in 1928 to peaks around 1932, correlating with economic despair and job loss.

Regional disparities amplified human suffering, with the Midwest's farming communities experiencing a 60% drop in income from collapsing crop prices and debt burdens predating the 1929 crash but intensified thereafter. Dust Bowl conditions in the Great Plains displaced thousands through soil erosion and drought, compounding poverty via failed harvests and forced migrations. These localized collapses underscored how sector-specific shocks, including agricultural overproduction and environmental degradation, sustained elevated unemployment beyond urban industrial woes.

### Demographic Shifts and Family Dynamics

The total fertility rate in the United States declined sharply during the Great Depression, dropping from approximately 3.0 births per woman in the 1920s to a low of 2.1 in 1936, reflecting economic uncertainty and reduced capacity to support additional children. This contraction in family size was driven by high unemployment and income instability, which discouraged childbearing even among married couples, as households prioritized immediate survival over expansion.

Marriage rates also fell precipitously, declining by about 20 percent from 1929 to 1933, as young adults postponed weddings amid job scarcity and financial hardship. Divorce rates followed a similar pattern, decreasing by 25 percent between 1929 and 1933, with economic constraints—such as the inability to afford separate households—binding couples together despite relational strains. These trends contributed to a stabilization of existing family units but at the cost of delayed family formation, as prospective spouses weighed the risks of starting households in a depressed economy.

Female labor force participation saw a modest increase during the 1930s, with the number of employed women rising 24 percent from 10.5 million in 1930 to 13 million in 1940, often through an "added worker" effect where wives or daughters entered low-wage jobs to offset male unemployment. This shift subtly altered family dynamics, as women's contributions to household income grew, though cultural norms and discriminatory policies limited their access to better-paying roles. Overall, these changes fostered more contracted nuclear families, with fewer children and delayed expansions, as economic pressures reshaped traditional structures toward austerity and mutual dependence.

### Racial and Gender Disparities

African Americans faced unemployment rates significantly higher than the national average during the Great Depression, often exceeding 50% in urban areas by 1931 while white unemployment reached 31.7%. This disparity stemmed from widespread "last hired, first fired" practices, where employers prioritized white workers amid economic contraction, exacerbating prior racial barriers in industrial and service sectors. In the rural South, the collapse of sharecropping systems further displaced black tenant farmers, as falling cotton prices and mechanization reduced demand for labor; by the mid-1930s, over 100,000 black sharecroppers had been evicted from plantations.

New Deal programs amplified these inequities in some cases, particularly through the Agricultural Adjustment Act (AAA) of 1933, which paid landowners to reduce crop production and acreage. White landowners frequently used these subsidies to evict black tenants and sharecroppers rather than share payments, leading to the displacement of an estimated 200,000 black agricultural workers between 1930 and 1940. Access to federal relief work, such as under the Works Progress Administration, was also uneven; in the South, local administrators often allocated fewer jobs to blacks, with only about 34% of unemployed southern blacks receiving aid compared to higher rates for whites.

Women experienced heightened vulnerability in the labor market, with many shifting to informal or low-wage roles like domestic service and garment work, where pay averaged 20-40% less than male-dominated industries. Married women's workforce participation remained low at under 10% entering the decade, but economic desperation prompted an "added worker" effect, increasing employment among young and single women to supplement family incomes amid male job losses. Black women, concentrated in domestic and laundry work, faced compounded discrimination; their unemployment rates mirrored broader black trends, often twice the white female average, with many exiting the labor force as discouraged workers by the late 1930s. Relief programs provided limited support for women-headed households, as eligibility criteria and local biases favored male breadwinners, leaving about 1.3 million female sole supporters without adequate aid.
