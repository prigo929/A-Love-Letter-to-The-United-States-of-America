# Colonial History

## Introduction

The colonial history of the United States refers to the period of European settlement and development in North America by primarily British colonists from the establishment of Jamestown in 1607 to the Declaration of Independence in 1776, during which thirteen self-governing colonies emerged along the Atlantic seaboard, laying the foundations for American institutions through economic expansion, religious experimentation, and territorial conflicts. These colonies, spanning from New Hampshire in the north to Georgia in the south, were chartered by the English crown and driven by motives of profit, evangelism, and strategic rivalry with other European powers such as Spain, France, and the Netherlands, resulting in a population growth from a few thousand settlers to over 2.5 million by 1775 through natural increase and immigration.

Regional variations defined colonial society: New England colonies like Massachusetts emphasized Puritan communalism and small-scale farming, fostering town meetings that presaged representative governance; Middle colonies such as Pennsylvania attracted diverse ethnic groups with fertile soils supporting grain exports; while Southern colonies like Virginia relied on cash crops such as tobacco and rice, sustained by indentured servitude evolving into chattel slavery imported from Africa, which by the mid-18th century comprised about 20% of the population in those areas. Interactions with indigenous populations involved initial trade and alliances but increasingly violent displacement, as European diseases decimated native numbers—reducing them by up to 90% in some regions—and settler expansion provoked wars like King Philip's War (1675–1678), enabling colonial land acquisition through military superiority and legal claims under doctrines of discovery.

Economically, the colonies achieved self-sufficiency via mercantilist ties to Britain, exporting raw materials like timber and furs while importing manufactured goods, though smuggling and local manufacturing grew amid Navigation Acts restrictions, contributing to rising autonomy and resentment over taxation without representation by the 1760s. Defining characteristics included the diffusion of English common law, Protestant work ethic, and Enlightenment ideas, which supported literacy rates exceeding 70% in New England and innovations in agriculture, yet controversies arose from internal inequalities, such as elite land monopolies and the entrenchment of racial slavery as a labor system justified by pseudoscientific racial hierarchies, setting precedents for future divisions. This era's legacy encompasses both the pragmatic adaptation to a harsh frontier—yielding resilient institutions—and the causal roots of indigenous subjugation and enslaved labor, driven by demographic pressures and resource competition rather than abstract ideologies.

## Motivations and Early European Ventures

### Mercantilist Economic Imperatives

Mercantilism dominated European economic thought from the late 16th to the 18th century, emphasizing state-directed policies to achieve a positive balance of trade, accumulate bullion reserves, and enhance national power through colonial expansion. Under this system, colonies were regarded as appendages of the mother country, compelled to export raw materials at low cost while importing finished goods, thereby subsidizing metropolitan industries and naval strength. European powers justified territorial claims in North America as strategic necessities for monopolizing lucrative commodities, with policies like export bounties and import restrictions enforcing dependency; for instance, Britain's Navigation Acts of 1651 onward mandated that colonial goods be shipped only in British vessels to designated ports, channeling wealth toward the crown.

Spain's mercantilist pursuits in the New World prioritized the extraction of precious metals, fueling initial explorations into territories that later bordered the United States. Following Columbus's voyages in 1492, conquistadors like Hernán Cortés and Francisco Pizarro amassed vast silver shipments from mines in Mexico and Peru, with annual fleets from 1561 transporting over 180 tons of silver to Seville by the early 1600s, bolstering Spain's Habsburg treasury but distorting its domestic economy through inflation. In North American outposts such as Florida and the Southwest, Spanish efforts focused on securing trade routes and mission economies to support this bullion flow, though yields were modest compared to southern empires, underscoring the imperative to control Atlantic shipping lanes against interlopers.

English and French ventures in eastern North America adapted mercantilism to regional resources, prioritizing staples like timber, fish, and furs to offset Europe's deficits. England's colonial charters, such as Virginia's in 1606, explicitly aimed to counterbalance imports by developing naval stores and cash crops; by 1660, tobacco exports from the Chesapeake reached 18 million pounds annually, funneled through London under enumerated lists to maximize duties. France, granting monopolies like those to the Compagnie des Cent-Associés in 1627 for New France, centered on the fur trade, where beaver pelts from the Great Lakes and Mississippi Valley supplied European hatters; annual exports peaked at 200,000 pelts by the mid-1700s, sustaining outpost networks amid sparse settlement. These imperatives not only drove settlement but also sparked rivalries, as each power enforced exclusions to preserve trade asymmetries.

### Religious and Ideological Drivers

The Spanish Crown's colonization of territories that would become parts of the United States, such as Florida and the Southwest, was propelled by a fervent Catholic missionary imperative rooted in the Reconquista's completion in 1492 and papal authorizations like the 1493 Inter Caetera bull, which granted Christian monarchs rights to claim and evangelize non-Christian lands. This ideological framework framed indigenous peoples as souls in need of salvation, justifying conquest as a divine duty; missionaries accompanied expeditions to convert natives, as seen in early Florida outposts established by Pedro Menéndez de Avilés in 1565, where Franciscan friars aimed to supplant perceived paganism with Catholicism. The dual goal of spiritual redemption and territorial pacification intertwined, with missions serving as bases for both conversion and colonial control, though native resistance and cultural clashes often undermined these efforts.

In contrast, English Protestant settlers emphasized separation from the established Church of England amid intensifying religious conflicts under James I and Charles I. The Pilgrims, a Separatist faction of Puritans numbering about 102 aboard the Mayflower, arrived at Plymouth in December 1620 after fleeing persecution in Leiden, Holland, driven by a desire to worship without state interference and establish a covenant-based community modeled on biblical principles. Their Mayflower Compact formalized self-governance under God's law, reflecting an ideological commitment to congregational autonomy over hierarchical Anglicanism. This separatist zeal contrasted with non-separating Puritans, who sought to reform the Church from within but similarly viewed New England settlement as a providential errand to build a "city upon a hill" free from corruption.

The Great Migration of Puritans, peaking between 1630 and 1642 with over 20,000 arrivals to Massachusetts Bay Colony under John Winthrop, amplified these drivers, motivated by fears of spiritual decay in England and a millenarian vision of America as a site for pure Reformed Christianity. Ideologically, both groups subscribed to a Protestant ethic tying personal piety to communal prosperity, interpreting survival amid hardships—like the Pilgrims' first winter mortality rate exceeding 50%—as tests of faith, while dismissing Catholic missions as idolatrous. French Jesuit efforts in northern regions, though secondary to economic aims, echoed Catholic zeal through missions like those among the Hurons starting in 1615, prioritizing conversion over large-scale settlement. These religious motivations, often fused with notions of European civilizational superiority, provided moral legitimacy for displacement of natives, though empirical outcomes revealed limited conversions and frequent syncretism rather than wholesale transformation.

### Spanish Exploration and Initial Conquests

Spanish exploration of North America commenced in the early 16th century, motivated by the pursuit of gold, slaves, and strategic expansion following successes in the Caribbean and Mesoamerica. Expeditions targeted regions now comprising the southeastern and southwestern United States, often involving armed forces that clashed with indigenous populations. These ventures yielded limited immediate conquests but mapped territories claimed for the Spanish Crown, enduring native resistance, logistical failures, and high mortality rates from disease and starvation.

Juan Ponce de León, appointed governor of Puerto Rico, led the first documented European expedition to the North American mainland, departing San Juan on March 3, 1513, with three ships and about 200 men. The fleet made landfall on April 2 or 3 near present-day St. Augustine, Florida, which Ponce named La Florida on account of its lush vegetation during Easter season (Pascua Florida). He formally claimed the territory for Spain, seeking gold and rumored rejuvenating waters, though primary accounts emphasize resource extraction over mythical pursuits. Encounters with Timucua and Calusa peoples involved skirmishes, with the expedition capturing natives for enslavement before withdrawing due to hostile resistance and supply shortages; Ponce abandoned further immediate colonization efforts.

In 1528, Pánfilo de Narváez commanded a larger force of five ships and approximately 400 men, landing near Tampa Bay in April to explore and conquer interior regions for gold and settlement. Ordered by Charles V to establish colonies, Narváez's overland party of 300, including horses and supplies, separated from the ships, leading to navigational failures and attacks by Apalachee warriors. Starvation and drownings decimated the group during a desperate coastal trek in makeshift barges; Narváez perished at sea, and only four survivors, including Álvar Núñez Cabeza de Vaca, reached Mexico after years among indigenous groups, providing early ethnographic accounts but no territorial gains. The expedition's collapse highlighted the perils of underestimating terrain and native alliances.

## Early Failures and Experimental Settlements

### Lost Colony of Roanoke and English Setbacks

The English crown granted Sir Walter Raleigh a patent in 1584 to explore and settle territories in North America, leading to initial reconnaissance voyages to Roanoke Island off present-day North Carolina. That year, ships commanded by Philip Amadas and Arthur Barlowe surveyed the region, reporting fertile lands and cooperative native inhabitants under Chief Wingina, though these accounts emphasized potential for profit in commodities like sassafras rather than verified sustainability. These optimistic dispatches prompted further investment but overlooked logistical challenges such as supply chains across the Atlantic.

In 1585, Raleigh dispatched a military expedition of approximately 107 men under Ralph Lane to establish a fort on Roanoke, accompanied by artists and scientists including John White and Thomas Harriot, who documented flora, fauna, and native customs. The settlers, focused on exploration and defense rather than agriculture, quickly exhausted supplies and clashed with local Secotan and Croatoan tribes after disputes over food tribute, culminating in the killing of Wingina in 1586. Rescued by Sir Francis Drake's fleet amid a hurricane, the survivors abandoned the site, highlighting early English vulnerabilities to native hostilities, inadequate provisioning, and failure to adapt to local ecology.

Raleigh then shifted to a civilian model, sending 118 settlers—men, women, and children—in 1587 under John White as governor, intending relocation to the Chesapeake but landing at Roanoke due to pilot Simon Fernandes's insistence. The group included White's daughter Eleanor Dare and her husband Ananias; their daughter Virginia Dare, the first English child born in the Americas, arrived on August 18, 1587. Facing immediate supply shortages and native tensions following the prior expedition's conflicts, the colonists urged White to return to England for relief, which he did on August 27, leaving them with limited provisions and instructions to signal distress via a cross if abandoning the site.

### Spanish Perseverance in Hostile Environments

Despite repeated setbacks from indigenous resistance, tropical diseases, starvation, and logistical failures, Spanish explorers mounted successive expeditions into the southeastern North American interior, demonstrating institutional commitment to territorial claims under the requerimiento doctrine and mercantilist imperatives. Juan Ponce de León's 1513 voyage, departing Puerto Rico on March 3 with three ships and approximately 200 men, made landfall near present-day St. Augustine between April 2 and 3, charting over 600 miles of coastline and claiming La Florida for Spain amid encounters with hostile Calusa and Timucua peoples who repelled settlement attempts with archery and ambushes. A 1521 follow-up effort by Ponce, involving 200 settlers and provisions for a permanent outpost, collapsed after Calusa warriors wounded him mortally with poisoned arrows, forcing evacuation to Cuba.

Pánfilo de Narváez's 1528 incursion exemplified the perils of overland traversal in Florida's swampy terrain and hurricane-prone coasts; landing near Tampa Bay in April with 400 men, horses, and five ships, the force splintered after Narváez ordered ship abandonment, leading to inland marches plagued by famine, native attacks, and sepsis, with only four survivors—Álvar Núñez Cabeza de Vaca among them—reaching Mexico after eight years of captivity and wandering. Hernando de Soto's 1539–1543 campaign, launching from Cuba on May 18 with 600 Europeans, 213 horses, and swine herds, penetrated deep into the Mississippi Valley, clashing with Apalachee, Cofitachequi, and Choctaw forces in battles like Mabila (October 1540), where Spanish steel and armor prevailed but at costs exceeding 200 casualties from combat, fever, and desertion; de Soto perished from illness in 1542, and fewer than 300 returned to Spain, having unwittingly disseminated smallpox and measles that decimated native populations by 90% in some regions.

These expeditions' high attrition—often 80–95% mortality—stemmed from underestimating the Southeast's causal challenges: dense subtropical forests impeding supply lines, venomous fauna, and unified indigenous coalitions leveraging numerical superiority and terrain knowledge, as chronicled in survivor accounts emphasizing Spanish overreliance on coerced native porters. Yet Crown-backed persistence, fueled by papal bulls granting exclusive dominion and fears of French encroachment, culminated in Pedro Menéndez de Avilés's 1565 founding of San Agustín (St. Augustine) on September 8, with 2,000 soldiers constructing fortifications against storms and Timucua raids; this outpost endured sieges and supply shortages through naval resupply from Havana, marking the first enduring European foothold north of Mexico by prioritizing defensive presidios over extractive conquest. Such resilience reflected Spain's state apparatus—integrating military, ecclesiastical, and administrative hierarchies—contrasting ad hoc private ventures elsewhere, enabling gradual consolidation amid adversity.

## Spanish Colonial Domains

### Florida and Southeastern Outposts

Spanish exploration of Florida began with Juan Ponce de León's expedition in 1513, during which he landed near present-day St. Augustine, claimed the region for Spain, and named it La Florida. Subsequent ventures, including Pánfilo de Narváez's 1528 expedition, ended in disaster with shipwrecks and few survivors, yielding minimal colonization but revealing the peninsula's challenging terrain and hostile indigenous resistance. Hernando de Soto's 1539–1543 expedition marked a deeper incursion, landing near Tampa Bay in May 1539 with over 600 men, 200 horses, and swine; it traversed the Southeast, wintering among the Apalachee near present-day Tallahassee in 1539–1540, where forces raided native villages for food and slaves, contributing to significant indigenous mortality from violence and introduced diseases, though no permanent settlements resulted.

Permanent Spanish presence solidified in 1565 under Pedro Menéndez de Avilés, who founded San Agustín (St. Augustine) on September 8 after landing at the Timucua village of Seloy, establishing it as a military outpost to secure treasure fleets and counter French incursions. This followed Menéndez's destruction of the French Huguenot Fort Caroline in September 1565 and the subsequent massacre of over 200 French shipwreck survivors at Matanzas Inlet, actions driven by royal orders to expel Protestant rivals and assert Catholic dominance. St. Augustine served as the capital and primary presidio, fortified with wooden stockades initially and later stone structures, supporting garrisons of 100–300 soldiers amid subsistence agriculture and trade with missions.

Southeastern outposts expanded through Franciscan missions established from the late 16th century, targeting Timucua, Guale, and Apalachee peoples to facilitate conversion, labor extraction, and buffer against northern threats. By the 1650s, over 30 missions dotted the landscape, including San Buenaventura de Potano in Timucua territory (north-central Florida), Santa Catalina de Guale on the Georgia coast, and San Luis de Apalachee near Tallahassee, where indigenous communities produced maize, cattle, and deerskins for Spanish use, with mission populations peaking at around 26,000 natives by 1680 before epidemics and warfare reduced them. These outposts relied on native caciques' cooperation but enforced tribute and relocation, sparking revolts like the 1597 Guale uprising, which destroyed 10 missions before Spanish reprisals restored control.

### Southwest Missions and Pueblos

The Spanish colonization of the Pueblo region in present-day New Mexico began with Juan de Oñate's expedition departing Mexico in early 1598, comprising approximately 400 colonists, including soldiers, settlers, and Franciscan friars tasked with evangelizing indigenous populations. Oñate's forces crossed the Rio Grande near present-day El Paso in May 1598 and established the first permanent Spanish settlement at the Tewa Pueblo of Ohke, renamed San Juan de los Caballeros, where friars initiated baptisms and rudimentary mission activities among the estimated 60,000-80,000 Pueblo inhabitants across roughly 100 villages. This marked the onset of a mission system integrating religious conversion with Spanish governance, where Franciscans sought to supplant Pueblo polytheistic practices—centered on kachina spirits and agricultural rites—with Catholicism, often through coercion tied to the encomienda labor system.

By the 1620s, Franciscan efforts expanded, with permanent missions constructed at key pueblos such as San Lorenzo de Picurís in 1629 and others among the Tiwa, Tewa, and Keresan groups, totaling around 30 missions serving about 90 villages by mid-century. These adobe structures, often built atop or adjacent to kivas (Pueblo ceremonial chambers), functioned as centers for mass baptisms—numbering tens of thousands by 1630—while enforcing Spanish cultural assimilation, including bans on traditional dances and feather use, which friars viewed as idolatry. Secular authorities extracted tribute in corn, cotton, and labor for mining and farming, exacerbating tensions amid environmental stressors like the megadrought of 1630-1680, which halved Pueblo populations through famine and disease to roughly 17,000 by 1680.

Cumulative grievances—enforced conversions, crop confiscations, and vulnerability to Apache raids—culminated in the Pueblo Revolt of August 10, 1680, orchestrated by Popé of San Juan Pueblo, uniting 17 pueblos in a synchronized uprising that killed 21 Franciscan missionaries and 376 other Spaniards, destroying 40 churches and expelling survivors southward to El Paso del Norte. The rebels dismantled mission infrastructure and revived kachina cults, achieving the only successful indigenous expulsion of European colonizers in North America, sustaining autonomy for 12 years.

### Pacific Coast Extensions

Spanish exploration of the Pacific Coast began in 1542 when Juan Rodríguez Cabrillo, commanding two ships under the Spanish flag, sailed northward from Baja California, reaching San Diego Bay on September 28 and continuing to the Oregon coast before winter storms forced a return. Cabrillo's voyage marked the first documented European contact with Alta California but yielded no settlements due to hostile native encounters and navigational challenges.

In 1602–1603, Sebastián Vizcaíno's expedition remapped the coastline, renaming San Diego Bay and identifying Monterey as a potential harbor, though persistent native resistance and logistical difficulties delayed colonization efforts for over 150 years. Vizcaíno's detailed charts informed later ventures, but Spain prioritized interior conquests until geopolitical pressures from Russian fur traders in the north prompted renewed interest in securing the coast.

The pivotal push came with the 1769 Portolá expedition, dispatched from Baja California under Governor Gaspar de Portolá and Franciscan missionary Junípero Serra to establish presidios and missions. Arriving at San Diego on July 1 after a grueling sea and overland journey involving 90 soldiers, 100 mules, and thousands of livestock, the group founded Mission San Diego de Alcalá on July 16—the first permanent European settlement in Alta California. Portolá then led an overland party north, reaching Monterey Bay in May 1770 and discovering San Francisco Bay on November 2, 1769, while Serra established Mission San Carlos Borroméode Carmelo as the expedition's northern anchor.

## French Colonial Enterprises

### Northern Frontiers and Fur Trade

The French fur trade propelled colonial expansion into the northern frontiers, known as the pays d'en haut or Upper Country, encompassing the Great Lakes and Upper Midwest regions that would later form parts of the United States. Driven by European demand for beaver pelts used in hat-making, this commerce became the economic foundation of New France, funding further exploration, missionary activities, and sparse settlements. French traders, operating from bases like Quebec established in 1608, pushed westward via river and lake routes, relying on alliances with Indigenous groups such as the Hurons and Algonquins to access fur-rich territories.

Early explorations laid the groundwork for sustained trade networks. In 1634, Jean Nicolet, dispatched by Samuel de Champlain, reached the Green Bay area of present-day Wisconsin, becoming the first European documented to enter the region. Accompanied by seven Hurons and Winnebagos, Nicolet sought a route to Asia but instead initiated diplomatic and trade contacts, demonstrating firearms and wearing silk robes to impress locals, which facilitated initial exchanges of furs for European goods. These ventures, amid the Beaver Wars where Iroquois disrupted Huron-French supply lines in the 1640s-1650s, underscored the trade's volatility and the necessity of military support for commercial routes.

By the late 17th century, the French established a chain of fortified trading posts across the Great Lakes to secure the interior trade. Notable among these was Fort Pontchartrain du Détroit, founded in 1701 by Antoine de la Mothe Cadillac at the site of modern Detroit, serving as a hub for pelts from the upper lakes. Other key outposts included Fort Michilimackinac on the straits between Lakes Michigan and Huron around 1671, Fort St. Joseph in 1691, and Fort La Baye (Green Bay) formalized in 1717. These posts, often manned by small garrisons of soldiers and traders, facilitated the annual transport of tens of thousands of pelts—peaking at over 22,000 beavers in 1626—via canoe brigades of voyageurs who paddled birch-bark craft loaded with goods and returns.

### Mississippi Valley and Louisiana Territory

In 1673, Jesuit missionary Father Jacques Marquette and fur trader Louis Jolliet led an expedition from Michilimackinac, traveling via Green Bay and the Fox and Wisconsin rivers to the upper Mississippi, confirming its connection to the Gulf of Mexico but turning back near the Arkansas River due to Spanish presence risks. Their journey mapped key waterways, facilitating future French penetration into the interior for trade and missions.

René-Robert Cavelier, Sieur de La Salle, built on this in 1682 by descending the Ohio and Mississippi rivers to the Gulf of Mexico with a party including Henri de Tonti, formally claiming the vast drainage basin—spanning from the Great Lakes to the Gulf and east-west between the Appalachians and Rockies—as La Louisiane for France under Louis XIV. La Salle established temporary forts like Fort Prud'homme near Memphis and attempted a settlement at Fort St. Louis on the Illinois River, though his 1684 Gulf colony in Texas failed disastrously. These efforts asserted nominal French sovereignty but yielded limited permanent control amid Native hostilities and logistical strains.

French administration intensified after 1699 under Pierre Le Moyne d'Iberville's Gulf expeditions, with posts like Fort Maurepas (Biloxi, 1699) and Mobile (1702) serving as early capitals. In 1718, Jean-Baptiste Le Moyne de Bienville founded New Orleans on a Mississippi crescent bend, 95 miles from the Gulf, as the strategic hub for trade and defense, incorporating levees and grid planning amid swampy terrain. The Louisiana Territory, granted to the Company of the Indies in 1717, encompassed roughly 828,000 square miles but featured sparse settlement—peaking at under 10,000 Europeans by 1760—concentrated along rivers.

## Minor European Powers' Attempts

### Dutch New Netherland

The Dutch exploration of the North American Atlantic coast began with Henry Hudson's 1609 voyage commissioned by the Dutch East India Company, during which he sailed up the river now named after him, establishing early trading contacts with indigenous groups and laying the basis for Dutch territorial claims in the region. In 1614, Dutch explorer Adriaen Block mapped the area and applied the name New Netherland to the territory encompassing parts of present-day New York, New Jersey, Delaware, and Connecticut, initially focused on fur-trading outposts rather than large-scale settlement. The Dutch West India Company, chartered in 1621 to manage Atlantic trade and colonization, assumed control of these efforts by 1623, directing the construction of Fort New Amsterdam on Manhattan Island between 1625 and 1626 as a defensive trading hub.

New Amsterdam, the colony's administrative center, was formally organized in 1625 when Director Peter Minuit arrived with settlers, including Walloons from present-day Belgium and France, and reportedly purchased Manhattan Island from local Lenape groups in 1626 for goods valued at 60 guilders—equivalent to about 24 dollars at the time—though the transaction's legal nature under indigenous land concepts remains debated among historians due to differing cultural understandings of property. The colony's economy centered on the fur trade, particularly beaver pelts obtained from Algonquian and Iroquoian tribes like the Mohawks, who secured a near-monopoly by controlling interior supply routes; between 1626 and 1632, Dutch exports of furs alone generated significant revenue, underscoring the trade's primacy over agriculture or other pursuits. By 1630, the European population numbered around 300, growing slowly to a peak of approximately 9,000 by the 1660s, with diverse settlers including Dutch Calvinists, Germans, Scandinavians, and enslaved Africans imported for labor in farms and construction. Governance under the West India Company emphasized commercial priorities, with directors like Willem Kieft (1638–1647) and Peter Stuyvesant (1647–1664) wielding autocratic authority, often prioritizing profit extraction over settler welfare or indigenous diplomacy.

Relations with Native American groups deteriorated into violence, exacerbated by trade disputes, land encroachment, and cultural clashes. Kieft's War (1643–1645) erupted when Kieft ordered preemptive attacks on Lenape and Wappinger communities in response to refugee influxes from Mohawk raids, resulting in hundreds of indigenous deaths and retaliatory strikes that devastated Dutch outposts, ending only with a fragile peace treaty in 1645. The Peach War of 1655 followed a disputed killing over stolen peaches, drawing in Munsee and Susquehannock warriors who briefly occupied New Amsterdam and raided settlements, killing dozens and capturing over 100 captives before a Dutch counteroffensive restored control. Subsequent Esopus Wars (1659–1663) in the Hudson Valley further strained resources, as Dutch forces under Stuyvesant subdued Lenape and Esopus groups through fortified campaigns, but these conflicts highlighted the colony's military vulnerabilities and reliance on indigenous alliances for fur procurement.

### Swedish Delaware River Settlements

The colony of New Sweden was founded in 1638 by the New Sweden Company, a venture backed by Swedish, Dutch, and German investors seeking to establish a trading outpost along the Delaware River to compete in the fur trade. Peter Minuit, previously director of New Netherland, led the initial expedition aboard the ships Kalmar Nyckel and Fogel Grip, arriving at Delaware Bay in March 1638 and proceeding upriver to "The Rocks" near present-day Wilmington, Delaware, where Fort Christina was constructed as the first permanent settlement. The Swedes purchased approximately 67 miles of land from Lenape chiefs to legitimize their claim, fostering initial amicable relations that facilitated trade in furs, corn, and other goods.

Expansion occurred under subsequent governors, with Peter Hollander Ridder arriving in 1640 to reinforce Fort Christina and extend territorial purchases northward along the river's western bank, encompassing areas from modern Philadelphia to New Castle, Delaware. Johan Printz, governor from 1643 to 1653, oversaw significant development, establishing his residence at New Gothenburg on Tinicum Island, constructing Fort Elfsborg at the bay's entrance to deter Dutch incursions, and Fort Korsholm at the Schuylkill River confluence. The colony's economy centered on fur trading with the Lenape and Susquehannock peoples, supplemented by tobacco cultivation and rudimentary agriculture, with Finnish settlers—comprising up to 22% of the population—introducing techniques like log cabin construction and slash-and-burn forestry for tar production. Population estimates peaked at around 400 inhabitants in the mid-1640s, though the settlement remained modest, relying on intermittent reinforcements.

Challenges mounted due to supply shortages, with no ships arriving between 1648 and 1654 amid Sweden's European wars, leading to population decline to about 130 by 1653, exacerbated by desertions, harsh conditions, and internal dissent against Printz's authoritarian rule. Johan Risingh's arrival in 1654 with new colonists briefly revitalized efforts, including the capture of the Dutch-held Fort Casimir (renamed Fort Trefaldighet), but provoked retaliation from New Netherland's director Peter Stuyvesant. In September 1655, Stuyvesant led a force of 317 men and seven vessels, capturing Fort Christina and other outposts by September 15, compelling Swedish surrender after minimal resistance. The conquest ended Swedish sovereignty after 17 years, though many colonists were permitted to remain as Dutch subjects, later integrating under English control following the 1664 takeover of New Netherland; descendants preserved cultural elements, such as Lutheran churches, in the region.

### Russian Alaskan Enclaves

Russian exploration of Alaska began with Vitus Bering's expeditions under Tsar Peter the Great. In 1728, Bering sailed through what became the Bering Strait but did not sight the North American mainland. A subsequent voyage in 1741, part of the Great Northern Expedition, saw Bering and Aleksei Chirikov reach the Alaskan coast and Aleutian Islands, confirming the separation of continents and sparking interest in fur resources.

Following these voyages, Russian promyshlenniki—independent fur hunters—ventured into the region from Siberia starting in the 1740s, initiating mass hunting of sea otters and other furbearers whose pelts fetched high prices in China. This trade drove the establishment of the first permanent Russian settlement in 1784 at Three Saints Bay on Kodiak Island by Grigorii Shelikhov, who built a fort and trading post while subjugating local Alutiiq people through force and hostage-taking to secure labor for hunting. By the 1790s, settlements expanded to Unalaska in the Aleutians and other Kodiak sites, with Russian forces compelling Aleuts to hunt under duress, leading to population declines from disease, overwork, and violence.

In 1799, Tsar Paul I chartered the Russian-American Company as a monopoly to administer Russian America, granting it rights to trade, governance, and colonization from the Aleutians to California. Headquartered initially at Kodiak and later at New Archangel (Novo-Arkhangelsk, modern Sitka) after its founding in 1799 and fortification following a 1804 battle against Tlingit resistance, the company oversaw a network of enclaves focused on extraction rather than large-scale settlement. Russian personnel numbered only about 800 by the 1840s, supplemented by several thousand native and Creole (mixed Russian-native) workers; the company promoted Orthodox Christianity through missions but prioritized profits, exporting furs valued at millions of rubles annually in peak years.
