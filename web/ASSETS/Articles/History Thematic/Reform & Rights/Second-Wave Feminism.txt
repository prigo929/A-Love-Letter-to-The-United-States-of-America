# Second-Wave Feminism

## Introduction

Second-wave feminism refers to the feminist activism and ideology that emerged in the United States during the early 1960s and persisted into the 1980s, primarily seeking to dismantle legal barriers to women's equality in employment, education, and reproduction while challenging traditional gender roles in family and society.Key organizational efforts included the founding of the National Organization for Women (NOW) in 1966 by figures such as Betty Friedan, whose book The Feminine Mystique (1963) critiqued the dissatisfaction of middle-class housewives, galvanizing broader participation. Legislative successes encompassed the Equal Pay Act of 1963, which aimed to prohibit wage discrimination based on sex, and Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972, mandating equal access to educational programs receiving federal funding, though enforcement and outcomes varied. The 1973 Supreme Court decision in Roe v. Wade legalized healthcare debate nationwide, representing a major victory for reproductive autonomy, yet it highlighted divisions within the movement over strategy and scope.

Despite these advances, second-wave feminism faced substantial criticisms for its predominant focus on white, middle-class concerns, often marginalizing women of color, working-class individuals, and those from lower socioeconomic strata, as evidenced by the systemic exclusion documented in analyses of its organizational dynamics and policy priorities. Internal debates intensified over issues like pornography and sexuality, splitting advocates into anti-pornography factions emphasizing harm to women and sex-positive groups defending personal agency, which fragmented coalitions by the late 1970s. Empirical data on societal impact reveal mixed results; while female labor force participation rose significantly—from about 38% in 1960 to over 50% by 1980—gender wage gaps narrowed modestly, with full-time women earning 72% of men's wages by 1990, but progress stalled thereafter amid persistent occupational segregation. Scholarly assessments, often produced within ideologically aligned academic environments prone to left-leaning biases, tend to emphasize empowerment narratives while underplaying causal factors like economic shifts and technological changes that independently advanced women's opportunities. Ultimately, the movement's legacy includes expanded legal protections but also unintended consequences, such as heightened family instability correlated with rising divorce rates post-no-fault laws influenced by feminist advocacy.

## Definition and Historical Context

### Temporal Boundaries and Distinction from Other Waves

Second-wave feminism is generally dated from the early 1960s to the early 1980s. The movement's onset is often traced to 1963, marked by the publication of Betty Friedan's The Feminine Mystique, which critiqued the dissatisfaction of middle-class housewives and catalyzed broader discussions on gender roles, alongside the enactment of the Equal Pay Act that year. Its decline accelerated in the late 1970s amid internal divisions over issues like pornography and sexuality, culminating in the failure of the Equal Rights Amendment (ERA) to achieve ratification by its 1982 deadline, after which momentum shifted toward subsequent phases.

In distinction from the first wave, which primarily targeted legal and political inequalities such as women's suffrage—achieved in the United States via the 19th Amendment in 1920—second-wave feminism extended advocacy into social, cultural, and personal spheres. First-wave efforts, spanning the late 19th to early 20th centuries, focused on formal rights and were largely led by middle-class white women seeking access to education, property, and voting. Second-wave activists, building on these foundations, challenged pervasive inequalities in workplaces, reproductive rights, domestic labor, and family structures, emphasizing the slogan "the personal is political" to link private experiences with systemic patriarchy.

Unlike the third wave, which emerged in the early 1990s and prioritized intersectionality—integrating race, class, and sexuality with gender—and individual empowerment through reappropriation of femininity and sexuality, the second wave maintained a more collective focus on universal gender equality often critiqued for its initial emphasis on white, middle-class concerns. Third-wave feminism reacted against perceived second-wave rigidities by embracing diversity, ambiguity in gender expression, and cultural critiques, while second-wave efforts centered on institutional reforms like anti-discrimination laws and challenging traditional roles without substantial early attention to intersecting oppressions. This periodization, while useful, is not without debate among historians, who note overlaps and continuities rather than strict delineations between waves.

### Core Tenets and Objectives

Second-wave feminism advanced the tenet that women should attain substantive equality with men, addressing not only legal formalities but also entrenched social, economic, and cultural barriers that perpetuated female subordination. This wave emphasized critiquing and reforming patriarchal structures in institutions such as the workplace, education, and family, where women faced discrimination and limited opportunities despite postwar economic expansions. A foundational critique came from Betty Friedan's 1963 book The Feminine Mystique, which identified the "problem that has no name"—widespread dissatisfaction among educated middle-class housewives confined to domestic roles, arguing that true fulfillment required pursuits beyond homemaking and motherhood.

Core objectives centered on economic independence and workplace equity, including equal pay for equal work and elimination of sex-based hiring barriers, as formalized in demands by the National Organization for Women (NOW), established on June 30, 1966, to enforce Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 against employment discrimination. The movement sought broader access to higher education and professional fields, challenging quotas and stereotypes that restricted women to traditionally female occupations. Reproductive autonomy formed another pillar, with goals to secure legal access to contraception and healthcare debate, enabling women to control fertility and participate fully in public life without mandatory childbearing.

The tenets also included recognizing personal grievances—such as domestic violence and unequal household labor—as manifestations of systemic sexism, addressed through consciousness-raising groups that fostered collective awareness and action. Objectives extended to reforming family laws to ensure equitable divorce settlements, child custody, and spousal support, aiming to dismantle legal dependencies that reinforced women's subordination. While branches diverged—liberal feminists focusing on integration into existing systems and radicals on uprooting patriarchy—shared aims prioritized empirical evidence of disparities, like wage gaps where women earned 59 cents to a man's dollar in 1963, to justify reforms.

## Origins and Catalysts

### Postwar Societal Shifts and The Feminine Mystique

Following World War II, millions of American women who had entered the workforce in defense industries and other sectors during the war were systematically displaced to accommodate returning servicemen, with federal policies and societal expectations promoting a return to traditional domestic roles. Labor force participation rates for women dropped sharply from wartime peaks, as employers and government campaigns urged women to vacate jobs for men, reinforcing the ideal of women as homemakers focused on child-rearing and household management. This shift coincided with the baby boom, spanning 1946 to 1964, during which approximately 76 million children were born in the United States, amplifying cultural pressures for women to prioritize family over personal ambition.

In the 1950s, economic prosperity and suburban expansion further entrenched the "feminine mystique," a pervasive cultural narrative propagated through media, advertising, and psychology that portrayed fulfillment for women exclusively through marriage, motherhood, and consumerism within the nuclear family. Marriage rates reached historic highs, with the median age at first marriage falling to 20.8 for women by 1956, while women's college enrollment increased but often led to limited career prospects beyond teaching or nursing, fostering widespread but unarticulated dissatisfaction among educated housewives. Retrospective surveys indicate that about half of married women employed in 1950 had worked prior to 1940, yet postwar norms suppressed broader workforce re-entry, contributing to a sense of isolation and purposelessness in suburban settings.

Betty Friedan's The Feminine Mystique, published on February 19, 1963, by W.W. Norton & Company, crystallized this discontent by identifying what she termed "the problem that has no name"—a pervasive ennui and identity crisis among middle-class American women confined to domesticity despite material comforts. Drawing from interviews with her 1942 Smith College classmates and critiques of influential thinkers like Sigmund Freud and post-Freudian theorists who pathologized women's ambitions outside the home, Friedan argued that the mystique stifled women's potential, advocating for education, work, and self-realization as antidotes. The book, which sold over 1.3 million copies in its first few years, challenged the era's complacency by exposing how magazines and experts had idealized homemaking while ignoring women's intellectual and professional aspirations.

### Emergence of Key Organizations and Consciousness-Raising

The National Organization for Women (NOW) was founded on June 30, 1966, by 28 women, including Betty Friedan and Pauli Murray, at the Third National Conference of Commissions on the Status of Women held in Washington, D.C. from June 28 to 30. This event, attended by hundreds of representatives, focused on actionable strategies to address sex discrimination, prompting the group's formation as an alternative to the limited scope of government commissions. NOW's founding statement emphasized enforcing Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 to eliminate employment discrimination against women, alongside goals for equal opportunities in education and politics.

Parallel to liberal organizations like NOW, radical feminist groups developed grassroots methods emphasizing personal testimony. New York Radical Women (NYRW), established in 1967, introduced consciousness-raising (CR) as a core practice, where small groups of 8-12 women met weekly to discuss topics such as marriage, work, and sexuality, aiming to uncover shared experiences of oppression. CR sessions followed guidelines to prioritize speaking from personal experience without debate or advice, fostering the realization that individual issues stemmed from systemic patriarchy—a concept later formalized in slogans like "the personal is political."

By 1969, groups like Redstockings, formed in New York City, refined CR techniques, advocating for its use in public speak-outs, such as the 1969 healthcare debate hearings where women testified directly about their experiences rather than relying on male experts. These practices proliferated across the U.S., with thousands of CR groups by the early 1970s, serving as both organizational hubs and tools for ideological development in radical feminism. While effective in mobilizing women and challenging privatized notions of gender inequality, CR's emphasis on consensus and experiential validation sometimes prioritized subjective narratives over broader empirical scrutiny.

## Ideological Branches

### Liberal Feminism

Liberal feminism emerged as a primary ideological branch of second-wave feminism, emphasizing the achievement of gender equality through targeted legal, political, and institutional reforms within the liberal democratic system. Proponents argued that barriers to women's advancement stemmed from discriminatory laws and practices rather than inherent societal structures, advocating for equal opportunities in employment, education, and public life via incremental changes such as anti-discrimination legislation and affirmative action policies. This approach prioritized individual rights and merit-based access, contrasting with more transformative visions by focusing on integration into existing institutions rather than their fundamental restructuring.

The National Organization for Women (NOW), founded on June 30, 1966, by 28 activists including Betty Friedan, Pauli Murray, and Aileen Hernandez during the Third National Conference of State Commissions on the Status of Women, exemplified liberal feminist organizing. NOW's inaugural Statement of Purpose, adopted on October 29, 1966, demanded "action to bring women into full participation in the mainstream of American society now, assuming all the privileges and responsibilities thereof in truly equal partnership with men," targeting enforcement of Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 to eliminate sex-based employment discrimination. Under Friedan's first presidency, NOW grew to over 300 chapters by 1968, lobbying for workplace equity, credit access for women, and child care provisions.

Betty Friedan, whose 1963 book The Feminine Mystique documented the alienation of educated housewives confined to domestic roles, catalyzed liberal feminist discourse by framing women's discontent as a crisis solvable through expanded roles in paid work and public spheres. Friedan co-founded NOW to translate these critiques into advocacy for legal equality, including support for the Equal Rights Amendment and healthcare debate rights as means to personal autonomy, though she later critiqued what she saw as excessive focus on sexuality over economic issues in the movement. Her work influenced policy pushes like the 1970 congressional hearings on women's economic status, which highlighted wage gaps—women earned 59 cents for every dollar men earned in 1960—and spurred reforms.

### Radical Feminism

Radical feminism, a prominent ideological branch of second-wave feminism, crystallized in the United States during the late 1960s, positing patriarchy—defined as systemic male dominance over women—as the foundational and universal source of female oppression, superseding economic or racial factors. Proponents argued that this structure manifests in institutions like marriage, heterosexuality, and reproduction, necessitating not incremental reforms but a complete overthrow to achieve liberation. Emerging from disillusionment with the male-dominated New Left and civil rights movements, radical feminism rejected liberal integrationist strategies, instead advocating separatism, direct action, and cultural subversion to dismantle male supremacy.

Formative groups such as New York Radical Women, cofounded in 1967 by Shulamith Firestone and Pam Allen, exemplified early activism through high-profile disruptions, including the 1968 protest against the Miss America pageant, where participants symbolically discarded bras and other items to critique women's objectification as livestock. Successor organizations like Redstockings, established in 1969, and New York Radical Feminists further advanced tactics such as public speak-outs on healthcare debate and rape, framing these as political manifestations of patriarchal violence rather than isolated crimes. Consciousness-raising (CR) sessions, pioneered by Kathie Sarachild in 1968 within Redstockings, served as a core method: small, women-only groups shared intimate experiences—from menstruation to marital coercion—to uncover patterned oppressions, operationalizing the mantra "the personal is political" without deference to experts or therapeutic norms. These practices, drawn partly from civil rights organizing, prioritized non-hierarchical dialogue to build revolutionary solidarity.

Theoretical foundations were laid in landmark texts, including Firestone's The Dialectic of Sex (1970), which traced women's subjugation to biological imperatives of pregnancy and childcare, proposing cybernetic womb technology and the abolition of the nuclear family to sever "love's" tyrannical bonds. Kate Millett's Sexual Politics (1970) complemented this by analyzing canonical literature—from Henry Miller to Freud—as propaganda for patriarchal sex roles, contending that erotic dominance enforces political subjugation and that patriarchy predates capitalism as the original class system. These works influenced campaigns against pornography and prostitution, viewed as extensions of rape culture, with later radicals like Andrea Dworkin asserting in the 1980s that heterosexual intercourse itself embodies occupation, though such absolutist claims diverged from earlier emphases on collective restructuring.

### Socialist and Other Variants

Socialist feminism emerged within second-wave feminism as a critique of both liberal feminism's focus on legal equality within capitalist structures and radical feminism's emphasis on patriarchy as the sole root of oppression. Proponents contended that women's subordination arises from the intersection of patriarchal relations and capitalist production, where unpaid domestic labor reproduces the workforce essential to capitalism, rendering full gender liberation contingent upon dismantling class exploitation. This perspective drew on Marxist analysis but insisted on elevating gender dynamics as analytically co-equal to economic ones, rejecting economistic reductions that subsumed women's issues under class struggle.

Key initiatives included the Wages for Housework campaign, launched by Selma James in 1972 at a UK women's liberation conference and formalized internationally by 1975 through publications like the Wages for Housework Notebooks, which demanded remuneration for domestic and caring labor to expose its role in subsidizing capital accumulation. Influential theorists like Heidi Hartmann, in her 1979 essay "The Unhappy Marriage of Marxism and Feminism," critiqued orthodox Marxism for marginalizing patriarchy as a secondary phenomenon, advocating instead a dual-systems theory where household production under male control parallels but operates semi-autonomously from wage labor. Organizations such as the Chicago Women's Liberation Union, active from 1969 to 1977, embodied this approach by integrating socialist analysis with grassroots activism on issues like healthcare debate and workplace discrimination, asserting that reforms alone could not eradicate intertwined oppressions.

Marxist feminism, often overlapping with socialist variants, prioritized historical materialism to explain how capitalism appropriates women's reproductive labor—childbearing, housework, and socialization—as unwaged value creation, distinct from but enabling surplus extraction in the public sphere. Figures like Silvia Federici extended this in campaigns against primitive accumulation's gendered impacts. Other strands, such as cultural feminism, diverged by valorizing innate female differences and separatism over class analysis, promoting woman-centered spirituality and critiques of male-dominated rationality, though these were sometimes contested as essentialist within broader second-wave debates.

## Major Initiatives and Legal Achievements

### Workplace and Economic Reforms

Second-wave feminists prioritized eliminating sex-based wage disparities and employment barriers, advocating for legislation that mandated equal compensation for equivalent roles and prohibited discrimination in hiring, promotion, and workplace conditions. The Equal Pay Act of 1963, signed into law by President John F. Kennedy on June 10, required employers to provide equal pay to men and women performing substantially equal work under similar conditions, addressing overt pay discrimination prevalent in industries where women earned approximately 59 cents for every dollar paid to men in 1963. This act built on earlier advocacy but marked a direct response to documented wage inequities highlighted in reports from the President's Commission on the Status of Women.

The Civil Rights Act of 1964 further advanced these goals through Title VII, which banned employment discrimination on the basis of sex alongside race, color, religion, and national origin, covering private employers with 15 or more employees. Enacted on July 2, 1964, and enforced by the newly created Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC), Title VII targeted practices such as sex-segregated job classifications, mandatory retirement ages differing by sex, and discriminatory policies on marriage or pregnancy, which had previously confined women to lower-paying roles. Early enforcement was uneven, with the EEOC initially reluctant to prioritize sex discrimination, prompting feminist activism to demand rigorous implementation.

The National Organization for Women (NOW), founded on June 30, 1966, emerged as a key driver of workplace reforms, explicitly committing in its Statement of Purpose to secure for women "full participation in the mainstream of American society" by enforcing Title VII and challenging barriers to economic equality. NOW organized protests, filed complaints with the EEOC, and lobbied for stronger protections, contributing to increased female labor force participation, which rose from 37.7% in 1960 to 51.3% by 1980 according to Bureau of Labor Statistics data. These efforts correlated with expanded access to professional fields, though persistent occupational segregation and the unadjusted gender wage gap—hovering around 60 cents on the dollar in the 1970s—highlighted limitations of equal-pay-for-equal-work mandates, as they did not address undervaluation of female-dominated occupations.

### Reproductive Rights and Family Law Changes

Second-wave feminists advocated for expanded access to contraception, culminating in the 1965 Supreme Court decision in Griswold v. Connecticut, which invalidated state bans on contraceptive use by married couples under a right to marital privacy derived from the Bill of Rights. This ruling built on earlier efforts by birth control advocates, including Connecticut feminists inspired by Margaret Sanger, who challenged restrictive laws through organizations like the Connecticut Birth Control League. The decision facilitated broader distribution of devices and information previously limited by statutes in 24 states as of 1965.

The right extended to unmarried individuals via Eisenstadt v. Baird in 1972, enabling feminists to frame contraception as essential to women's autonomy over reproduction and career pursuits, aligning with campaigns for safer, more accessible methods amid the 1960s introduction of the birth control pill. Abortion rights became a core focus, with activists forming groups like the National Association for the Repeal of Abortion Laws (NARAL) in 1969 to lobby for legalization. The 1973 Roe v. Wade decision legalized healthcare debate nationwide in the first trimester, influenced by second-wave litigation and protests that highlighted illegal procedures' risks, with estimates of 200,000–1.2 million annual clandestine healthcare debates pre-ruling. This was paired with state-level reforms; by 1970, four states had liberalized laws following feminist advocacy.

In family law, second-wave efforts targeted inequities in divorce, property division, and marital obligations, though feminists were not primary drivers of no-fault divorce adoption. California's 1969 no-fault law, signed by Governor Ronald Reagan, eliminated fault-based grounds like adultery or cruelty, simplifying proceedings and spreading to all states by 1985; studies link it to reduced female suicide rates (8–16% drop) and intimate partner violence by easing exits from unhappy marriages. While women's groups supported these changes for empowering women economically, the reforms originated from judicial overload concerns rather than feminist initiatives, with data showing divorce rates tripling from 2.2 per 1,000 in 1960 to 5.2 in 1980.

### Educational and Public Access Gains

Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972, enacted on June 23, 1972, prohibited discrimination on the basis of sex in any education program or activity receiving federal financial assistance, marking a pivotal legal advancement for women's access to educational opportunities. This legislation addressed longstanding barriers, including quotas limiting female admissions to universities and sex-segregated vocational training, thereby facilitating greater female participation in higher education and public schooling. Implementation extended to public institutions, reducing discriminatory practices in course offerings and extracurricular activities, though enforcement challenges persisted due to varying institutional compliance.

Women's college enrollment rates rose substantially during the second wave, with the proportion of female undergraduates increasing from approximately 39% in 1970 to 49% by 1980, reflecting expanded access amid declining gender gaps in immediate post-high-school enrollment. For instance, the female immediate college enrollment rate among high school completers climbed from 31% in 1961 to higher figures by the late 1970s, correlating with Title IX's removal of overt barriers like admission preferences for males. These gains were uneven, with greater progress in coeducational public universities than in elite private institutions, and primarily benefited white women, as racial minorities faced intersecting discriminations.

The emergence of women's studies programs further institutionalized feminist perspectives in academia, with the first such program established at San Diego State University in 1970, followed by rapid proliferation to hundreds of campuses by the early 1980s. These interdisciplinary initiatives integrated gender analyses into curricula, challenging male-centric syllabi and promoting research on women's historical and social roles, though critics later noted their potential for ideological uniformity over empirical breadth. Public access gains extended beyond campuses, as Title IX influenced equitable treatment in public libraries and community education programs, diminishing restrictions on women's participation in adult literacy and extension courses.

## Cultural and Social Ramifications

### Transformations in Gender Roles and Family Dynamics

Second-wave feminism critiqued the traditional division of labor in which women were expected to prioritize homemaking and child-rearing, advocating instead for economic independence and shared domestic responsibilities. This ideological shift, articulated in works like Betty Friedan's The Feminine Mystique (1963), contributed to broader cultural reevaluation of gender norms, encouraging women to pursue careers outside the home. Empirical data reflect this transformation: the female labor force participation rate in the United States rose from 37.7% in 1960 to 43.3% in 1970 and 51.5% by 1980, driven in part by feminist campaigns for workplace equality and access to education. By the late 1970s, dual-earner married-couple families had increased to approximately 52% of households, up from 44% in 1967, with median incomes for such families exceeding $23,000 in 1978—about 20% higher than single-earner counterparts.

These changes altered family dynamics, fostering a move toward egalitarian ideals but also correlating with instability. No-fault divorce laws, adopted starting with California in 1969 and spreading nationwide by the mid-1970s amid feminist support for easier marital dissolution, facilitated a surge in divorces; the U.S. crude divorce rate climbed from 2.2 per 1,000 people in 1960 to 5.2 by 1980. Studies attribute part of this rise to shifting gender norms that reduced tolerance for unequal partnerships, though economic pressures and legal reforms were concurrent factors. Cohabitation and remarriage rates also increased from the 1960s onward, reflecting diminished emphasis on lifelong monogamous marriage as the normative family structure.

Fertility patterns shifted similarly, with the total fertility rate declining from 3.65 births per woman in 1960 to 1.77 by 1980, alongside rising mean age at first marriage (from 20.3 for women in 1960 to 22.0 in 1980) and higher proportions of never-married adults. Feminist emphasis on reproductive autonomy and career prioritization over early childbearing influenced these trends, as evidenced by cohort analyses linking egalitarian gender attitudes to delayed family formation. While proponents viewed these developments as liberating, critics, including some demographers, note correlations with reduced family cohesion and population-level fertility below replacement levels, though disentangling feminist ideology from broader socioeconomic drivers like contraceptive availability remains challenging.

### Impacts on Sexuality, Media, and Arts

Second-wave feminists sought to liberate female sexuality from patriarchal constraints, promoting the idea that the personal is political, as articulated in manifestos like the 1968 "No More Fun and Games" conference proceedings. This led to advocacy for women's sexual autonomy, including widespread support for the birth control pill's approval in 1960 and subsequent cultural shifts toward premarital sex and experimentation, with surveys showing a rise in reported female sexual partners from an average of 2.2 in 1960 to 4.3 by 1975 among college-educated women. However, internal divisions emerged during the "sex wars" of the late 1970s and 1980s, where radical feminists like Andrea Dworkin argued that heterosexuality under patriarchy constituted institutionalized subordination, viewing pornography not as liberation but as a blueprint for violence against women, influencing ordinances in cities like Minneapolis in 1983 to classify porn as sex discrimination.

In media, second-wave activism critiqued portrayals that objectified women, such as advertising's emphasis on domesticity and beauty, prompting the formation of independent outlets like Ms. magazine in 1972, which reached 500,000 subscribers by 1973 and challenged male-dominated narratives by featuring stories on equal pay and reproductive rights. Mainstream media responded unevenly; while feminist influence contributed to shows like The Mary Tyler Moore Show (1970–1977), which depicted single career women without traditional marriage plots, news coverage often framed the movement negatively, with analyses of 1968–1982 U.S. and U.K. newspapers revealing dominant themes of extremism and family disruption rather than substantive policy gains.

The arts saw the rise of a feminist movement that confronted male-centric canons, with initiatives like the 1971 "Womanhouse" collaborative installation in Los Angeles, organized by Judy Chicago and Miriam Schapiro, transforming a derelict house into spaces exploring domestic drudgery and bodily experiences through installations like the "Menstruation Bathroom." Key works included Chicago's The Dinner Party (1974–1979), a triangular table honoring 39 historical women with vulvar motifs, viewed by over 1 million people upon its 1979 debut and sparking debates on essentialism versus universality in art. Artists such as Cindy Sherman, through her Untitled Film Stills series (1977–1980), deconstructed media stereotypes of femininity, while performance pieces by Carolee Schneemann, like Interior Scroll (1975), asserted female eroticism against abstract expressionist norms, contributing to increased female representation in galleries from under 10% in 1970 to 30% by 1980.
