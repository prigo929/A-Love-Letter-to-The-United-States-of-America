# The New Great Migration

## Introduction

The New Great Migration is the demographic reversal in which African Americans have increasingly relocated from the Northeast, Midwest, and West back to the Southern United States since the 1970s, countering the northward exodus of the original Great Migration from 1910 to 1970. This trend has elevated the share of the Black population in the South from 53 percent in 1970 to 57 percent in 2020, with recent data indicating that 94 percent of national Black population growth occurred in the region between July 2020 and July 2021.

Primarily propelled by economic expansion in Southern metropolitan areas, including job opportunities in sectors like technology, finance, and logistics, alongside lower living costs and strengthened family networks, the migration disproportionately involves younger, college-educated individuals originating from non-Southern states. Key destinations include Atlanta, Georgia; Houston and Dallas, Texas; and Charlotte, North Carolina, where Black population growth has outpaced national averages, fostering urban revitalization and influencing regional political dynamics through heightened Black voter concentrations. This shift underscores the South's modernization, with improved infrastructure and diminished overt racial barriers post-Civil Rights era contributing to its appeal over deindustrializing Northern cities. While smaller in absolute scale than its predecessor, the New Great Migration represents a sustained realignment driven by market incentives and personal affinities rather than escape from systemic oppression.

## Historical Context

### Origins in the Post-Civil Rights Era

The dismantling of legal segregation through the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 marked a pivotal shift, eroding the Jim Crow system's enforcement of racial barriers in the South and enabling African Americans to reassess migration patterns without the constant threat of legalized discrimination and disenfranchisement. These legislative changes, by federally prohibiting discrimination in public accommodations, employment, and voting, reduced the structural incentives that had propelled the original Great Migration northward from 1910 to 1970, where over 6 million blacks fled violence, sharecropping exploitation, and limited opportunities. Early evidence of reversal appeared in the 1970s, as desegregation facilitated returns driven by kinship networks and diminishing northern industrial appeal, with census surveys showing small net black gains to the South throughout the decade.

U.S. Census Bureau data confirmed this trend's acceleration between 1975 and 1980, when the South achieved a net black in-migration of 108,000 individuals, primarily from the Northeast and Midwest, reversing decades of out-migration and signaling the New Great Migration's onset. This shift reflected causal improvements in southern conditions post-desegregation, including safer public spaces and reduced extralegal intimidation, which lowered the perceived risks of relocation for economic reconnection to family roots and nascent regional growth.

Atlanta emerged as an early focal point, bolstered by black political gains such as the 1973 election of Maynard Jackson as the first African American mayor of a major southern city, which amplified local empowerment and attracted returnees through expanded business access and community leadership. Jackson's administration prioritized affirmative contracting, fostering black economic footholds that complemented the post-civil rights erosion of exclusionary practices, drawing initial waves to the city as a symbol of viable southern reintegration. These developments laid the groundwork for broader reversal, distinct from northern deindustrialization's push factors, by emphasizing pull dynamics rooted in reformed legal environments.

### Distinctions from the Original Great Migration

The original Great Migration, spanning roughly 1910 to 1970, involved the departure of approximately 6 million African Americans from the South to northern, midwestern, and western urban centers, primarily as a response to systemic violence, economic exploitation under sharecropping, and legal segregation. In contrast, the New Great Migration represents a partial reversal of this outflow, with the share of the Black population residing in the South rising modestly from 53% in 1970 to 57% by 2020, reflecting net in-migration alongside natural population growth rather than a comparably massive exodus. This shift has been smaller in relative scale, increasing the Southern Black population's proportion by only about 4 percentage points over five decades, compared to the original migration's drastic reduction of the South's Black share from over 90% in 1910 to 53% by 1970.

Unlike the original movement, which was largely propelled by immediate survival imperatives amid overt racial terror and restricted opportunities, the New Great Migration is characterized by voluntary choices oriented toward economic and professional advancement. Participants are disproportionately younger and higher-skilled, with college-educated Black individuals leading the influx into Southern states such as Georgia, Texas, and Maryland, often prioritizing career agency over mere escape. Data indicate that more educated Black migrants now move to Southern destinations at rates exceeding those with lower education levels, marking an upwardly mobile demographic profile absent in the earlier migration's predominantly low-wage labor seekers.

Economically, the New Great Migration aligns with pull factors rooted in the South's accelerated growth, including the Sun Belt's expansion, where regional economic output has surpassed the Northeast's after decades of divergence driven by business relocations and infrastructure development. This contrasts with the original migration's push dynamics, as Southern states have benefited from policies fostering lower operational costs and amenities like milder climates, enabling GDP per capita in Sun Belt areas to outpace northern counterparts since the late 20th century. Such developments have inverted earlier regional disparities, attracting skilled Black professionals to opportunities in expanding sectors rather than repelling them through coercion.

## Primary Causes

### Economic Incentives

Southern states have leveraged right-to-work laws, which prohibit compulsory union membership, to foster business-friendly environments that attract manufacturing and other industries, creating job opportunities that draw Black migrants from Northern cities. All Southern states maintain such legislation, enabling greater operational flexibility for employers and lower labor costs compared to union-dense regions in the Northeast and Midwest, where unionization rates average higher and contribute to slower manufacturing recovery. This policy framework has supported a manufacturing resurgence, including foreign direct investment in automotive production; for instance, Alabama's Mercedes-Benz plant in Tuscaloosa, opened in 1997, has generated thousands of jobs and spurred supplier networks employing diverse workforces, including Black workers in a state where manufacturing output grew 25% from 2010 to 2020.

Lower corporate and personal tax structures further incentivize relocation, particularly in states like Texas and Florida, which impose no state income tax on wages, allowing skilled workers to retain more earnings amid comparable or rising job availability in tech, finance, and logistics. These fiscal advantages have accelerated job growth in high-skill sectors; Texas added over 1.2 million jobs from 2010 to 2020, with sectors like professional services expanding rapidly, while Florida's no-income-tax policy correlates with net domestic migration gains of 1.1 million residents in the same period, including disproportionate Black inflows tied to employment hubs. Corporate tax rates in the South average below the national mean, at around 5-6% effective rates in states like Georgia and North Carolina, versus 8-10% in many Northern states, drawing relocations such as fintech firms to Atlanta.

In urban centers like Atlanta, the Black middle class has expanded since the 1990s through influxes of tech and finance positions, with the metro area now hosting the highest concentration of Black tech professionals nationwide and adding over 40,000 professional jobs from 2010 to 2020. This growth reflects deregulated entrepreneurship barriers in the South, where lower regulatory hurdles and union avoidance enable small business formation rates 20% above Northern averages, providing Black entrepreneurs access to capital and markets less encumbered by collective bargaining mandates. Median Black household incomes in Southern metros have shown sharper increases than in the North, rising 15-20% in cities like Atlanta and Houston from 2010 to 2019, driven by these sectoral expansions.

### Quality of Life and Cost Factors

Housing affordability constitutes a major draw for migrants in the New Great Migration, with median home values in Southern metros like Houston ($324,000 in 2023) and Atlanta ($398,000) often 20-40% below equivalents in Northern cities such as Chicago ($346,000) and Detroit ($225,000, though with higher surrounding metro costs due to urban decay). These differences persist despite national price pressures, enabling greater access to single-family homes and suburban living for middle-class Black families fleeing dense, high-cost Northern urban cores. Complementing this, Southern states maintain lower effective property tax rates—averaging 0.4% to 0.6% in places like Alabama and Texas, versus 1.5% to 2.2% in Illinois and Michigan—which preserve disposable income and support homeownership without proportionally higher service burdens.

Violent crime disparities further incentivize relocation, as FBI Uniform Crime Reporting data reveal rates in Rust Belt hubs like Detroit (1,781 per 100,000 residents in recent analyses) and Baltimore exceeding those in Southern peers such as Dallas (863 per 100,000) and Houston (1,075 per 100,000), though both regions show variability. Chicago's rate hovers around 540-900 per 100,000 depending on reporting periods, still prompting outflows amid perceptions of unchecked urban violence linked to policy environments favoring reduced enforcement and high welfare inflows that correlate with dependency cycles. These safety concerns drive families toward Southern areas offering relatively stable neighborhoods, where lower overall cost of living indices—10-20% below Northern averages—facilitate investments in private security and community amenities.

Broader quality of life enhancements, including milder climates and expanded green spaces in Sun Belt suburbs, amplify these economic pulls, though empirical studies attribute much of the migration to escaping Northern policy-induced fiscal strains like elevated sanctuary-related expenditures and inefficient public services that inflate living expenses without commensurate benefits. Overall cost comparisons underscore Southern metros' edge, with composite indices placing states like Georgia and Texas at 88-92 versus Illinois' 94.5, allowing migrants to achieve higher standards of living on equivalent incomes.

### Social and Familial Pulls

A significant driver of the New Great Migration involves reconnection with ancestral roots and extended family networks, particularly among second- and third-generation descendants of the original Great Migration. Surveys of return migrants indicate that family ties play a central role, with a 1973 study in Birmingham, Alabama, finding that more than half of respondents relocated for familial reasons, often to reunite with relatives or care for aging parents. This pattern persists in contemporary analyses, as Black Americans from deindustrialized Northern cities cite the desire to strengthen kinship bonds and support multi-generational family structures, which have become more prevalent in Southern states amid rising elderly populations and caregiving needs.

Cultural affinity for Southern social environments further bolsters these familial pulls, as migrants express preference for the region's communal norms over the perceived isolation and transience of Northern urban life. Demographers note that established Black communities in the South provide a sense of familiarity and mutual support, drawing younger professionals and retirees alike through personal and cultural ties to hometowns or ancestral regions. These decisions reflect pragmatic assessments of social embeddedness rather than mere nostalgia, with migrants leveraging family and community networks to navigate life transitions, countering narratives in certain media sources that frame Southern returns as retreats to backward conditions.

Higher rates of Black church attendance in the South, which correlate with migration choices per demographic studies, underscore the role of longstanding community institutions in fostering these pulls, though economic factors remain distinct. Overall, such non-material incentives have contributed to net Black population gains in the South, with Census data showing reversals from outflows of over 287,000 African Americans in 1965–1970 to inflows of nearly 350,000 by 1995–2000, sustained into the 21st century.

## Demographic Profile

### Characteristics of Migrants

Migrants in the New Great Migration are primarily younger Black Americans, with notable inflows from those aged 16 to 26 documented between 2000 and 2018 using Census Bureau migration flow data. This contrasts sharply with the original Great Migration (1910–1970), which drew predominantly rural, unskilled laborers escaping Southern oppression; the new wave consists of urban-oriented individuals making voluntary choices based on contemporary opportunities.

Education levels among these migrants are markedly higher, with college-educated Black adults spearheading the movement; for instance, net migration rates for Black college graduates to the South reached 23.9 per 1,000 adults from 1995 to 2000, outpacing less-educated cohorts. American Community Survey data from 2016–2020 further indicate disproportionate representation of college graduates among recent movers, reflecting a selective influx that exceeds the education profiles of stationary Black populations in both origin and destination regions.

These movers typically fall into middle-income brackets and include professionals leveraging skills in expanding sectors, drawn to Southern metropolitan areas with robust professional networks. Origins extend beyond traditional Northern states to include Western regions, such as California, signaling a broader "exodus" pattern among educated cohorts seeking affordability and growth. Family units are common, often motivated by kinship ties and lifestyle improvements, though the flow encompasses diverse household types from young singles to empty-nest retirees.

### Scale and Trends in Migration Data

U.S. Census Bureau data indicate that the proportion of the Black population residing in the South rose from 53% in 1970 to 57% in 2020, driven by net domestic migration inflows reversing prior outflows.

Net migration gains for the South accelerated in the 1990s, with the region recording a net inflow of 346,546 Black persons by 2000, compared to 179,975 by 1990. These trends persisted into the 2000s, with record levels of Black migration to Southern states as documented in Census analyses.

Interstate migration flows, as tracked by the Census Bureau, reveal significant reversals: outflows from high-population Black states like New York and Illinois to growth hubs in Georgia, Texas, and Florida, contributing to regional population redistribution.

## Geographic Patterns

### Leading Southern Destinations

The principal metropolitan destinations for Black migrants in the New Great Migration are concentrated in the Sun Belt states, particularly Atlanta, Houston, Dallas-Fort Worth, and Charlotte, where Black population inflows have driven local demographic expansions. Atlanta's metro area recorded Black population growth exceeding 1 million residents between 2000 and 2020, with the total surpassing 2.3 million by the 2020 Census, reflecting sustained net migration tied to expanded transportation infrastructure like Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport, the world's busiest by passenger volume.

Houston and Dallas-Fort Worth metros in Texas have similarly boomed, each gaining over 125,000 net Black residents in the 2010-2020 decade alone, fueled by metro-area infrastructure projects such as highway expansions and port developments accommodating energy-sector hubs. Texas as a state saw its Black population rise by approximately 964,000 from 2000 to 2020, from 2.4 million to 3.4 million. Charlotte, North Carolina, followed closely with a Black population increase of 121,889 over the two decades prior to 2019, linked to banking-sector-driven urban investments in uptown revitalization.

These patterns highlight distinctions between Sun Belt boomtowns like Houston and Dallas-Fort Worth, which leverage energy and logistics infrastructure for rapid absorption of inflows, and traditional Deep South hubs like Atlanta in Georgia, where state-level Black population growth reached about 964,000 over the same 2000-2020 period, from 2.3 million to 3.3 million, amid suburban tract developments. Overall, these metros accounted for a disproportionate share of the South's 94% capture of national Black population growth during peak migration years like 2020-2021.

### Shifts in Origin Regions

The New Great Migration has been characterized by significant net outflows of Black Americans from traditional Northern and Midwestern industrial centers, reversing the patterns of the original Great Migration. Between 2000 and 2019, the share of the national Black population residing in the Midwest declined from 19% to 17%, while the Northeast saw a drop from 18% to 17%, reflecting slower growth and net domestic out-migration amid broader population increases elsewhere. Specific cities like Chicago and Detroit experienced sharper absolute declines, with Chicago's Black population falling from approximately 1.065 million in 2000 to 802,000 in 2020—a roughly 25% reduction—and Detroit's from 775,000 to 515,000, a 33% drop, driven by net migration losses to Southern destinations. These outflows from states such as Illinois, Michigan, New York, and New Jersey accounted for a substantial portion of the cross-regional reversal, with American Community Survey (ACS) data indicating consistent net domestic migration of Black residents away from the Northeast and Midwest toward the South throughout the 2010s.

Deindustrialization in these origin regions played a causal role in accelerating the shifts, as the loss of manufacturing jobs in the 1970s and beyond eroded economic bases in areas like Chicago and Detroit, prompting Black households to seek opportunities elsewhere. By the 2010-2020 period, ACS migration flows confirmed the reversal, with the Midwest and Northeast registering negative net Black migration balances annually, contributing to the South's Black population share rising to 57% by 2020.

Post-2010, the Western United States emerged as an additional source of outflows, particularly from California (including Los Angeles and San Francisco) and Washington, where high living costs and stagnant job growth for Black workers fueled net migration to the South, supplementing the longstanding Northern drains. While intra-Southern moves also occurred, the dominant trend involved cross-regional reversals from non-Southern origins, with the Northeast and Midwest continuing to lose 20-30% of their Black population shares in key urban areas over the decade. This pattern underscores a broader reorientation away from legacy industrial hubs toward Southern economic hubs, as evidenced by sustained negative net flows in ACS county-to-county data.

## Economic Consequences

### Growth in Southern Metros

The influx of Black migrants during the New Great Migration has bolstered the labor force in Southern metropolitan areas, contributing to employment gains in sectors like finance, technology, and energy. In cities such as Charlotte, North Carolina, and Raleigh, North Carolina, Black professionals have driven job growth, with Charlotte ranking among the top destinations for Black workers due to expansions in banking and tech industries. Similarly, Atlanta has emerged as a primary magnet, with its Black population expanding five-fold from 1970 to 2020, providing a demographic dividend of skilled workers that supports metro-wide economic activity.

This migration correlates with accelerated GDP growth in Southern states, where per capita output has risen faster than the national average in recent years, as tracked by the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA). For instance, states like Texas and Florida—key recipients of Southern-bound migrants—recorded real GDP increases exceeding national trends in the second quarter of 2025, with the region's Sun Belt metros accounting for much of the nation's population and firm relocations. BEA data indicate that Southern metros, benefiting from inbound labor, have seen their share of U.S. GDP climb, with 90.8% of national output concentrated in metro economies as of 2024.

Policies in no-income-tax Southern states, such as Texas and Florida, have amplified these effects by attracting migrants and fostering business starts, thereby expanding the tax base through higher property values and sales tax revenues without corresponding spikes in welfare expenditures. Black entrepreneurship has surged in these areas, with migrants launching ventures at rates supporting local booms; for example, Black-owned employer businesses nationwide grew from 2.2% of all firms in 2017 to 3.3% in 2022, with disproportionate gains in Southern hubs like Atlanta, where approximately 37% of local businesses are Black-owned. This influx of productive workers and owners has yielded net fiscal positives, as evidenced by lower per capita public spending pressures in growing metros compared to stagnant Northern counterparts.

### Resource Drains from Northern Areas

The outmigration of Black residents from Northern cities has contributed to demographic hollowing, particularly in long-standing urban centers like Chicago and Detroit. In Chicago, the Black population declined by about 25% from 2000 to 2019, reflecting a net loss driven by domestic relocation to Southern states. Similarly, Detroit experienced a sustained exodus, with the city's Black population falling from over 775,000 in 2000 to around 500,000 by 2020, a drop exceeding 35% amid broader depopulation trends. These shifts have elevated vacancy rates in affected neighborhoods; for instance, Detroit's overall housing vacancy hovered above 20% in the 2010s, correlating with concentrated Black population losses that reduced neighborhood densities and intensified maintenance burdens on remaining infrastructure.

Such population outflows impose fiscal strains by eroding the tax base while fixed service costs persist. In cities like Chicago, the departure of working-age Black residents—often numbering in the tens of thousands annually—has diminished property and income tax revenues, with analyses indicating that net losses since 2000 equate to forgone contributions from over 200,000 individuals. Detroit's municipal budget has faced analogous pressures, as the Black middle class, which led the exodus post-2000, typically generates disproportionate per-capita revenue through higher earnings and homeownership. This results in elevated per-resident spending on public services like schools and transit, where enrollees and users decline faster than administrative overhead, perpetuating cycles of deferred maintenance and service cuts.

The selective nature of this migration amplifies resource drains, as empirical patterns show out-migrants from Northern cities to the South tend to be positively selected on socioeconomic traits. Data on reverse flows indicate that movers often possess above-average education and income relative to stayers, with Black households relocating southward exhibiting median earnings 10-20% higher than those remaining in origin metros. Consequently, sender cities retain higher concentrations of poverty and dependency, where post-outmigration Black poverty rates in places like Chicago and Detroit have stagnated above 25-30%, sustaining demand for welfare and social services without corresponding revenue growth. This self-selection dynamic, rooted in causal factors like job prospects and family ties, leaves behind structurally challenged populations, hindering fiscal recovery and exacerbating urban decay.
