# Energy in the United States

## Introduction

Energy in the United States refers to the extraction, production, distribution, and consumption of primary energy sources that fuel the nation's industrial base, transportation network, electricity generation, and residential needs, underpinning the world's largest economy. In 2023, total primary energy production reached a record 102.83 quadrillion British thermal units (quads), exceeding consumption of 93.59 quads and solidifying the country's status as a net energy exporter. Fossil fuels—predominantly petroleum (38% of consumption), natural gas (36%), and coal—accounted for about 84% of production and remain dominant due to their high energy density and reliability for baseload power and transport. The shale revolution, propelled by hydraulic fracturing and horizontal drilling technologies since the early 2000s, has positioned the U.S. as the world's top producer of oil and natural gas, achieving energy independence by reducing import dependence and enabling exports that enhance geopolitical leverage and consumer savings estimated in hundreds of billions annually. Nuclear power contributes a steady 8-9% share, providing carbon-free dispatchable electricity, while renewables—solar, wind, hydro, and biomass—grew to about 9% of consumption amid policy incentives, though their intermittency necessitates fossil fuel backups for grid stability. Key challenges include balancing affordable, secure supply with environmental pressures, as regulatory shifts and subsidies influence the transition dynamics without displacing fossil fuels' foundational role in economic productivity.

## Overview

### Current Energy Balance

In 2023, U.S. total primary energy production reached a record 102.86 quadrillion British thermal units (quads), exceeding domestic consumption of 93.59 quads and solidifying the country's status as a net energy exporter since 2019. This surplus marked the fifth consecutive year of net exports, driven primarily by increased output in natural gas and petroleum.

The trend persisted into 2024, with production surpassing 103 quads for the first time and consumption totaling 94.2 quadrillion British thermal units (quads). This equates to an approximate average power demand of 3,000–3,300 gigawatts (GW) when converted to electrical equivalents and averaged over the year, though this includes non-electric direct fuel uses (e.g., transportation, heating). For comparison, electricity-only average demand is around 468 GW, yielding record net exports of 9.3 quads. Preliminary data for 2025 indicate continued exporter status, supported by robust domestic production amid stable demand. Fossil fuels dominated production, comprising approximately 84% of total output in 2023, with natural gas and petroleum leading gains.

Natural gas production is forecasted to rise across scenarios, with dry natural gas output projected to increase to 42.6–44.3 trillion cubic feet by the early 2030s, reflecting 5–24% growth from 2023 levels depending on market conditions. Petroleum imports remain minimal, at less than 5% of supply, while liquefied natural gas (LNG) exports are expanding rapidly, with over 5 billion cubic feet per day in added capacity scheduled for 2025–2026.

### Key Statistics and Trends

In 2024, U.S. primary energy production achieved a record 103 quadrillion British thermal units (quads), marking a 1% increase from the 2023 record, while consumption totaled approximately 94 quads, also up 1% year-over-year. Fossil fuels comprised 82% of total primary energy consumption, with petroleum at 35.3 quads, natural gas significant, coal declining, nuclear at 9%, and renewables at 9%.

U.S. electricity demand is projected to reach 4,191 billion kilowatt-hours in 2025, a record high, with residential sales forecasted at 1,525 billion kWh. Growth factors include electric vehicle charging, which accounted for 11 terawatt-hours or 0.25% of total electricity in 2024, alongside rising data center loads. In 2024, renewables generated 17% of electricity, with wind and solar combined surpassing coal's 15% share for the first time.

Natural gas consumption hit a record 115.9 billion cubic feet per day in February 2025, 5% above the prior February peak from 2021, reflecting seasonal demand surges. Annual averages are expected to climb to 91.6 billion cubic feet per day in 2025.

## Historical Development

### Pre-20th Century Foundations

In the colonial era and early republic, the United States relied overwhelmingly on biomass, particularly wood, for energy needs, which accounted for the vast majority of consumption used in heating, cooking, and rudimentary industry. By 1776, wood was the dominant energy source, supplemented by animal muscle power and limited water mills for mechanical tasks. This dependence persisted into the mid-19th century, with wood comprising approximately 85% of total U.S. energy consumption as late as 1860, fueling household fires, iron forges, and early manufacturing. Deforestation pressures from expansive settlement and agrarian expansion began straining supplies, particularly in the Northeast, prompting gradual shifts toward alternatives.

The transition to fossil fuels accelerated in the early to mid-19th century as coal adoption enabled steam power, which powered factories, steamboats, and railroads critical to industrialization. Coal production surged from negligible levels in the early 1800s to become a viable substitute for wood by the 1840s, offering denser energy density and transportability via emerging rail networks. By the late 19th century, coal had overtaken wood in industrial applications, providing efficient fuel for steam engines that drove economic expansion, though wood remained prevalent in rural and domestic settings. This shift was causally linked to resource scarcity and technological advancements, such as improved mining and steam engine designs, rather than coordinated policy.

Petroleum's entry began modestly with Edwin Drake's drilling of the first commercial oil well in Titusville, Pennsylvania, on August 27, 1859, which struck oil at a depth of 69.5 feet and initiated small-scale production primarily for kerosene lighting. Initial output from the well reached up to 40 barrels per day, sparking a regional boom in shallow wells, but national production remained limited to under 10 million barrels annually by 1900, constrained by rudimentary refining and distribution. Oil supplemented rather than supplanted coal and wood pre-1900, with applications focused on illuminants amid whale oil shortages.

### 20th Century Expansion and Dependency

Following World War I, U.S. energy production expanded rapidly to support industrialization, electrification, and the burgeoning automotive sector, during which the United States led global electricity generation for most of the 20th century until China surpassed it in 2011. Coal output surpassed 500 million short tons annually by the 1920s and oil production climbing from approximately 443 million barrels in 1919 to over 1 billion barrels by 1929, driven by discoveries in fields like Spindletop's extensions and California's coastal reserves. This growth reflected causal dynamics of demand-pull from economic expansion and technological adoption, such as internal combustion engines supplanting steam power, though domestic supply constraints began emerging as consumption outpaced new finds. Coal, leveraging vast Appalachian reserves, fueled over 70% of energy needs into the 1940s, providing reliable baseload for steelmaking and railroads.

World War II accelerated oil production to meet Allied demands, with U.S. crude output rising from 3.6 million barrels per day in 1940 to a wartime peak of 4.7 million barrels per day by 1945, effectively doubling wartime supply contributions through efficiency measures and field expansions in Texas and Oklahoma. Postwar economic boom sustained this trajectory, but by the 1950s, surging vehicle ownership—reaching 50 million cars by 1955—propelled petroleum consumption beyond domestic capacity, initiating import reliance from Venezuela and the Middle East. Nuclear power emerged as a supplement, with the first commercial reactor online in 1957; by the 1990s, it generated 19-20% of U.S. electricity via high-capacity-factor plants built mostly between 1970 and 1990. Coal solidified dominance in power generation, accounting for over 50% of electricity by the late 1980s through low-cost steam plants.

The 1973 OPEC embargo, triggered by U.S. support for Israel, quadrupled oil prices from $3 to $12 per barrel, exposing vulnerabilities as imports constituted 35% of consumption and caused fuel shortages, inflation spikes to 11%, and a recession with GDP contracting 0.5% in 1974. The 1979 Iranian Revolution compounded this, with prices surging to $40 per barrel and imports hitting 46% of supply, underscoring causal risks of geopolitical dependence on unstable suppliers. Net petroleum imports escalated, reaching 60% of consumption by 2005 amid flat domestic production post-1970 peak. In response, the Energy Policy and Conservation Act of 1975 established the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, initially targeting 750 million barrels to buffer shocks, marking a shift toward supply security realism over unchecked import growth. These crises highlighted how foreign leverage disrupted U.S. energy flows, prompting conservation and diversification efforts without resolving underlying demand-supply imbalances.

### 21st Century Shale Revolution and Independence

The advent of widespread hydraulic fracturing combined with horizontal drilling in the early 21st century unlocked vast shale resources, particularly in formations like the Marcellus, Permian, and Bakken, propelling U.S. oil and natural gas production to unprecedented levels starting around 2008. U.S. shale oil output surged from approximately 0.5 million barrels per day (mbpd) in 2008 to over 7 mbpd by 2019, contributing to a near doubling of total petroleum liquids production to 19.5 mbpd from 9.1 mbpd in 2009. Natural gas production similarly expanded, with marketed output reaching a record average of 125 billion cubic feet per day (Bcf/d) by 2023, up 4% from 2022, driven largely by associated gas from shale oil wells. This technological shift reversed decades of declining domestic output, reducing reliance on imports and stabilizing energy prices through increased supply.

By 2019, these gains culminated in the United States achieving annual net total energy exporter status for the first time since 1957, with exports totaling 23.6 quadrillion British thermal units (quads)—an all-time high—exceeding imports by a net margin. Crude oil and natural gas plant liquids production hit 31.8 quads, while natural gas reached 34.9 quads, both records at the time, enabling surplus exports amid global demand. This milestone reflected the shale revolution's causal impact on energy balance, as domestic output outpaced consumption growth, shifting the U.S. from net importer to exporter without relying on non-fossil alternatives.

Production records persisted into the 2020s, with crude oil averaging 13.2 mbpd in 2024—surpassing the 2023 high by 2%—and natural gas holding steady near 38 trillion cubic feet annually, despite geopolitical disruptions and fluctuating global prices. Associated natural gas from shale operations grew 7.9% in 2023 to 17.1 Bcf/d, underscoring the integrated oil-gas dynamics of shale plays. These trends sustained net exporter status through 2025, even as international demands rose, bolstering economic resilience.

## Primary Energy Production

### Fossil Fuels Dominance

Fossil fuels, comprising petroleum, natural gas, and coal, accounted for approximately 84% of total U.S. primary energy production in 2023, totaling over 86 quadrillion British thermal units out of 102.9 quadrillion. This dominance persisted into 2024, with production reaching a record 103 quadrillion British thermal units, where natural gas contributed 38% and crude oil 27%, supplemented by coal. These sources provide scalable, dispatchable energy essential for baseload power in electricity generation, where natural gas and coal together supplied over 50% of output in 2024, offering reliability absent in weather-dependent alternatives.

The shale revolution since 2008 has driven economic efficiencies, with increased natural gas supply reducing Henry Hub prices by over 70% from peaks above $8 per million British thermal units to averages around $3 by 2012, enabling displacement of higher-cost coal and cutting overall energy costs by an estimated $203 billion annually for consumers. This causal shift lowered electricity prices while maintaining grid stability through combined-cycle gas plants, which ramp quickly to meet variable demand.

By 2024, the United States solidified its position as the world's leading producer of both crude oil, at about 13 million barrels per day or roughly 13% of global output, and natural gas, capturing 25.5% of worldwide production in 2023 with continued growth. Exports of these fuels reached 30% of domestic production, enhancing energy security and global supply chains without compromising domestic availability. This production surge underscores fossil fuels' role in achieving U.S. energy independence, with net exports of petroleum products and natural gas exceeding imports since 2019.

### Coal Production and Decline

U.S. coal production reached its historical peak of 1,172 million short tons in 2008, driven by strong domestic demand for electricity generation. By 2023, output had fallen to 578 million short tons, less than half the peak level, with forecasts for 2024 estimating further decline to around 512 million short tons. This sustained reduction reflects primarily market-driven factors, including the shale revolution's expansion of low-cost natural gas supplies, which displaced coal in power generation due to superior economics rather than regulatory mandates alone.

Coal's share of U.S. electricity generation dropped to 14.9% in 2024, a record low, with generation volume declining 3.3% from the prior year to 653 billion kilowatt-hours. Natural gas, benefiting from historically low prices in 2024, captured additional market share in the electric power sector, outcompeting coal on cost and efficiency grounds. Despite abundant domestic reserves—estimated at 252 billion short tons of recoverable resources, equivalent to over 400 years of supply at recent production rates—coal extraction has become uneconomic in many basins relative to gas alternatives.

Production remains regionally concentrated, with Wyoming and Montana accounting for 46% of 2024 output, primarily low-sulfur subbituminous coal from the Powder River Basin suitable for export markets. Appalachian states, including West Virginia, Pennsylvania, and Kentucky, produce higher-quality bituminous coal but have seen sharper declines due to competition and aging infrastructure. U.S. coal exports reached their highest levels since 2018 in 2023 and 2024, supported by global demand from Asia and Europe, though domestic utilization continues to wane.

### Natural Gas Expansion

U.S. natural gas consumption reached a record 115.9 billion cubic feet per day (Bcf/d) in February 2025, surpassing the previous high from 2021 by 5%. Annual average consumption is projected to increase 1% to 91.4 Bcf/d for the full year, driven by demand across sectors. Dry natural gas production is forecast to rise from 103.2 Bcf/d in 2024 to 107.1 Bcf/d in 2025, reflecting ongoing supply abundance from technological advances in extraction. Projections indicate production will grow by more than 15% through 2040, peaking near 119 Bcf/d around 2032 before stabilizing, supported by efficient resource recovery.

The expansion has been propelled by shale formations, particularly the Marcellus in Appalachia and the Permian Basin in Texas and New Mexico. Marcellus production remains the largest among U.S. shale plays, benefiting from low break-even costs and high capital efficiency. In the Permian, marketed natural gas output increased 12% in 2024 to 25.4 Bcf/d, contributing significantly to national totals despite associated gas from oil drilling. These regions underscore the scalability of hydraulic fracturing and horizontal drilling, enabling rapid response to market signals and ensuring dispatchable supply that can meet variable demand without the intermittency challenges of renewables.

Liquefied natural gas (LNG) exports have more than doubled since 2016, rising from an average 0.5 Bcf/d to 11.9 Bcf/d by 2023, with forecasts reaching 14.6 Bcf/d in 2025. This growth highlights the U.S. shift to a net exporter, leveraging abundant domestic production to serve global markets while maintaining ample reserves for internal use.

### Petroleum Output and Exports

United States crude oil production reached record levels in 2025, averaging 13.5 million barrels per day (mb/d) according to forecasts from the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA), with a peak of 13.6 mb/d recorded in July. This output, equivalent to about 40% of global demand growth, was predominantly driven by tight oil extraction in the Permian Basin, which produced over 5.6 mb/d by late 2024 and continued to account for roughly 45% of national crude volumes. The region's efficiency gains, including longer laterals and enhanced completions, sustained growth despite fewer active rigs.

Domestic refining capacity held steady at 18.4 mb/d operable atmospheric distillation as of January 1, 2025, supporting the processing of both domestic and imported crudes into transportation fuels. This stability, amid minimal net capacity changes, allowed utilization rates to average around 91% annually. The United States has maintained net petroleum exporter status since 2020, with total exports of crude and products surpassing imports, a shift solidified by the post-2010s shale revolution that reversed decades of import dependency.

This energy independence has provided a geopolitical buffer, markedly reducing reliance on OPEC supplies; domestic production first exceeded imports in 2013, diminishing vulnerability to Middle Eastern disruptions that previously influenced U.S. foreign policy. In 2025, net crude imports fell to historic lows of about 1.9 mb/d, the lowest since 1971, further insulating the economy from external supply shocks.

### Nuclear Power Contributions

Nuclear power contributes approximately 19% of the United States' total electricity generation, providing a consistent baseload supply from 94 operational commercial reactors across 54 plants as of 2025. These reactors, primarily pressurized water and boiling water designs, achieve high reliability with median capacity factors exceeding 90% over recent years, enabling continuous operation independent of weather or diurnal cycles. This dispatchable, zero-emission output supports grid stability, contrasting with intermittent renewables and supplementing fossil fuels during peak demand.

The completion of Vogtle Units 3 and 4 in Georgia marked the first new reactor additions in over three decades, with Unit 3 entering commercial service in July 2023 and Unit 4 in April 2024, adding over 2.2 gigawatts of capacity. Despite these advancements, nuclear expansion has stagnated since the 1990s due to escalating construction costs, protracted regulatory approvals, and financing challenges rather than inherent safety flaws or public opposition tied to accidents.

The U.S. nuclear fleet maintains an exemplary safety record, with no fatalities from radiation exposure in commercial operations and incidents like the 1979 Three Mile Island partial meltdown resulting in no adverse health effects or injuries. Lifecycle greenhouse gas emissions from nuclear power are minimal, typically 10-20 grams of CO2-equivalent per kilowatt-hour, orders of magnitude lower than coal (around 820 g/kWh) or natural gas (490 g/kWh), primarily from mining and construction rather than operations. Waste volumes remain small and manageable, stored securely on-site or in federal repositories, underscoring nuclear's potential for scalable, low-carbon energy despite policy and economic barriers.

### Hydropower Stability

Hydropower contributes approximately 6% to U.S. electricity generation, maintaining a relatively stable share over decades despite fluctuations in output. In 2023, it accounted for 240 billion kilowatthours out of total utility-scale generation of 4,178 billion kilowatthours. The conventional hydropower fleet consists of 2,252 plants with a total generating capacity of 80.58 gigawatts, concentrated primarily in states with suitable geography such as Washington, California, and Oregon, which host about 50% of the nation's capacity.

Major development occurred during the early to mid-20th century, particularly under New Deal initiatives in the 1930s, including the establishment of the Tennessee Valley Authority in 1933 to build dams for flood control, navigation, and power generation. The TVA operates 29 hydroelectric dams, many constructed in the 1930s and 1940s, exemplifying federal investment in large-scale infrastructure that peaked hydropower's role in the national grid. Capacity expanded significantly from the 1920s to the 1970s, tripling conventional hydropower output by 1980, but construction slowed markedly thereafter due to exhausted prime sites, rising environmental regulations, and high costs of new dams.

Post-1980 growth has been minimal, with annual capacity additions averaging less than 100 megawatts, limited by geographic constraints and opposition to new large-scale dams that could disrupt ecosystems and displace communities. Capacity factors have trended downward at four-fifths of U.S. hydropower plants since 1980, reflecting aging infrastructure and variable hydrology rather than expansion. Pumped storage hydropower, totaling 23,167 megawatts in 2023 across 18 states, supports grid peaking and storage but does not drive overall capacity growth, comprising 96% of utility-scale energy storage yet focused on balancing intermittent sources rather than baseload addition.

### Wind and Solar Intermittency

Wind and solar photovoltaic capacity expanded rapidly in the United States, collectively accounting for 17% of electricity generation in 2024, surpassing coal for the first time. Installed wind capacity reached 153 GW by the end of 2024, predominantly onshore installations concentrated in states like Texas and Iowa. Solar capacity grew to 236 GWdc by year-end, with utility-scale additions of 41.4 GW reflecting scalable panel deployment amid federal incentives.

Intermittency inherent to these sources—driven by diurnal solar cycles and variable wind speeds—necessitates overbuilding capacity by 2-3 times nameplate ratings to ensure reliable output, alongside complementary battery storage or fossil fuel backups to balance supply-demand mismatches. Studies indicate that without such measures, achieving firm power from renewables alone demands substantial excess generation, often curtailed during peak production periods. In California, utility-scale wind and solar curtailment rose 29% to 3.4 million MWh in 2024, attributable to transmission constraints and oversupply.

Offshore wind projects encounter distinct obstacles, including protracted permitting, elevated per-MW costs exceeding onshore equivalents, and documented avian mortality risks from turbine collisions, prompting mitigation research. By mid-2025, policy shifts under the Trump administration rescinded federal funding allocations totaling $679 million for port infrastructure, intensifying scrutiny and stalling development pipelines. These factors have limited offshore contributions to under 1 GW operational capacity as of 2024.

### Geothermal and Biomass Limitations

Geothermal energy in the United States is constrained by its geographic specificity, with viable hydrothermal resources limited primarily to western states such as California, Nevada, and Hawaii, where high-temperature reservoirs are accessible. Installed geothermal capacity stood at approximately 2.7 gigawatts (GW) as of the end of 2023, accounting for about 0.4% of total U.S. electricity generation in 2024. Despite its potential for reliable baseload power due to the constant heat source, expansion has been stagnant, with high upfront drilling and exploration costs—often exceeding those of other renewables—discouraging widespread development outside favorable sites.

Biomass energy, derived mainly from wood, agricultural residues, and municipal waste, contributes around 1.2% to U.S. electricity generation, with total capacity of about 5 GW including 2.7 GW from waste and 2.2 GW from wood-derived sources as of late 2024. While classified as renewable, biomass combustion faces limitations from low energy density and conversion efficiencies, resulting in emissions profiles that release more carbon dioxide per megawatt-hour than coal in some analyses—up to 65% higher due to incomplete regrowth offsets and combustion inefficiencies. Lifecycle assessments indicate net positive CO2 impacts over short timescales, as carbon release precedes sequestration by decades, challenging claims of carbon neutrality absent rigorous sustainability verification. Growth remains limited, with projected slight declines in generation to 21.6 billion kilowatt-hours in 2025 amid competition from cheaper alternatives.

Together, geothermal and biomass represent less than 2% of U.S. electricity output, underscoring their niche roles amid scalability barriers: site dependency for geothermal and environmental trade-offs for biomass, including air pollutants like nitrogen oxides and particulate matter beyond CO2. These factors have kept additions minimal, with no significant capacity expansions reported in recent years, prioritizing other renewables for broader deployment.

## Energy Consumption Patterns

### Sectoral Demand Breakdown

In 2023, U.S. primary energy consumption totaled 93.6 quadrillion British thermal units (quads), distributed across end-use sectors as follows: the industrial sector consumed approximately 29 quads (31% of total), the transportation sector 25 quads (27%), the residential sector 20 quads (21%), and the commercial sector 17 quads (18%). These figures reflect delivered energy to end users, excluding losses in electricity generation and conversion. The industrial sector relies heavily on natural gas for processes like manufacturing and chemicals, alongside coal in specific applications such as steel production, while petroleum derivatives support petrochemical feedstocks. Transportation demand is overwhelmingly met by petroleum products, which supplied 89% of the sector's energy in 2023, with refined products like gasoline and diesel powering the vast majority of vehicles and aircraft.

Residential and commercial sectors together accounted for 37 quads, primarily through natural gas for heating and cooking, and electricity for lighting, appliances, and cooling. Electricity end-use has trended upward in these sectors due to increased adoption of devices and air conditioning, though overall primary energy per capita has declined from a 1979 peak of approximately 360 million Btu per person to about 280 million Btu in 2023—a roughly 22% reduction driven by efficiency improvements in appliances, buildings, and vehicles. In transportation, electrification remains marginal; electric vehicle electricity demand reached only 11 terawatt-hours (TWh) in 2024, equivalent to 0.25% of total U.S. electricity consumption.

### Regional and Temporal Variations

Texas leads U.S. energy production, particularly in the Permian Basin, which accounted for 46% of national crude oil output in 2023. This region's dominance extends to associated natural gas, contributing significantly to Texas's overall energy surplus, with the state exporting refined products and liquefied natural gas to meet domestic and international demand. In contrast, the Northeast relies heavily on imported natural gas via pipelines from production basins in the Appalachians and Midwest, as local production remains minimal and consumption for space heating drives regional deficits during cold months.

California exemplifies import-dependent consumption despite leading in renewables, generating 57% of its in-state electricity from sources like solar and hydro in 2024, yet importing about 25% of total electricity needs, primarily from neighboring states and Canada, to balance variable supply and high demand. The Midwest, anchored by coal and biofuels, shows elevated per capita energy use in states like North Dakota due to industrial and agricultural demands, while the Pacific Northwest benefits from stable hydropower but faces seasonal export variations tied to precipitation.

Per capita energy consumption varies markedly by region, with Southern states like Louisiana exhibiting rates up to 908 million BTU per person annually, driven by petrochemical industries and air conditioning needs in humid climates, compared to lower figures in the Northeast, such as Rhode Island's 161 million BTU, reflecting denser urban populations and milder summers. Energy-producing regions like the Mountain West, including Wyoming, report higher per capita totals from extraction activities, exceeding 500 million BTU, underscoring how resource endowments amplify local intensities beyond national averages.

### Projections for Demand Growth

The U.S. Energy Information Administration's (EIA) Short-Term Energy Outlook from October 2025 forecasts modest growth in total U.S. primary energy consumption through 2026, with increases tied to sustained economic activity and population expansion rather than efficiency gains alone. This outlook projects overall demand rising by approximately 1% annually, reflecting baseline trends in industrial output and household needs without incorporating stringent decarbonization policies.

Electricity consumption is anticipated to drive much of the near-term uptick, expanding at 2% to 3% per year to reach 4,305 billion kilowatt-hours in 2026 from 4,191 billion in 2025. Key drivers include surging data center loads for artificial intelligence computing, which EIA estimates could add hundreds of terawatt-hours annually, alongside electric vehicle charging and reshoring of energy-intensive manufacturing like semiconductors and batteries. These factors outweigh potential efficiency improvements in appliances and buildings, as GDP growth—projected at 2.1% in 2025 and 1.9% in 2026—amplifies sectoral demands.

Petroleum product consumption, encompassing liquid fuels for transportation and industry, is expected to stabilize at around 20.6 million barrels per day by 2026, supported by steady vehicle miles traveled and petrochemical feedstocks amid moderate economic conditions. Natural gas demand holds relatively steady, with minimal net change forecasted as residential and power sector usage balances against industrial steadiness, underscoring resilience in fossil fuel baselines absent policy-induced shifts. Population growth to 345 million by 2026 further underpins these projections, as per capita energy needs persist despite historical decoupling trends.

## Electricity Generation and Infrastructure

### Generation Mix by Source

In 2025, natural gas accounted for approximately 40% of U.S. net electricity generation (39.7% in some reports), down slightly from prior years, while coal contributed about 16.5%, nuclear power 17.3%, and renewables reached higher shares including hydropower. Wind stood at around 10.2% and solar (including utility-scale and behind-the-meter) around 8%, combining to 17-19% for wind and solar. Dispatchable sources—natural gas, coal, and nuclear power—accounted for about 75% of generation, underscoring their essential role in providing reliable baseload and dispatchable power. The intermittency of wind and solar continues to require backups from these dispatchable sources to maintain grid stability amid ongoing renewables growth and increasing electricity demand.

Significant state-level variations exist in the generation mix, influenced by geography, resource endowments, and policy. Coal remains prominent in Midwestern and Appalachian states like West Virginia (over 90% of generation from coal in some years) and Indiana, where it supports industrial loads and lacks viable alternatives. In contrast, the Southwest features high solar penetration, with California deriving about 25% from solar photovoltaic and Arizona benefiting from utility-scale desert installations, though both states supplement with natural gas for reliability. Texas, the largest generator, blends natural gas (over 40%), wind (25%), and growing solar, reflecting its oil/gas heritage and vast open spaces for renewables. These disparities highlight how local factors override national trends, with EIA data showing coal-heavy states in the Midwest resisting phase-outs more than coastal regions pushing renewables.

### Grid Capacity and Reliability Challenges

The United States electricity grid maintains an installed generation capacity of approximately 1,200 gigawatts (GW) as of 2025, encompassing a mix of fossil fuels, nuclear, and renewables, though effective summer capacity is lower due to derates and maintenance. This infrastructure, much of which dates to the mid-20th century, faces systemic aging challenges, with over 70% of transmission and distribution lines exceeding their 50-year design life and many transformers and substations operating 40 to 70 years beyond intended spans. The American Society of Civil Engineers assigned the energy sector a D+ grade in 2025, citing vulnerabilities from deferred maintenance amid rising demand from electrification and data centers.

Reliability metrics reveal increasing strain, with the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC) reporting elevated risks of supply shortfalls in multiple regions for 2024-2025 due to declining planning reserve margins, projected to average 16% by the mid-2030s from 29% in 2024, driven by generator retirements and load growth outpacing additions. System Average Interruption Duration Index (SAIDI) values, measuring average outage minutes per customer annually, have trended higher in recent years, with national averages around 100-150 minutes excluding major events, though extreme incidents amplify impacts. Dispatchable resources like natural gas and nuclear provide essential spinning reserves and grid inertia for frequency stability, whereas variable renewables necessitate additional balancing mechanisms, exacerbating reserve adequacy pressures during peak or low-output periods.

The 2021 Texas winter storm in the ERCOT grid exemplified these vulnerabilities, where failures across generation types— including 58% of unplanned outages from natural gas units due to frozen equipment and fuel supply disruptions, alongside wind turbine icing and coal plant issues—led to cascading blackouts affecting millions for days, underscoring the limits of non-weatherized infrastructure and the challenges of dispatching intermittent sources in extremes. Transmission bottlenecks compound capacity constraints, with congestion curtailing renewable integration and contributing to localized shortages, despite overall U.S. transmission and distribution losses averaging about 5% of generated electricity. High-voltage direct current (HVDC) upgrades are increasingly recognized as necessary for reducing losses over long distances, enhancing interconnectivity, and accommodating remote renewable siting, though deployment lags behind needs for grid modernization.

### State-Level Generation and Interconnections

The United States electricity grid comprises three primary interconnections: the Eastern Interconnection, serving over 120 million customers across 36 states from the Atlantic to the [Rocky Mountains](/page/Rocky Mountains) and managed by regional transmission organizations like PJM Interconnection and Midcontinent Independent System Operator (MISO); the Western Interconnection, covering the western states; and the Texas Interconnection, operated largely independently by the Electric Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT). These interconnections operate as asynchronous systems with limited transfer capacity at their seams, such as the boundary between the Eastern and Western grids, which restricts efficient power flows and exposes seams to pricing volatility from mismatched regional supply and demand conditions.

State-level generation mixes vary significantly within these interconnections, influencing interconnection dynamics. In Wyoming, part of the Western Interconnection, 2023 net generation reached 40.5 billion kWh, with coal accounting for 71.5% and wind for 18.4%, enabling the state to export surplus baseload and variable power to high-demand neighbors like California. Florida, within the Eastern Interconnection, generated 278.5 billion kWh in 2023, dominated by natural gas at 71.6% and solar at 6.1%, reflecting a reliance on dispatchable gas for reliability amid growing intermittent solar output. California, also in the Western Interconnection, imported approximately 28% of its 2023 electricity needs—totaling about 86 billion kWh—from sources including Pacific Northwest hydropower and Southwest coal and gas plants, supplementing its domestic mix of natural gas (45.8%) and renewables (33.2%, primarily solar).

Planned renewable expansions exacerbate integration strains across interconnections. In 2025, the United States added 3,288 MW of wind capacity, concentrated in regions like the Plains states within MISO and SPP, increasing variability that requires enhanced transmission coordination and backup reserves to mitigate curtailments and reliability risks at interconnection seams. ERCOT's isolated structure, handling 90% of Texas's load with heavy wind and gas reliance, has experienced acute volatility during peak demands, as seen in the 2021 freeze, underscoring seams' role in amplifying localized stresses without broader grid balancing.
